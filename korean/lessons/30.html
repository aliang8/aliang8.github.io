출입 = enter and exit

Notes: 출입 refers to both actions of entering and exiting a place (like a building or a door or something like that). As a noun, it is often found before another noun describing that something is related to ‘entering and exiting.’ For example:

출입구

출입문
doorway, place where one can exit/enter
출입국사무소
immigration office (the office of entering and exiting the country)

It is also commonly used before ‘금지’ to have a meaning similar to “do not enter.”

출입 금지
do not enter

As a verb, it refers to the action of entering and exiting. For example:

여기에 출입하고 싶으면 회원카드를 보여줘야 돼요

전하다 = to convey, to deliver

Common Usages:

전해오다
to pass down

Notes: This word can be used when handing something from one person to another. When used like this, the translation of “deliver” is usually appropriate. For example:

그 용돈을 아이에게 전해 주세요
Please give/hand/deliver this allowance/money to the child

It is also used for “words” that are handed from one person to another. When used like this, the translation of “convey” is usually appropriate. For example:

그가 저에게 무슨 말을 전했는지 기억이 안 나요
I don’t remember what that person told (conveyed to) me
거실을 깨끗하게 정리하고 엄마한테 진심으로 감사하다는 말을 전했다
We organized the living room (cleanly), and thanked mom from the bottom of our hearts

버리다 = to throw away

Introduction

You have now learned a lot about using ~는 것 (or one of its forms) with a clause to describe an upcoming noun. For example:

내가 가고 있는 곳
the place I am going
내가 만난 사람
the person I met
내가 먹을 음식
the food I will eat

In this lesson you will learn to add ~는지 to show that the preceding clause is a guess or something uncertain.

1

A Clause of Uncertainty:~는지

A good name for ~는지 is the “clause of uncertainty.” Placing ~는지 at the end of a clause marks that clause as a guess, a question or something uncertain.

Uncertainty often comes with a question word in the sentence. For example:

저는 친구가 어디 가는 것을 몰라요

Broken into simpler pieces, that sentence is:

저는 (—) 몰라요
I don’t know (—-)

What don’t you know? You don’t know the noun in the brackets:

저는 (친구가 어디 가는 것을) 몰라요

So the sentence reads:

저는 친구가 어디 가는 것을 몰라요
I don’t know where my friend is going

But because “친구가 어디 가는 것” is uncertain, the clause takes ~는지 instead of ~는 것. For example:

저는 친구가 어디 가는지 몰라요
I don’t know where my friend is going

Note that the future tense ~겠다 is commonly added to 모르다 in sentences like these. Used this way (as “모르겠다”), 모르다 has no future meaning. It is just a common, slightly more polite way to say that one “does not know something.” So the sentence above is more commonly written or spoken as:

저는 친구가 어디 가는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know where my friend is going

You will keep seeing “모르겠다” instead of a present tense conjugation of 모르다 in the rest of this lesson and throughout your studies.

By default, a clause with a question word (누구, 뭐, 언제, 어디, 왜, etc.) usually takes ~는지 because of the uncertainty it carries. For example:

엄마가 누구랑 먹는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know who mom is eating with
엄마가 뭐 먹는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know what mom is eating
엄마가 어디서 먹는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know where mom is eating
엄마가 왜 먹는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know why mom is eating

A question word is not required, though. All that is needed is uncertainty in the sentence. When there is no question word in a sentence with ~는지, the English translation usually uses “if.” For example:

엄마가 지금 먹고 있는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if mom is eating now

More examples below. Notice too that the final word does not have to be 모르다. Any verb or adjective that makes sense after the uncertain clause can be used. For example:

그 사람을 왜 데려오는지 물어봤어요
I asked him why he is bringing that person
비상출입구가 어디 있는지 찾았어요
We found where the emergency exit is
해안까지 어떻게 가는지 물어봤어요
I asked how to get to the beach/coast
엄마가 무슨 재료를 쓰고 있는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know what ingredients mom is using
정부가 외국인 선생님 예산을 왜 줄이는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know why the government is decreasing the budget for foreign teachers
학생들은 선생님들이 돈을 얼마나 버는지 몰라요
Students don’t know how much money teachers earn
저는 그 학생이 어느 대학교를 다니는지 기억(이) 안 나요
I don’t remember which university that student attends

Past tense:
The same idea works for a guess, question or uncertainty in the past tense. Add ~았/었 to the verb at the end of the uncertain clause, then ~는지. For example:

저는 엄마가 왜 먹었는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know why mom ate
저는 엄마가 뭐 먹었는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know what mom ate
저는 엄마가 언제 먹었는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know when mom ate
저는 엄마가 어디서 먹었는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know where mom ate
저는 엄마가 밥을 먹었는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if mom ate

The form above (~았/었는지) is officially correct. In speech, though, ~ㄴ/은지 is very common instead. For example:

저는 엄마가 왜 먹은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know why mom ate
저는 엄마가 뭐 먹은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know what mom ate
저는 엄마가 언제 먹은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know when mom ate
저는 엄마가 어디서 먹은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know where mom ate
저는 엄마가 밥을 먹은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if mom ate

There is no real difference between the two sets of sentences, especially in speech, and both sound natural to a Korean speaker. ~았/었는지 is the correct grammatical form; ~ㄴ/은지 is more of a spoken form.

Other examples:

그 셔츠를 언제 버렸는지 기억이 안 나요
I don’t remember when I threw away that shirt
열쇠를 어디 두었는지 잊어버렸어요
I forget where I put my keys
우리가 이것을 언제 정했는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know when we set it
그가 저에게 무슨 말을 전했는지 기억이 안 나요
I don’t remember what that person told me (conveyed to me)

Future tense:
The same idea works for a guess, question or uncertainty in the future tense. Add ~ㄹ/을 to the verb at the end of the uncertain clause, then ~지. For example:

택배가 언제 올지 모르겠어요
I don’t know when the delivery will come
용돈을 얼마나 줄지 모르겠어요
I don’t know how much allowance I should give
오후에 비가 올지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if it will rain in the afternoon
수술을 받을지 확실하지 않아요
It is not certain if I will get surgery
내일 공원에 갈지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if I will go to the park tomorrow
내일 영화를 볼지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if I will see a movie tomorrow

When the uncertain clause has no question word, the English translation often uses “might.” For example

오후에 비가 올지 모르겠어요
It might rain in the afternoon tomorrow
수술을 받을지 모르겠어요
I might get surgery
내일 공원에 갈지 모르겠어요
I might go to the park tomorrow
내일 영화를 볼지 모르겠어요
I might see a movie tomorrow

English speakers often wonder how one Korean sentence can translate to different things in English. It doesn’t, really. “~ㄹ/을지 몰라요” just says that something may or may not happen, and both translations above (“I don’t know if” and “might…”) say exactly that. Some Korean sentences simply do not map perfectly onto English. Focus on the general meaning of the Korean sentence and lean less on the English translations. The nuance of “~ㄹ/을지 몰라요” can be rendered many ways in English, none of them perfect.

2

Using~는지 with Adjectives

~는지 can also attach to an uncertain clause predicated by an adjective, but then the form is ~ㄴ/은지 rather than ~는지. The difference between ~는지 and ~ㄴ/은지 is the same as between ~는 and ~ㄴ/은 when verbs and adjectives describe an upcoming noun. For example:

먹는 것
가는 것

행복한 것
밝은 것

Some examples of ~ㄴ/은지 with adjectives:

제가 준 것이 괜찮은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if the thing that I gave is good
이 빛이 충분히 밝은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if this light is bright enough
제가 구한 아르바이트가 좋은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if the job I found is good
제가 가져온 자료가 충분한지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if I brought enough materials
제가 강아지를 기르고 싶은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if I want to raise a puppy
그 책이 얼마나 긴지 모르겠어요
I don’t know how long that book is

For adjectives in the past or future tense, add the same things as with verbs. For example:

그 시대가 그렇게 길었는지 깨닫지 못했어요
I didn’t realize that era was so long
그 일이 힘들지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if that work will be difficult
내일 날씨가 쌀쌀할지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if tomorrow’s weather will be chilly
3

If… or not…

All the examples so far mention only one situation. You can mention more than one by connecting more than one verb or adjective to ~는지. The simplest way is to add the opposite situation, followed by ~는지. For example:

내일 영화를 볼지 안 볼지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if I will see a movie tomorrow or not
수술을 받을지 안 받을지 확실하지 않아요
It is not certain if I will get surgery or not
그가 제 말을 들었는지 안 들었는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if he was listening to me or not
저는 엄마가 밥을 먹었는지 안 먹었는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if mom ate or not
제가 구한 아르바이트가 좋은지 안 좋은지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if the job I found is good or not

With non-하다 verbs (like 먹다) you repeat the verb to say “I don’t know if mom ate or not.” With 하다 verbs, you can usually shorten the sentence by dropping the word before ~하다 the second time. So instead of:

저는 엄마가 공부했는지 안 공부했는지 모르겠어요

You could just say:

저는 엄마가 공부했는지 안 했는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if mom studied or not
Remember that Korean people love shortening their sentences; dropping the redundant second “공부” is more natural in Korean.

In all of the above, two possibilities are listed and the speaker does not know which will happen. These examples use the positive and negative outcomes of the same situation. You can also list two (or more) unrelated outcomes. For example:

내일 공원에 갈지 영화를 볼지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if I will see a movie or go to the park tomorrow
제가 구한 아르바이트가 좋은지 나쁜지 모르겠어요
I don’t know if the (part-time) job I found is good or bad
수술을 받을지 그냥 약으로 치료할지 확실하지 않아요
It is not certain if I will get surgery or just treat it with medicine

~지 can also form a question, for example asking somebody if they know how to do something. The sentence most often ends with 알다. For example:

서울에 어떻게 가는지 알아요?
Do you know how to get to Seoul?
그 단어를 어떻게 발음하는지 알아요?
Do you know how to pronounce that word?
그 학생이 책을 왜 버렸는지 알아요?
Do you know why that student threw out his book?
물을 어떻게 막는지 알아요?
Do you know how to block the water?

Clauses with ~지 are called “clauses of uncertainty” here because that fits most situations, but sometimes 지 marks something certain. For example, the answers to those questions would be:

서울에 어떻게 가는지 알아요
I know how to get to Seoul
그 단어를 어떻게 발음하는지 알아요
I know how to pronounce that word
그 학생이 책을 왜 버렸는지 알아요
I know why that student threw out his book

In those examples 지 technically does not mark uncertainty. So why use 지? Here, the question word in the sentence makes 지 a more natural noun than 것.

There is another way to say that one “knows how to do something” (based more on ability than knowledge). It is covered in Lesson 85.

4

Attaching~도 to~지

~도 is often attached to ~지. Adding ~도 to ~지 can have two meanings:

1) The usual “too,” “also” or “either” meaning of ~도. For example:

저는 밥도 먹었어요
I ate rice too
저도 밥을 먹었어요
I also ate rice
저는 밥도 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat rice either

This first meaning is covered in a later lesson. It is really about ~도 rather than ~지. One example sentence so you can see what is meant:

문을 열지도 몰라요
You don’t even know how to open the door

Here we focus on the more ambiguous meaning of ~도, described in #2:

2) Very little meaning or purpose at all. Look at the two sentences below:

내일 비가 올지 모르겠어요
It might rain tomorrow
내일 비가 올지도 모르겠어요
It might rain tomorrow

Assuming ~도 is not carrying the meaning from #1 above (which is possible), ~도 does not really change the sentence. The same goes for these two:

내일 공원에 갈지 모르겠어요
I might go to the park tomorrow
내일 공원에 갈지도 모르겠어요
I might go to the park tomorrow

What exact nuance does ~도 add to sentences like these (again, assuming it is not the ~도 from #1)? Years of study and exposure point to one answer: they are essentially the same. It is tempting to think they cannot be exactly the same and that ~도 must do something.

Two conversations with Korean speakers show how subtle the difference is, and that even Korean people do not really agree on it.

The first was with a Korean person who speaks fluent English.

—————————————————————————————————————-

Q: What difference in nuance do you feel between these two sentences:

내일 비가 올지 모르겠어요
It might rain tomorrow
내일 비가 올지도 모르겠어요
It might rain tomorrow

A: The ~도 makes it sound like you don’t know if it will happen or not. It might happen, but it also might not.

Q: Isn’t that implied in the first sentence too?

A: Technically yes, but they are just two ways of saying the same thing. Like “I don’t know if it will rain tomorrow or not” and “It might rain tomorrow.”

Q: Wouldn’t that first sentence be better written as
“내일 비가 올지 안 올지 모르겠어요.”

A: Ah, yes. I feel these two sentences mean exactly the same thing:
내일 비가 올지도 모르겠어요
내일 비가 올지 안 올지 모르겠어요.
The ~도 adds that extra nuance that something might happen or not.

The second conversation was with a teacher who teaches Korean grammar to Korean high school students, so her grammar knowledge should be excellent (though native speakers do not always see their own grammar clearly). She speaks no English, so the conversation was in Korean:

—————————————————————————————————————-

Q: What difference in nuance do you feel between these three sentences:
내일 비가 올지 모르겠어요
내일 비가 올지도 모르겠어요
내일 비가 올지 안 올지 모르겠어요

A: The first two are identical. The third one lists the two possibilities: “it might rain” or “it might not rain.”

Q: Another Korean speaker said the ~도 in the second sentence also implies those two possibilities, and that the second and third sentences mean the same thing. What do you think?

A: I don’t feel that when I hear it. The first two are the same, and the third one lists more possibilities.

——————————

So two Korean speakers, one with excellent English and one with deep grammar knowledge, gave opposite answers. The conclusion from this, and from everything else:

~ㄹ/을지 모르다 and
~ㄹ/을지도 모르다

Have the same, or effectively the same meaning.

Here is when ~도 is used in this way.

~도 attaches to uncertain clauses in the future tense to express uncertainty about whether something will happen (or not). You will rarely see ~도 on a past or present tense uncertain clause unless it carries the meaning from #1 above.

~도 is not added to uncertain clauses that contain a question word. For example, this would be unnatural:

비가 언제 올지도 모르겠어요

This “rule” suggests that ~도 is fairly close to the “if or not” meaning the first speaker described. Just as “or not” would be unnatural in the following English sentence, ~도 would be unnatural in its Korean translation:

비가 언제 올지도 모르겠어요
I don’t know when it will rain or not

Again, this is not the ~도 from #1. In that usage, ~도 attaches to ~는지, ~았/었는지 or ~ㄹ/을지 with the meaning ~도 usually has on nouns, and it can attach to uncertain clauses with question words. That meaning is covered in a future lesson.

All that for one syllable.
And we are not done. That syllable (지) has another meaning, one that is easier to pin down.

5

I have been doing X for Y –지

So far this lesson has treated ~는지 as a grammatical principle attached to the clause before it. For example:

저는 친구가 어디 가는지 모르겠어요
I don’t know where my friend is going

When ~는지 is added to 가다, there is no space between 가다, ~는 and 지. In this usage ~지 is not a noun; it is part of a larger ending that attaches to verbs or adjectives.

지 has another meaning, completely unrelated to the ~지 described above. We cover it in this lesson too.

In this other meaning, ~지 follows a verb with ~ㄴ/은 attached.

Notice that ~ㄴ/은 is the same addition that is added to verbs in the past tense of ~는 것

For example, you will see:

사귀다 + ㄴ/은 지
사귄 지

먹다 + ㄴ/은 지 먹은 지

Here is what you are looking at.

Remember that ~ㄴ/은 (like ~는 in the present tense and ~ㄹ/을 in the future tense) is added to verbs that describe an upcoming noun. For example:
우리가 먹은 밥
The food we ate
우리가 먹는 밥
The food we eat
우리가 먹을 밥
The food we will eat

Here 지 is a noun, but of a kind we can call a “pseudo-noun.” These nouns can be described by a verb (using ~는 것) or by an adjective, like any noun, but they cannot stand on their own.

You will meet more of these nouns as you go. Some pseudo-nouns coming up soon:

적 in ~ㄴ/은 적이 없다 | Introduced in Lesson 32

(For example: 그것을 한 적이 없어요
I haven’t done that)

수 in ~ㄹ/을 수 있다 | Introduced in Lesson 45

(For example: 그것을 할 수 있어요
I can do that)

줄 in ~ㄹ/을 줄 알다 | Introduced in Lesson 85

(For example: 그것을 할 줄 알아요
I know how to do that)

Here is the situation where the pseudo-noun 지 is used.

Again, placed after a verb with ~ㄴ/은 attached:

사귄 지
먹은 지

… then followed by an indication of time:

사귄 지 6개월
먹은 지 5분

… then followed by 되다 in the past tense:

사귄 지 6개월 됐다
먹은 지 5분 됐다

Remember, 지 is officially a noun, and nouns have meaning. “지” represents the period of time that has passed since the action took place until now. For English speakers it is hard to picture a noun standing for a figurative period of time like this. Here is an image of what “지” means in “사귄 지 6개월 됐다:

Put into a sentence, this could be translated as follows.

여자 친구랑 사귄 지 6개월 됐어

Possible translations:
= I have been going out with my girlfriend for six months
= It has been six months since I started/have been going out with my girlfriend

Another example, one that shows how much context matters in these sentences:

밥을 먹은 지 5분 됐다

Imagine you are eating and your friend walks in and sees you eating. If your friend asks “how long have you been eating?” you could answer:

밥을 먹은 지 5분 됐다
I have been eating for five minutes

= It has been five minutes since I started/have been eating

Now imagine you are not eating, and your friend walks in and sees you not eating. If your friend asks “how long has it been since you last ate?” you could answer with the very same sentence. 지 marks the time from when the action took place until now. The action may still be going on, or it may have stopped. When it has stopped and you want to say how long it has been since it last happened, the Korean sentence is the same as when the action is continuing. Only the English translation changes with the context. So in reply to “how long has it been since you last ate?” you could say:

밥을 먹은 지 5분 됐다
I haven’t eaten for five minutes

= It has been five minutes since I last ate

That is the explanation of 지. Before going deeper, here are some examples to get you used to these sentences.

In the examples below, the translations assume the action is still occurring, so the speaker is saying how long it has been since the action started.

한국에서 산 지 25년 됐어요
I have been living in Korea for 25 years

= It has been 25 years since I started living in Korea

강아지를 기른 지 10년 됐어요
I have been raising a dog for 10 years

= It has been 10 years since I started raising a dog

그 그룹이 훈련을 받은 지 다섯 시간 됐어요
That group has been receiving training for 5 hours

= It has been 5 hours since that group started receiving training

이 아르바이트를 한 지 2 주일 됐어요
I’ve had this part-time job for 2 weeks

= It has been 2 weeks since I started this part-time job

한국에 온 지 2년 됐어요
I have been in Korea for 2 years

= It has been 2 years since I came to Korea

————————-

Let’s discuss some things.

As discussed, 지 in “밥을 먹은 지 5분 됐다” can refer to the time passed (up to now) since one started eating, or since one finished eating. Context tells you which translation fits.

This double meaning only works with certain verbs. For example:

밥을 먹은 지 5분 됐다

This can be used to mean:

= I have been eating for five minutes (in the case that you are currently eating), or
= It has been five minutes since I last ate (in the case that you are currently not eating)

Now go back to the first sentence we built with 지:

여자 친구랑 사귄 지 6개월 됐어

As you have seen, this sentence can mean:

= It has been six months since I started/have been going out with my girlfriend

For that translation to work, you must still be going out with your girlfriend. If you are not currently going out with her, you cannot use this sentence. It cannot mean “It has been six months since I was going out with my girlfriend.” For that, you need the opposite verb, for example:

여자 친구랑 헤어진 지 6개월 됐어
It has been six months since I broke up with my girlfriend

Likewise, look at this sentence with the translations provided:

이제 결혼한 지 1년 됐어요
I have been married for one year

= It has been a year since I got married

That sentence works if you are currently married, but not if you are not.

Which verbs allow this double meaning? It seems to come down to whether the action can repeat or continue. Eating, for example, is not a single instant; the action keeps going.

–

Exercising is not a single instant either; it keeps going. If you are exercising hard and look sweaty, your friend might ask “how long has it been since you started exercising?” You could say:

운동한 지 한 시간 됐어요
I have been exercising for one hour

= It has been one hour since I started exercising

But if you just came home and threw your gym bag on the couch, your friend might ask “how long has it been since you last exercised (or stopped exercising)?” You could again say:

운동한 지 한 시간 됐어요
I haven’t exercised in an hour

= It has been an hour since I stopped exercising

–

Showering is not a single instant; it keeps going. If you are in the shower, your friend might ask “how long has it been since you started showering?” You could say:

샤워한 지 10분 됐어요
I have been showering for 10 minutes

= It has been 10 minutes since I started showering

But if your friend sniffs your armpit and finds it very stinky, your friend might ask “how long has it been since you last showered?” You could again say:

샤워한 지 10분 됐어요
I haven’t showered in 10 minutes

= It has been 10 minutes since I stopped showering

–

Some words do not keep going, though. 결혼하다 refers to the act of getting married, not the state of being married. It does not start or stop; it just happens. So in a sentence like:

이제 결혼한 지 1년 됐어요

You would have to still be married to say that. You never “started” or “stopped” getting married. You just got married, and 지 marks the time from that point until now.

The same applies to 오다, which you saw in an earlier example:

한국에 온 지 2년 됐어요
I have been in Korea for 2 years

= It has been 2 years since I came to Korea

Here 오다 refers to the (completed) act of arriving in Korea. It does not start or finish; it just happens, and 지 marks the time from that point until now. So you would have to still be in Korea to say that sentence.

Another example is 졸업하다 (to graduate):

고등학교를 졸업한 지 1년 됐어요
I graduated one year ago

= It has been a year since I graduated

You would have to have graduated to say that. You never “started” or “stopped” graduating. You just graduated, and 지 marks the time from that point until now.

The good news: real conversations are never this complicated. It only seems so because these sentences have no context. In everyday talk, other information makes the meaning clear. You can also state explicitly that a certain amount of time has passed since an action finished, by describing 지 with a negative sentence. For example:

우리가 안 만난 지 2주일 됐어요
We haven’t met in 2 weeks
밥을 안 먹은 지 아홉 시간 됐어요
I haven’t eaten in nine hours
용돈을 안 받은 지 1년 됐어요
I haven’t received (an) allowance in a year

————————-

지 refers to the period from when an action occurs until the present. You cannot use 지to refer to a period that ended at some other point in the past. To say how long an action went on in the past, use sentences like this:

저는 두 시간 동안 먹었어요
I ate for two hours

English speakers will point out that “I ate for two hours” and “I had eaten for two hours” differ in meaning. Korean usually does not distinguish these and relies on context instead.

————————-

The same kind of sentence can ask how long one has been doing something, using 얼마나 or words like 오래. For example:

한국어를 공부한 지 얼마나 되었어요?
How long have you been studying Korean?
운동한 지 오래 됐어?
Have you been exercising for a long time?

The construction “얼마 안 되다” is a common way to say you have not been doing something for very long. For example

제가 한국에서 산 지 얼마 안 됐어요
I haven’t been living in Korea for very long
제가 우리 학교에서 일한 지 얼마 안 됐어요
I haven’t been working at our school for very long

You also saw in Lesson 28 that this is one of the accepted cases where ~은 can be added to 있다. For example:

여기에 있은 지 얼마나 되었어요?
How long have you been here for?
