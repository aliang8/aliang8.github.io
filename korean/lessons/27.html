내용 = contents

Notes: The word “내용” in Korean is much more common than the English word “contents.” 내용 is broadly used to refer to the “contents” in a book, video, story, or any other source of information. In Korean, it would be common to say something like “저는 그 영화의 내용을 안 좋아했어요” which would translate to “I didn’t like that movie’s content.” In English, it would be more common to just say “I didn’t like that movie.”

Common Usages:

영화 내용

Examples:

기술 = technology, skill

Common Usages:

첨단 기술
cutting edge technology
과학 기술
science and technology

Notes: This word means both “technology” and “skill.” A lot of the times when a word has two difference meanings, their respective Hanja equivalents are different. 기술 is one word, that means both “technology” and “skill.” It doesn’t make sense to me either. The context can tell you what translation should be best.

For example, when used to mean “technology:”

지난 50년 동안 과학 기술은 사람들의 생각을 많이 변화시켰어요
Technology has really changed the way people think over the past 50 years
다음 10년의 의료기술개발은 대한민국에 중요하다
The development of medical technology over the next 10 years is important to Korea

On the other hand, when used to mean “skill”

저는 학생들이 과학에 관심이 생기게 하는 기술이 없어요
I don’t have the skills to make students interested in Science
학교를 다닐 때 실용적인 기술을 많이 배우는 게 아주 중요해요
It is important to learn practical skills when you attend school

밝히다 = to lighten something, to reveal

The pronunciation of this word is closer to “발키다”

Notes: This word is used when making something brighter. For example:

However, in this same sense it can be used to refer to “brightening” a situation with more information. This is commonly translated to “reveal.” For example:

지나다 = for something to pass by

Notes: This can be used in a variety of situations, each with the same general meaning of something “passing by.” It is often used after an indication of time, for example:

그때부터 시간이 많이 지났어요
A lot of time has passed since then

Notice here that ~이/가 should be placed on the indication of time.

It can also be used to indicate that one has passed a location. For example:

우리는 30분 전에 그 도시를 지났어요
We passed that city 30 minutes ago

Notice here that ~을/를 should be placed on the location

Common Usages:

지나가다
to go past

Examples:

근무하다 = to be employed

The noun form of this word (“근무”) translates to “work”

Notes: This word is very similar to 일하다. However, when using 일하다, the place where one works should have ~에서 attached to it. For example:

저는 삼성에서 일해요
I work at 삼성.

I have noticed that ~에 or ~에서 are possible when indicating that place where one works using 근무하다. For example, both of these are fine:

그 선생님이 우리 학교에 10년 동안 근무했어요

그 선생님이 우리 학교에서 10년 동안 근무했어요

1

Describe Past Tense with~던 and~았/었던

One caveat before starting: this lesson describes differences that most Korean people cannot put into words. Native speakers feel subtle distinctions intuitively but struggle to express them. The explanations below do their best to describe that feeling.

For a long time now you have been able to describe a following noun with adjectives. For example:

예쁜 여자
pretty girl
똑똑한 학생
smart student
나쁜 선생님
bad teacher

In the previous lesson you learned to describe a following noun with verbs. For example, in the present tense:

영어를 공부하는 학생
the student that/who studies English
한국어를 가르치는 선생님
the teacher that/who teaches Korean

… the future tense:

영어를 공부할 학생
the student that/who will study English
한국어를 가르칠 선생님
the teacher that/who will teach Korean

… and the past tense:

영어를 공부한 학생
the student that/who studied English
한국어를 가르친 선생님
the teacher that/who taught Korean

As you learned in the previous lesson (and as the two examples just above show), you can attach ~ㄴ/은 to a verb stem to describe a following noun in the past tense.

Besides ~ㄴ/은, there are other ways to describe a following noun in the past tense. Both of them involve the grammar pattern ~더~. Notice the “~” both before and after “더”: ~더~ attaches to things, and things attach to it.

~더~ is a fairly advanced pattern with several uses. It is covered in detail late in Unit 5, with ~던가 (Lesson 117), ~더라 (Lesson 118) and ~더니 (Lesson 119). For now, we focus on the nuance ~더~ adds when describing a following noun.

When ~더~ appears in a grammar pattern, it signals that the speaker is recalling or remembering some fact from past experience. ~더~ attaches to a verb stem, and ~ㄴ/은 (the same ~ㄴ/은 that describes nouns in the past tense from the previous lesson) attaches to ~더~.

Simply put, one use is: Verb stem + ~더~ + ~ㄴ/은

The result is Verb stem + ~던, which describes a following noun in the past tense. For example:

내가 입던 옷

The hard part of this lesson is understanding the difference between these:

Full sentences come later, once the subtle differences between the ways of describing a noun in the past tense have been explained.

  • 내가 입은 옷 = The clothes I wore
  • 내가 입던 옷 = The clothes I wore

Despite their similar (or identical) translations, the two constructions above differ.

First, remember that ~더~ signals that the speaker is recalling something experienced. In the second example, the speaker is explicitly saying that this comes from memory, something the person remembers doing or experiencing.

The first example is just a simple fact, without this added “memory” feeling. That does not mean it is not being recalled; it just is not expressed explicitly.

This sounds vague. When Korean speakers try to describe the image they have of ~더~ (and specifically ~던 compared to ~ㄴ/은), they tend to gesture as if something were popping out of their head.

In theory, ~던 could be translated differently. For example:

내가 입던 옷
The clothes I recall wearing

But that translation would not normally be used. The “recalling” feeling of ~더~ here is very subtle, more of a feeling, and unless context says otherwise, the focus is on the clothes rather than the recollection.

Besides this “memory from experience” feeling, ~던 in the construction above also indicates that the action happened repeatedly in the past. “내가 입던 옷” is a good example: the clothes being described were worn on multiple occasions (which makes sense, since they are clothes).

With “내가 입은 옷,” you are just stating a simple fact, with no information about “memory” or whether you wore the clothes once or many times. All it says is that at some time in the past you wore those clothes. ~ㄴ/은 is a very simple way to say that.

So ~던 adds personal experience or memory and says the action happened repeatedly. With that in mind, look at these examples and think about which is correct:

  • 내가 입던 옷
  • 내가 사던 옷

In the first example, “wearing” a piece of clothing can happen repeatedly, so this construction is possible.
In the second, “buying” a piece of clothing does not happen repeatedly. Once you buy it, you do not buy it again. So this construction is unacceptable (or at least very uncommon).

That pair shows the “repeated” meaning of ~던 using two different verbs. Below, the same verb is used, with ~던 on the first and ~ㄴ/은 on the second. Look at these and think about which is correct:

  • 내가 태어난 도시
  • 내가 태어나던 도시

In the first example, ~ㄴ/은 marks a simple past fact with no extra meaning unless the sentence implies it: “the city that I was born in.”
In the second, “being born” cannot happen repeatedly, so it is hard to imagine a situation where this would be appropriate.

There is one more subtlety of ~던. We know the speaker is recalling from memory and that the action happened repeatedly. It is also possible that this repeated action continues into the present (or whatever time the sentence describes). Unless the sentence says otherwise, the repeated action has not stopped.

Back to the classic “clothes” example to spell this out:

내가 입은 옷
The speaker is describing clothes he wore. There is no hidden meaning. All we know is that at some point in the past he wore them. Often? Once? Does he still wear them? Has he stopped? All of that is ambiguous, and rightly so. In conversation you often do not need to describe the noun precisely; “the clothes I wore” is enough. The English sentence is just as ambiguous as the Korean one.

내가 입던 옷
The speaker is describing clothes he recalls wearing (the function of ~더~). They were worn repeatedly in the past and are likely still worn now, not necessarily at this moment but “these days” (or at whatever time the sentence describes). Nothing says he stopped wearing them, and unless stated otherwise, he probably still wears them from time to time.

The constructions so far have no context. In real sentences you will usually have more information to help you read them. For example, the construction above in a sentence:

내가 지금까지 입던 옷을 내일 버릴 거야
Tomorrow, I am going to throw out the clothes that I have been wearing until now

The ~던 in this sentence tells us:

  • You have a recollection of wearing the clothes
  • You wore the clothes repeatedly in the past
  • The clothes haven’t “stopped” being worn – and they are still being worn “until now.” Of course, you won’t be wearing them after today because you’ll be throwing them out.

Here are many more examples:

내가 읽던 책은 재미없었어
The book I was reading wasn’t funny
이 빵은 내가 자주 먹던 빵이야
This bread is bread that I ate often
제가 매일 마시던 주스를 샀어요
I bought the juice that I drink everyday
여기가 내가 근무하던 곳이야
This place (here) is the place that I worked
2015년에 쓰던 달력을 내일 버릴 거예요
Tomorrow, I am going to throw out the calendar that I used to use in 2015
제가 자주 가던 약국이 오늘부터 문을 닫을 거예요
The pharmacy that I had been going to often will close its doors from today

In the examples above, the object is described by an action that “I” did. One subtlety of ~던 is the feeling of “remembering/recalling.” Here, of course I remember, because “I” did the action, so this subtle meaning, though present, seems irrelevant. Still, remember that ~더~ carries it.

When the object is described by an action that another person did, the subtleties of ~던 are more apparent. For example:

이 빵은 슬기가 자주 먹던 빵이야
This bread is bread that Seulgi eats often
이곳에 근무하던 우체국 직원이 삶을 영원히 포기했어요
The worker/employee who used to work at this post-office gave up on his life forever
길을 밝히던 불이 꺼지고 나서 길이 어두워졌어요
The street became dark after the light that used to brighten the street went out
방금 들어온 사람은 우리 가게에 자주 오던 손님이야
The person who just came in is a customer who comes to the store often

The English translation above uses the present tense “come.” You could argue that “came” or “used to come” fits better, since the person is described as a customer who came in the past. But in context they “just came in” and are probably still there, so the present tense works too. These sentences are hard to translate; focus on the Korean and use the English only as a reference.

Because ~던 (through ~더~) signals “remembering/recalling,” the bread is not just “bread that Seulgi eats often” but more specifically “bread that I remember/recall/experienced Seulgi eating often.”

Again, such translations are clumsy; a real translation would never include “remember/recall/experienced.” It is better to understand the subtle meaning and forget the English. There is no great translation for ~던. With everything you know about ~던, how would you translate this:

내가 입던 옷

Would you translate it to:

The clothes I wore
The clothes I used to wear
The clothes often wore

All of these could describe ~던 here, but none captures its subtleties. To add to the confusion, there is another way to describe a following noun that looks like ~던 and has a similar meaning. That comes next.

———————————–

So far you have seen ~던 describing a following noun and compared it to ~ㄴ/은. For example:

내가 입은 옷
The clothes I wore – (Simple description of the clothes in the past tense)
내가 입던 옷
The clothes I wore – (Recalling/remembering the clothes that were worn repeatedly in the past and continued to the point in time being described)

You can also add ~았/었~ between the verb stem and ~던. For example:

내가 입었던 옷
The clothes I wore

Now for the difference between ~았/었던 and ~던 or ~ㄴ/은.

When ~았/었던 is added to a verb to describe a following noun, the speaker is saying the action has completely finished and is not happening now. Back to the tried and tested example:

내가 입었던 옷
The speaker is describing clothes he wore in the past. Because ~더~ is included, these are clothes he recalls. They were worn at some time in the past (possibly stated elsewhere in the sentence) and are not clothes the speaker wears anymore.

The meaning and feeling of ~았/었던 is similar to ~ㄴ/은. Most Koreans would say at first glance that they are the same. Here is the best attempt to separate them.

As mentioned, “내가 입은 옷” states a simple fact, with no information about “memory,” about whether you wore the clothes once or many times, or whether you still wear them. All it says is that at some time in the past you wore those clothes. ~ㄴ/은 is a very simple way to say that.

“내가 입었던 옷” is similar, but ~더~ shows that the speaker is “remembering/recalling” this from experience. It also specifically says that “wearing the clothes” is finished and no longer happens. ~ㄴ/은 can express that too, but its meaning is broader and does not say so explicitly.

Koreans often assume that ~았/었던 means a lot of time has passed since the action. Many native speakers describe it this way, so it is hard to call that “incorrect.” But the interval between when the action stopped and now is actually irrelevant. For example, you could say:

오늘 아침에 입었던 바지

~았/었던 here does not show that the pants were worn long ago (“this morning” is not long ago). It shows that the pants were worn and are not being worn now. If you said this, you would have had to change or take off the pants at some point since the morning, because ~았/었던 says you no longer wear them. Compare the construction above with this:

오늘 아침에 입은 바지

Because the pants were worn (or put on) in the morning, you are probably still wearing them. This sentence does not explicitly say whether you took them off or still have them on.

Below are examples of ~았/었던. Notice how hard the nuance of ~았/었던 is to translate. The past perfect “had” is a common way to express the feeling in English, but context does not always require it. Try not to focus on the translations; remember the purpose of ~았/었던:

그 컴퓨터는 작년에 썼던 거예요
That is the computer I had used last year
저는 애기가 썼던 휴지를 다 버렸어요
I threw out all the tissue paper that the baby had used
한때 포기했던 시험을 다시 공부했어요
I again studied for the exam that I once gave up on
우리가 지난 번에 먹었던 곳에서 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat at the place that we ate at last time
대통령이 여기에 살았던 시민들에게 돈을 줬어요
The president gave money to those who once lived here
우리가 지난 주에 배웠던 내용을 다시 공부할 거예요
I will study the contents that we had learned last week again
작년에 사업에 실패했던 남자가 새로운 기술을 발견했어요
The man who had failed at (starting his own) business last year discovered a new technology
한때 저의 손님이었던 사람이 이제는 유명한 예술가가 되었다
The person who had been my customer at one time has now become a famous artist
고장으로 계속 멈추는 버스를 탔던 승객들이 다른 버스를 탔어요
The passengers who had been riding the bus that kept stopping because it is/was broken got on another bus

Here is everything so far, organized:

~ㄴ/은: Attached to a verb to describe a noun where the action happened sometime in the past. No extra meaning; all we know is that the action happened at some point.

~던: Attached to a verb to describe a noun where the action is recalled as happening repeatedly in the past and continuing to the present (or to the time described in the sentence).

~았/었던: Attached to a verb to describe a noun where the action is recalled as happening in the past, but has finished and does not happen now.

Distinguishing these by their English translations is not ideal; given all the possible situations, none translates perfectly. Still, the examples below show common translations for each:

내가 입은 바지
the pants I wore
내가 입던 바지
the pants I used to wear (I find this translation particularly insufficient)
내가 입었던 바지
the pants I had worn

Here is an image placing each one along a timeline, together with the other additions from Lesson 26:

English translations are deliberately left out, because describing when the pants are worn, in either language, depends heavily on context. Notice the exclamation mark “!” on the last two, showing the feeling that those actions are recalled from experience.

2

Describing Past Tense with~던 and~았/었던 (Adjectives)

That should feel comfortable by now. Unfortunately, we still need to cover how ~던 and ~았/었던 attach to adjectives.

As you know, adding ~ㄴ/은 to an adjective lets you describe a noun. For example:

예쁜 여자
(a) pretty girl

Although “예쁜” says the girl is currently pretty, we can infer she was pretty in the past too. Usually, if an adjective describes a noun a certain way in the present, the same was true in the past.

Earlier you learned ~았/었던 with verbs. ~았/었던 can also attach to adjectives with a similar function. With a verb, it says the action happened in the past but has finished and no longer occurs.

With an adjective, ~았/었던 says the noun was that way in the past but no longer is. For example, think how unfortunate this situation would be:

예뻤던 여자

This says the girl was (or “had been”) pretty, but her being “pretty” is over and she is no longer pretty. ~더~ also adds the “remembering/recalling” from experience subtlety. Forced into a translation, the construction above would be:

예뻤던 여자
The girl who I recall being pretty, but is not pretty anymore

So far we have covered the subtle differences between ~ㄴ/은, ~던 and ~았/었던 on verbs,

and between ~ㄴ/은 and ~았/었던 on adjectives.

Adding ~던 to an adjective is possible but very confusing.

Everything above was discussed point by point with a Korean grammar teacher: the differences between ~ㄴ/은, ~던 and ~았/었던 on verbs, then ~ㄴ/은 and ~았/었던 on adjectives. Then came the question:

All of this led me to this question:

“If ~던 is added to verbs to show that one ‘remembers/recalls’ an action occurring repeatedly in the past and continuing to the present, how can this meaning transfer to adjectives?”

It seemed unlikely to make sense, but she was shown this construction:

예쁘던 여자

She said it would not be used, and that adding “~던” directly to adjectives is incorrect because an adjective cannot “occur” repeatedly. Challenged to find a situation where ~던 attaches to an adjective, the answer came from a song.

Remember, this conversation is between a Korean person who teaches Korean to Korean high school students and a man from Canada.

The example is a bit above your level, but here are lines from the song “나에게 넌”:

소중했던 우리 푸르던 날을 기억하며
우~~후회없이 그림처럼 남아주기를
나에게 넌 내 외롭던 지난 시간을

Notice that ~던 is attached directly to two adjectives in these lyrics: to 푸르다 in the first line and to 외롭다 in the third. The first word, 소중하다, has ~았/었던 attached.

Hearing this, she reconsidered and said that ~던 on an adjective means the same as ~았/었던, but the latter is much more common.

That explanation does not hold up; ~았/었던 and ~던 cannot have identical meanings. The story is here only to show that even Koreans, including “experts” in their own language, struggle to distinguish these.

~던 on adjectives is not simple, because shown in isolation with a single noun it often sounds very unnatural. For example:

예쁜 여자
the pretty girl
예뻤던 여자
the girl who I recall being pretty, but is not pretty anymore

예쁘던 여자 – Grammatically correct, but Koreans would tell you it would never be said

In some cases, though, the adjective and noun make it appropriate. For example:

시끄러운 교실
the noisy classroom
시끄러웠던 교실
the classroom that I recall being noisy, but is not noisy anymore

시끄럽던 교실 – This construction is possible. The “repeated” function of ~던 does not really apply, but the function of continuing to the present does. If you said “시끄럽던 교실” to a Korean, they would assume the classroom was loud up until the present and just stopped being loud. With “시끄러웠던 교실,” they would say the classroom was loud further in the past, with a gap between when it stopped being loud and now.

This may explain why “예쁘던 여자” is unnatural. You cannot really recognize that a girl was pretty in the past, continuing right up to the present, and then suddenly not pretty anymore. But it is possible (however unfortunate) for a girl to have been pretty in the past, for time to pass, and for her to have stopped being pretty somewhere in between.

So some adjectives can describe some nouns with ~던, while with others it is inappropriate. For example:

Again, please do not pay too much attention to the English translations:

조용하던 길
The street that I recall being quiet until now
따뜻하던 날씨
The weather that I recall being warm until now
맑던 하늘
The sky that I recall being clear until now

For example:

맑던 하늘이 갑자기 어두워졌어요
The sky that I recall being clear until now suddenly became dark
따뜻하던 날씨가 비가 오고 나서 추워졌어요
The weather that I remember being warm until now got cold after it rained
조용하던 길이 사람들이 집에서 나오기 시작하고 나서  시끄러워졌어요
The street that I recall being quiet until now got loud after people started coming out of their houses

———————————

One point amid all this confusion: in most cases, in English or Korean (or any language), these subtle differences do not need to be distinguished. For example:

“The clear sky became dark”

Was the sky clear until the present and then became dark?
Was the sky clear sometime in the past and then became dark sometime in the past?
Was the sky clear sometime in the past and then became dark in the present?
Was the sky clear every day (habitually) before becoming dark?
Will the sky become clear again after becoming dark?

The answers to all of these questions, and any others you can think of, are irrelevant. Learners of Korean often overthink the meaning of something away from its context. In reality, context usually explains everything. For example, look at the ambiguous way “the sky” (하늘) is described in the following example:

맑? 하늘이 갑자기 어두워졌어요

Whatever replaces the “?” above, the rest of the sentence clarifies the meaning and result, much as the English sentences below all amount to the same thing:

The sky that was clear all of a sudden became dark
The sky that had been clear all of a sudden became dark
The sky that used to be clear all of a sudden became dark
The sky that is habitually clear all of a sudden became dark
The sky that I recall being clear all of a sudden became dark

———————————–

That is as far as this goes. As noted at the start, the difference between ~던 and ~았/었던 (compared with the patterns from the previous lesson) is very confusing. If you reach this point still a little unclear, you are in the same position as a typical Korean speaker.

That’s it!
