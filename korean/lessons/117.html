Introduction

In this lesson, you will meet the grammatical principle ~더~ and the meaning it creates. You will then combine –더- with another grammatical principle to create ~던가.

1

Recalling a Fact from Experience:~더~

~더~ is interesting. The connecting lines on both sides of 더 show that it not only attaches to something, but also has something attached to it.

Over the next three lessons, you will learn three grammatical principles that are commonly attached to ~더~.

~더~ + ~ㄴ/가
~던가 (for example = 아프던가)
~더~ + ~라
~더라 (for example = 아프더라)
~더~ + (으)니
~더니 (for example = 아프더니)

You first saw ~더~ in Lesson 27, where it described upcoming nouns as ~던 and ~았/었던.

The meaning ~더~ creates depends heavily on the principle attached to it. Still, constructions with ~더~ usually carry the meaning of ~더~ plus the meaning of the next principle. So before the upcoming lessons, here is a reminder of what ~더~ means from Lesson 27.

When ~더~ is part of a grammatical principle, it signals that the speaker is recalling (or asking somebody to recall) some fact from the past that was experienced. Keep this in mind when learning principles that include ~더~. It will also help you tell apart two similar-looking principles that differ only by ~더~. Let’s look at one application of ~더~: ~던가.

2

Asking Questions to Yourself Using~던가

In Lesson 21, you learned how to ask questions, including by ending a sentence with ~ㄴ/은가. Adding ~ㄴ/은가 to ~더~ forms ~던가, a specific way of asking a question.

That lesson noted that ~ㄴ/은가 is mostly used with adjectives, not verbs. However, the construction ~던가 can attach to verbs.

~던가 is most commonly used when the speaker is asking a question to him or herself. For example:

내가 이 책을 읽었던가?
Did I read this book?

~던가 is used when the speaker is unsure of something that was experienced, and so should probably be known. For some reason, perhaps a lapse in memory, the speaker cannot remember for sure and asks himself or herself to draw on that experience.

We do this in English too. Imagine you look at your bookcase and see about 100 books. You read a lot, so you sometimes cannot remember which books you have read. You pull one out and ask yourself whether you have read it before:

내가 이 책을 읽었던가?
Did/have I read this book?

Technically, you should know whether you have read the book. It is your experience. But sometimes you cannot remember perfectly.

In the example above, ~았/었 is attached to the word before ~더~ to show that the experience was in the past. Most of the time the speaker asks about a past experience, but a present tense conjugation is possible if the situation allows. For example:

내가 이 책을 읽던가?

This would translate to something like “Am I reading this book?” or “Do I read this book?”
Would this make sense anywhere? It sounds like the speaker cannot remember whether he was reading the book at all. Grammatically possible, but probably not the meaning you want.

Now look at these two examples, both of which are possible:

이 과자가 비싸던가?
Is this candy expensive?
이 과자가 비쌌던가?
Was this candy expensive?

Both sentences mean essentially the same thing, mainly because if something was expensive, it is usually still expensive now. The nuance is subtle. The second sentence (with ~았/었) refers to a specific time in the past when the speaker bought the candy and asks whether it was expensive then. The first (present tense) is not about a specific past experience but a general one that still applies. The speaker is asking himself about his experience of a general truth.

You typically cannot use ~던가 to ask yourself anything but a “yes” or “no” question. For example, the following sounds unnatural in Korean:

내가 이 책을 무슨 날에 읽었던가?

This asks yourself “what day did I read this book?”, which needs an answer other than “yes” or “no.” So the sentence is awkward.

You might conclude that you simply cannot use a “question word” like 언제, 어디, 뭐 in these sentences. Good thinking, but it is not that simple.

Remember, in Lesson 25 you learned how to use question words not to ask questions but to refer to something ambiguous (a place, time, person, etc.). For example, depending on how it is used, “뭐 먹었어요?” could have two meanings:
뭐 먹었어요?
What did you eat?
뭐 먹었어요?
Did you eat something?

You can use question words in sentences ending with ~던가, but only when they refer to something ambiguous. Some examples:

——————————-

내가 이 책을 언제 읽었던가?

This does not translate to “When did I read this book?” Instead, “언제” refers to an ambiguous time, so it translates to:

내가 이 책을 언제 읽었던가?
Did I read this book at some time?

——————————-

그 사람이 어디 갔던가?

This does not translate to “Where did that person go?” Instead, “어디” refers to an ambiguous place, so it translates to:

그 사람이 어디 갔던가?
Did that person go to some place?

——————————-

오빠가 나한테 뭐라고 했던가?

This does not translate to “What did my brother say to me?” Instead, “뭐” refers to an ambiguous thing (that was said), so it translates to:

오빠가 나한테 뭐라고 했던가?
Did my brother say something to me?
3

Asking Questions to Somebody Else Using~던가

~던가 is quite common for asking yourself a question. It is used to ask another person only in informal situations, usually an older person asking a much younger one. This is not worth dwelling on, but you should be aware of it. Some examples:

슬기가 많이 아프던가?
Is Seulgi really sick?
네가 생선을 먹던가?
Do you eat fish?
네가 핸드폰을 샀던가?
Did you buy a cell-phone?

This form is not very common, though, and it is usually more appropriate to ask with some other form, like ~ㄴ/은가? or ~나?
