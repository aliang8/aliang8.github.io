자라다 = to grow up

You can use 자라다 to indicate the location in which one grows up. For example:

When a person does something for the majority of his/her childhood, we often use the phrase “I grew up doing” in English. For example:

Or simply, to indicate the location that you grew up, we would say:

To create these phrases in Korean, you can use the word “자라다” (to grow up) in combination with ~(으)면서. For example:

Introduction

This lesson covers saying that one does two (or more) actions at the same time, using ~면서 and ~며. You will see how they are similar and how they differ.

1

To do two actions at the same time:~(으)면서

When two actions happen at the same time, you can join the two clauses in one sentence with ~(으)면서. ~면서 attaches to stems ending in a vowel (or in ㄹ), and ~으면서 attaches to stems ending in a consonant (other than ㄹ). A very simple example:

저는 밥을 먹으면서 공부했어요
I ate (rice) while studying

~(으)면서 shows that “eating rice” and “studying” happened at the same time. With verbs like this, ~(으)면서 is usually translated “as…” or “while…”. More examples:

저는 영화를 보면서 콜라를 마셨어요
I drank cola while watching a movie
학업을 진행하면서 직장도 다녀요
As I progress/continue my studies, I also go to work
이 기계를 돌리면서 땅콩을 갈아요
As you turn this machine, the peanuts get/are ground
도움이 필요하면 이 벨을 누르면서 말씀하세요
If you need help, speak out while pressing this button
눈이 길에 계속 쌓이면서 길이 점점 미끄러워져요
As snow piles up (gradually) on the road, it gets more and more slippery
우리가 거기에 가는 길을 몰라서 지도를 보면서 앞에 있는 차를 뒤따라가고 있어요
We don’t know how to get there, so we are looking at a map while following the car in-front of us
이 행사에서 지난 10년의 성공을 기념하면서 이 동영상을 보겠습니다
At this event, we will commemorate the success of the last 10 years and watch this video
저의 여자친구는 운동하면서 운동하고 있는 다른 남자들을 항상 쳐다봐요
My girlfriend always stares at other guys when she is exercising
다음 주에 부산에 가서 좋은 추억을 쌓으면서 재미있는 시간을 보내자
Next week let’s go to Busan and spend some fun time while piling up good memories
음악을 들으면서 다음 주 일정을 세웠어요
While listening to music, I set up the schedule for next week

Above, ~으면서 was said to attach to stems ending in a consonant other than ㄹ. So why is ~으면서 added to “들” here? The original word is “듣다” (to hear/to listen). Adding ~으면서 changes ㄷ to ㄹ. See the irregulars in Lesson 7.

~(으)면서 can attach to adjectives too. Its meaning is unchanged, but adjectives don’t “occur” the way verbs do, so “as…” or “while…” sometimes doesn’t fit. Instead, the speaker is saying something has the properties of both adjectives. For example:

이 음식은 매우면서 짜요
This food is spicy and salty
오늘 날씨가 흐리면서 쌀쌀해요
The weather today is cloudy and chilly
그 기념비가 아름다우면서 흥미로워요
That monument is beautiful and interesting

You may wonder how the sentences above differ from these sentences with ~고:

이 음식은 맵고 짜요
This food is spicy and salty
오늘 날씨가 흐리고 쌀쌀해요
The weather today is cloudy and chilly
그 기념비가 아름답고 흥미로워요
That monument is beautiful and interesting

Their meanings are very close, and you don’t really need to tell them apart (the English translations don’t). ~(으)면서 gives the feeling that the two descriptions are interrelated, while ~고 simply gives two descriptions. It is much like these two English sentences, which you wouldn’t need to distinguish either:

  • The food, while being spicy, is also salty
  • The food is spicy and salty

~면서 can also attach to 이다. Like adjectives, “이다” doesn’t “occur” the way a verb does. Instead the speaker says something “is” one thing while also being another. For example:

그 사람은 저의 부장이면서 친구예요
That person is my boss and (while also being) my friend
그 사람은 의사이면서 교수입니다
That person is a doctor and (while also being a) professor
요즘 핸드폰은 전화기이면서 컴퓨터예요
Cell phones these days are phones and (while also being) computers

When a person did something for most of their childhood, English uses “I grew up doing.” For example:

I grew up playing hockey
I grew up listening to that type of music

Or, for the place you grew up:
I grew up in Canada

In Korean, use “자라다” (to grow up) with ~(으)면서. For example:

저는 하키를 하면서 자랐어요
I grew up playing hockey
저는 아빠랑 같이 하키를 하면서 자랐어요
I grew up playing hockey with my dad
저는 그런 음악을 들으면서 자랐어요
I grew up listening to that type of music

Often we want to say that a person does an action while in some “state” or “position,” as in these sentences:

I studied while laying down
I watched a movie while sitting
I ate while standing up

You learned how to make these sentences in Lesson 17 by attaching ~아/어서 to these verbs. For example:
저는 누워서 책을 읽었어요
I lied down and read a book
나는 앉아서 쉴 거야
I’m going to sit down and relax
저는 줄에 서서 순서를 기다렸어요
I stood in line and waited for my turn

Attaching ~(으)면서 to these words gives a different meaning, probably not the one you want. For example, if you said:

저는 앉으면서 공부했어요

Remember, “앉다” means “to sit,” as in going from standing to sitting. So the sentence above says you studied while going from a standing position to a sitting position. You technically could do (and say) that, but 99.9% of the time that is not the meaning you want.

~(으)면서 has a second usage. Here the same person does both clauses, but the clauses directly oppose each other. It is typically used when talking about another person: “Ugh, he does this, but then does that!” For example:

그 여자가 식당을 열고 싶다고 하면서 요리를 못해요
That girl says she wants to open up a restaurant, but she doesn’t know how to cook
저의 와이프가 외국 브랜드를 좋아하면서 한국에서 만들어진 제품만 사요
My wife likes foreign brands, but only buys products made in Korea
그 사람이 한국에서 살면서 한국어를 할 수 없어요
That person lives in Korea, but he can’t speak Korean
그가 저에게 기념품을 사 준다고 하면서 아무 것도 안 샀어요
He said he would buy (and give) me a souvenir, but didn’t buy anything
그 사안이 급하다고 하면서 그냥 앉아 있어요
He said the matter is urgent, but is just sitting there

~도 can be added to ~(으)면서 here for extra emphasis. Lesson 107 covers adding ~도 to more complex grammatical principles like ~(으)면서.

That covers ~(으)면서. Before finishing, let’s compare ~(으)며 with ~(으)면서.

You can see ~(으)면서 (and ~(으)며, introduced next) in this Korean sign.

2

While:~(으)며

~(으)며 is a shortened form of ~(으)면서. In theory, ~(으)며 could replace it in every example above where two actions happen at the same time. For example:

저는 영화를 보며 콜라를 마셨어요
I drank cola while watching a movie
학업을 진행하며 직장도 다녀요
As I progress/continue my studies, I also go to work

In practice, ~(으)면서 is much, much more common in these sentences, especially when two actions literally go on continuously at the same time. This sentence, which says peanuts are ground continuously as the machine turns, is much better with ~(으)면서:

이 기계를 돌리면서 땅콩을 갈아요
As you turn this machine, the peanuts get/are ground

So use ~(으)면서 when two actions (or descriptions) happen at the same time, but know that ~(으)며 can technically do this too.

~(으)며 has another meaning that ~(으)면서 does not. Often ~(으)며 simply lists or connects two clauses with a similar idea. For example:

한국이 사계절이 있으며 겨울이 너무 추워요
In Korea, there is four seasons, so/and winters are very cold
이 제품은 가장 빠른 컴퓨터이며 한국에서 만들어졌어요
This product is the fastest computer, and it was made in Korea
저쪽으로 가면 앞문이 나오며 이쪽으로 가면 뒷문이 나와요
If you go that way, you will see the front door, and if you go that way, you will see the back door
주식이 더 많이 떨어질 수 있으며 이는 한국경제에 안 좋은 영향을 미칠 거예요
The stocks could drop more, and this could have a bad effect on the Korean economy
이 제품은 한국에서 매우 유명하며 이 제품을 사러 관광객들이 한국을 방문해요
This product is very popular in Korea, and many tourists visit Korea to buy it
우리 회사가 상반기에는 그 제품의 개발을 진행하며 하반기에는 판매를 시작할 거예요
In the first half of the year, our company will proceed with the development with that product, and in the second half of the year, we will start selling it

Here ~(으)며 is quite like ~고 from Lesson 17: it just connects two related clauses. ~(으)며 is common in formal settings where instructions go to a large group through overhead broadcasts or signs. A sign telling you not to jaywalk might read:

무단횡단이 불법이며 적발 시 벌금을 내야 돼요
Jaywalking is illegal, and/so if you are caught, you will have to pay a fine

If ~(으)며 replaced ~(으)면서 in the earlier examples where two clauses oppose each other, the feeling shifts toward this function of “connecting two clauses with a similar idea.” Take this sentence:

그 사람이 한국에서 살면서 한국어를 할 수 없어요
That person lives in Korea, but he can’t speak Korean

With ~(으)면서, as shown earlier, the two clauses oppose each other. If ~(으)며 replaced ~(으)면서 here, the sentence would more likely just connect the two clauses. For example:

그 사람이 한국에서 살며 한국어를 할 수 없어요
That person lives in Korea, and he can’t speak Korean

Notice the difference between “but” in the first example (with ~(으)면서) and in the second (with ~(으)며). In the second, the speaker is just connecting two related ideas. In the first, the speaker is specifically saying the two clauses oppose each other. The practical result is usually the same, but be aware of the feeling.

So ~(으)며 has a meaning that ~(으)면서 lacks (listing or connecting two clauses), and ~(으)면서 is the more common way to say two actions happen at the same time. The two are not identical.
