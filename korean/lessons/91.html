Introduction

In this lesson you will learn how to add ~거든(요) to the end of a sentence, or use it to connect two clauses. As with many Korean grammatical principles, the meaning of ~거든 depends on the situation and context. This lesson breaks down each meaning of ~거든(요) and how to use it.

1

Teaching the listener what he or she doesn’t know~거든(요)

In Lesson 37 you learned how a speaker can give a reason with ~아/어서. ~아/어서 usually connects two clauses to mean “so/therefore/because.” For example:

저는 밥을 먹고 있어서 지금 가고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to go now because I am eating
바닥이 추워서 실내화를 신었어요
I put on slippers (indoor shoes) because the floor is/was cold
우리는 비영리재단이라서 돈을 안 받아요
We don’t accept money because we are a non-profit organization

Given the right context, the clause after ~아/어서 can be left out. These sentences are usually answers to a question asking “why” something is or is not happening. For example:

Why don’t you want to go now?

저는 밥을 먹고 있어서…
Because I am eating…

Why are you wearing slippers?

바닥이 추워서…
… Because the floor is cold…

Why don’t you accept money?

우리는 비영리재단이라서…
Because we are a non-profit organization…

These are not really full sentences in English or Korean, but you will often hear them when the right context leads up to them.

You can also place ~거든(요) after a clause to give a reason. The three examples above could be written as:

Why don’t you want to go now?

저는 밥을 먹고 있거든요
Because I am eating

Why are you wearing slippers?

바닥이 춥거든
… Because the floor is cold

Why don’t you accept money?

우리는 비영리재단이거든
Because we are a non-profit organization

A real example: at a hotel breakfast buffet, a husband noticed his wife had taken about 10 macadamia nuts. He asked “마카다미아를 왜 이렇게 많이 가져왔어?” (Why did you take so many macadamia nuts?) She answered:

마카다미아를 좋아하거든!
Because I like macadamia (nuts)

Again, these are technically not full sentences in English, even though they are natural to say. You use them when somebody asks a question and you only need to give the reason. In other words, the speaker is giving (or teaching) information the listener does not know. The context does not have to come from another person’s question. The speaker can set up their own context and then give the reason. For example:

저는 요즘에 너무 늦게 자요. 일이 많거든요
These days I am going to bed too late. Because I have so much work.

In both situations, ~거든(요) gives the listener information they do not yet know. In a sense, this is the opposite of ~잖아(요) from the previous lesson.

~거든(요) attaches easily to verbs, adjectives and 이다, because adding ~ㄱ to a stem never triggers irregulars. Here are many examples:

In the examples below, the speaker provides the prior context that leads them to say a sentence with ~거든(요). This information could also come from another person; the speaker gives it here for simplicity, and so you can see clearly that ~거든(요) provides new information the listener did not already have.

문을 닫았어요. 이제 냉방 중이거든요.
I closed the door. Because the air conditioner is running.
셔츠를 입어야 될 것 같아요. 이두근이 너무 크거든요.
Looks like I’d better put on a shirt. Because my biceps are too big.
이 제품이 인기가 많아요. 품질이 아주 좋거든요.
This product is popular. Because the quality (of it) is very good.
컴퓨터를 쓸 때 고개를 그렇게 숙이지 마. 목이 아프거든
When you use a computer, don’t bend your head forward like that. Your neck will be sore(Korean people commonly use “

아프다” in the present tense to suggest that one does not do something because he or she will get/be hurt. For example, if somebody were about to pick up a heavy box, you could say “하지 마! 허리 아파!”

오늘 우리가 밖에 나가야 돼요. 날씨가 아주 화창하거든요
We need to go outside today. The weather is very bright and clear.
트럼프 대통령이 미국과 멕시코 국경에 벽을 못 세워요. 국경이 너무 길거든요.
Trump can’t build a wall on the border between America and Mexico. The border is too big.

So far every example has ~거든(요) attached to a present-tense word. You can also give the listener information about the past by attaching it to ~았/었. For example:

물속에 안 들어갔어. 너무 무서웠거든.
I didn’t go into the water. Because it was too scary
그 셔츠를 벗었어요. 얼룩이 셔츠에 생겼거든요.
I took that shirt off. Because the shirt got stained (a stain came up)
내가 멕시코 경찰에 잡혔어. 국경선을 넘었거든.
I was caught by the Mexican police. Because I crossed the border
이 화창한 날씨를 보니 기분이 아주 좋아요. 2주일 동안 비가 끊임없이 왔거든요.
I’m so happy to see this clear weather. Because it rained constantly for two weeks.
이제 직원들이 부장님을 아주 잘 하고 있어요. 부장님이 아직 자기 후계자를 지명하지 않았거든요.
Everybody is being nice to the boss now. Because he hasn’t chosen/designated his successor yet.

You can also give information about the future by attaching ~거든(요) to ~ㄹ/을 것이다. For example:

내일 못 가겠어. 친구를 만나러 서울에 갈 거거든
Tomorrow I won’t be able to go. Because I’m meeting a friend in Seoul
건강보험을 들어야 돼요. 거기서 의료를 못 받을 것이거든요
We need to get insurance. Because we won’t be able to get medical treatment there.
담배를 피워 보지 마세요. 한번만 해 보면 바로 중독될 것이거든요
Don’t try cigarettes (smoking). If you try it just once, you’ll get addicted.
Remember that 것 can be shortened to 거, in which case the “이” in 이다 can be omitted. In the example above, “~거든요” looks like it attaches directly to “거.” In effect it does, but it is a contraction of “갈 것이거든요.”

——————–

So far every example has ended with ~거든(요). Because ~거든(요) implies that the listener does not know the information before it, a sentence ending in ~거든(요) often sets up the next sentence. In other words, the sentence after ~거든(요) is usually something that now makes sense because the listener has heard the new information. Look at an example:

Imagine I just said this sentence:

제가 프랑스어도 할 수 있어요
I can speak French as well

Said on its own, the listener would not know how you are able to speak French. By leading with a sentence ending in ~거든(요), you explain how or why you can. For example:

제가 캐나다 사람이거든요. 그래서 프랑스어도 할 수 있어요
I’m Canadian. So, I can speak French as well.

In a way, the first sentence (the one ending in ~거든요) gives the listener new information that helps them make sense of what comes next. More examples:

일을 아직 안 했거든요. 그래서 오늘 회사에 가서 해야 될 것 같아요
I haven’t done that work yet. So, I’ll probably have to go to the office and do it.

어제 일을 다 했거든요. 그래서 오늘 할 일이 없어요
I finished all of my work yesterday. Therefore, I have nothing to do today.
오늘 친구를 만나러 서울 갈 거거든요. 혹시 같이 갈래요?
I’m going to Seoul to meet a friend tonight. Would you like to go together?
우리가 언제 할지 달력에 표시했거든요. 깜빡하지 말고 그 날에 꼭 와야 돼요
I marked it on the calendar. Don’t forget, and make sure you come that day.

These sentences can look odd in English simply because some grammatical principles have no perfect translation. “Providing information for the upcoming sentence while implying a slight sense of ‘because’” is the closest description.

————–

~거든 can also sit between two clauses to mean “if” or “when,” like ~(으)면 or ~ㄴ/는다면 from Lesson 43. The difference is that ~거든 is usually used when the second clause ends in an imperative (telling somebody to do something) or some other suggestive ending.

This usage is not very common, but it does come up now and then. For example:

바지가 너무 크면 교환해!
If the pants are too big, change them!
바지가 너무 크거든 교환해!
If the pants are too big, change them!
숙제를 다 하면 알려 줘!
Let me know when you are done your homework!
숙제를 다 하거든 알려줘!
Let me know when you are done your homework!

If you are studying for the intermediate TOPIK test, be ready for a question like this:

Choose the grammatical principle that can replace the underlined section in the following sentence:

길이 미끄러우면 버스를 타!

a) 미끄러운데
b) 미끄럽잖아
c) 미끄럽다가
d) 미끄럽거든

————–

Finally, people often attach ~거든(요) to 아니다 to disagree with some fact. Younger speakers do this a lot, usually to push their disagreement into the conversation. For example:

Person 1: 그 남자가 너무 잘생겼어
That man is so handsome
Person 2: 아니거든!
No! (He’s not!)
Person 1: 그 사람이 아주 겸손했어
That person is so modest
Person 2: 아니거든!
No! (He’s not!)
