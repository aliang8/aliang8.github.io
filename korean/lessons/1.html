저 = I, me (formal)

Common Usages:

저는

제가

저의

Notes: When used as the subject of a sentence, 저 translates to “I,” when used as the object of a sentence, “저” translates to “me.” For example:

저는 그 여자를 사랑해요
I love that girl
그 여자는 저를 사랑해요
That girl loves me

저 changes to 제 when 이/가 are attached. See Lesson 2 for more information. For example:

제가 한국을 좋아해요
I like Korea
저는 한국을 좋아해요
I like Korea

나 is used instead of 저 in informal situations.

이 = this

Notes: Certain types of words can be placed before nouns to describe them. For example, adjectives can be placed before nouns to describe them (for example: a pretty girl). 이 is a type of word called a “관형사,” which isn’t an adjective, but is another type of word that can be placed before nouns to describe them. In English, “관형사” translates to a “modifier” or “a determiner.” 이 is placed before nouns within reaching distance to indicate that one is talking about “this” specific thing. For example:

이 사람
this person

Other examples of determiners in Korean (and English) are:

그 사람
that person
모든 사람
all people
어떤 사람
which person

It is easy to distinguish between a determiner and an adjective in Korean. Although both placed before nouns to “describe” them, the dictionary (ordinary) form of an adjective always ends in ~다 and must change in order to describe them. For example, 예쁘다 changes to 예쁜 when placed before a noun to describe it (See Lesson 3 for more information). Determiners keep their original form when placed before a noun. This might be too much grammar for you at this point, but make a note of it.

Example:

이 차는 너무 비싸요
This car is too expensive
이 방은 넓습니다
This room is big/wide
저는 이 영화를 더 이상 보고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to watch this movie anymore
이 고기는 돼지고기입니다
This meat is pork
이 사람은 저의 누나예요
This (person) is my sister
그 = that

Notes: 그 is another example of a “관형사” in Korean. 그 is placed before nouns that are being referred to from context or a previous sentence. For example, if your father is talking about a specific person, you can refer to “that person” by saying “그 사람.”

Common Usages:

그것

그 사람

See more information about 관형사 in my description of “이” above.

Examples:

저 = that (when something is far away)

Notes: 저 is another example of a “관형사” in Korean. 저 is placed before nouns that are far away (not within reach). For example, if you want to refer to the person across the street, you can refer to “that person” by saying “저 사람.”

그 and 저 both translate to “that.” This often causes confusion for English speakers.

Also notice that “저” has more than one meaning. Earlier, you saw that “저” means “I/me.” Welcome to the world of Korean! Where often times one word has more than one meaning.

2

Sentence Word Order

Korean sentence structure is hard for English speakers to grasp at first. For Lesson 1, words in a Korean sentence come in this order:

Subject – Object – Verb (for example: I hamburger eat)
Or
Subject – Adjective (for example: I beautiful)

A quick explanation of “subject” and “object”, since later concepts depend on it.
The subject is the person or thing that acts. It does the action of the verb. The subject in each sentence below is underlined:

I went to the park
I will go to the park
My mom loves me
He loves me
The dog ran fast
The clouds cleared up

In English, the subject always comes before the verb.

The object is whatever the verb acts on. The object in each sentence below is underlined:

My mom loves me
The dog bit the mailman
He ate rice
Students studied Korean

In English, the object always comes after the verb. But a sentence with a verb does not need an object. For example:

I slept
I ate
He died

Sometimes the object is simply left out: “I ate” and “I ate rice” are both correct. Other verbs by nature cannot take an object. For example, you cannot put an object after “to sleep” or “to go:”

I sleep you
I go you

Sentences with adjectives also have subjects, but never an object. The subjects are underlined below:

School is boring
I am boring
The movie was funny
The building is big
My girlfriend is pretty
The food is delicious

Verbs and adjectives go at the end of a sentence. In fact, every Korean sentence and clause must end in one of the following:

– A verb
– An adjective, or
– 이다

The meaning of 이다 is covered later in this lesson. It is neither a verb nor an adjective, but it behaves like them. Every verb, every adjective and 이다 end in “다,” and these are the only words in Korean that can be conjugated.

Korean also has formality built into the language: you speak differently to a respected elder than to a friend. Words can change in many ways depending on formality, but the two most basic things to know are:

1) There are two ways to say “I” or “me” in Korean:

나, used in informal situations, and
저, used in formal situations.

2) There are many ways to conjugate a word. As we saw, 고맙다 can be conjugated in many ways, and you need to know which fits which situation. This is taught in Lesson 6. Until then, no distinction of formality is made and the focus is on sentence structure, so you will see both 나 and 저 used arbitrarily. Do not worry about which is used until Lesson 6.

Now we can talk about making Korean sentences.

3

Korean Particles(~는/은 and~를/을)

Most words in a Korean sentence have a particle attached. Particles mark the role of each word, specifically which word is the subject or object. They have no English equivalent and cannot be translated.
The particles for this lesson are:

는 or 은 (Subject)

Placed after a word to mark it as the subject of the sentence.

Use 는 when the last letter of the last syllable of the subject is a vowel. For example:

나
나는
저
저는

Use 은 when the last letter of the last syllable of the subject is a consonant. For example:

집
집은
책
책은

를 or 을 (Object)

Placed after a word to mark it as the object of the sentence.

Use 를 when the last letter of the last syllable is a vowel. For example:

나
나를
저
저를

Use 을 when the last letter of the last syllable is a consonant. For example:

집
집을
책
책을

Now we can build sentences with Korean word order and Korean particles.

1) I speak Korean = I는 Korean을 speak
는 is attached to “I” (the subject)
을 is attached to “Korean” (the object)

2) I like you = I는 you를 like
는 is attached to “I” (the subject)
를 is attached to “you” (the object)

3) I wrote a letter = I는 letter을 wrote
는 is attached to “I” (the subject)
을 is attached to “letter” (the object)

4) I opened the door = I는 door을 opened
는 is attached to “I” (the subject)
을 is attached to “the door” (the object)

5) My mom will make pasta = My mom은 pasta를 will make
은 is attached to “my mom” (the subject)
를 is attached to “pasta” (the object)

The same works for sentences with adjectives, which have no object:

1) My girlfriend is pretty: My girlfriend은 is pretty
“은” is attached to “my girlfriend” (the subject)

2) The movie was scary = The movie는 was scary
“는” is attached to “the movie” (the subject)

Now we move to real Korean words and sentences ending in 이다.

4

To be:이다

We start with simple sentences using 이다, which translates as “to be.” English speakers rarely notice how difficult “to be” is. Depending on the subject, it could be any of the underlined words below:

I am a man
He is a man
They are men
I was a man
They were men

Each sentence uses a different word (is, am, are, was, were) depending on subject and tense. In Korean, 이다 covers all of those “to be” words.

이다 can be conjugated, so it is similar to verbs and adjectives, but its rules are often (not always) different. Those differences are taught as they become important. For example, Lesson 52 shows that quoting a sentence with 이다 works differently than with verbs and adjectives.

Like adjectives, 이다 cannot act on an object. Only verbs can. For example:

I eat hamburgers (eat is a verb, the object is a hamburger)
She meets my friend (meet is a verb, the object is my friend)
They study Korean (study is a verb, the object is Korean)
We listen to music (listen is a verb, the object is music)

Those sentences can have objects because a verb is the predicate. Sentences predicated by adjectives have no object:

I am pretty
She is beautiful
They are hungry
We are smart

In English, adjectives need “to be” words like am, is and are. We cannot say:

I pretty
She beautiful
They hungry
We smart

Unlike English, Korean does not use 이다 in these sentences. 이다 does not mark that something “is” an adjective. Sentences predicated by adjectives are covered in Lesson 3.

So 이다 is not used in sentences like these:

I am pretty
She is beautiful
They are hungry
We are smart

But 이다 is used in sentences like these:

I am a man
He is a man
They are men
I was a man
They were men

이다 says that a noun is a noun. The basic structure of a sentence predicated by “이다” is:
[noun은/는] [another noun][이다]

[noun은/는] [another noun][이다]

For example:

I는 man이다
I am a man

Now substitute the words for “man” and “I,” which are:

나
I
남자
man

The Korean sentence looks like this:

나는 남자이다
I am a man

Notice that 이다 attaches directly to the second noun. Verbs and adjectives do not attach to nouns like this, but 이다 does. A space between the second noun and 이다 is incorrect. For example, this is wrong:

나는 남자 이다

“남자” may look like an object in that sentence, but it is not. 이다, like adjectives and unlike verbs, cannot act on an object, so the object particle on the second noun is incorrect. For example, this is wrong:

나는 남자를 이다

This lesson (and Lessons 2, 3 and 4) introduces simple Korean sentence structure. Until Lessons 5 and Lesson 6 you will not see conjugations and honorifics of verbs, adjectives and 이다. These words are very rarely used without them.

Conjugation matters, but so does structure. You cannot conjugate a sentence you cannot build, and you cannot build one you cannot conjugate. These lessons teach structure first, then how to conjugate the words inside it.

Every lesson uses example sentences to show the grammar. The examples in Lessons 1 – 4 are not conjugated, but under each one is a conjugated version in parentheses (one formal, one informal). Glance at them if you like, but conjugation is taught in Lessons 5 and 6 (verbs and adjectives) and Lesson 9 (이다).

Other examples of 이다 in use:

나는 여자이다
I am a woman

(나는 여자야 / 저는 여자예요)

나는 선생님이다
I am a teacher

(나는 선생님이야 / 저는 선생님이에요)

나는 사람이다
I am a person

(나는 사람이야 / 저는 사람이에요)

나는 ______이다
I am a _______

(나는 _______ 이야 / 저는 _____이에요)

You can put any noun into the blank to make these sentences.

“This” and “that” are often the subject of such sentences. Let’s see how 이것, 그것 and 저것 work with 이다.

5

This and That(이/그/저)

In the vocabulary above, the word for “this” is 이.
Use 이 for something within touching distance (this pen, the one you are holding). As in English, “이” (this) goes before the noun it describes. For example:

이 사람
This person
이 남자
This man
이 여자
This woman
이 차
This car
이 탁자
This table
이 의자
This chair

There are two words for “that”: 그 and 저. Early learners always mix up “그” and “저.”

Use 그 for something from a previous sentence or previous context, whether or not you can see it. You do not know enough Korean yet for real examples, but in “I don’t like that man [whom your friend mentioned a moment ago]”, that “that” is how “그” works.

Use 저 for something you can see but cannot touch because it is too far away.

Put “그” or “저” before a noun to say “this” or “that” thing, just as with “이.”

이 사람
This person
그 사람
That person
저 사람
That person
이 남자
This man
그 남자
That man
저 남자
That man
이 여자
This woman
그 여자
That woman
저 여자
That woman
이 의자
This chair
그 의자
That chair
저 의자
That chair
이 탁자
This table
그 탁자
That table
저 탁자
That table

Although “그” and “저” translate the same, they are not the same word in Korean.

One of the most common Korean words is “것”, meaning “thing.” When 이, 그 or 저 go before “것,” the result is a compound word, so there is no space between them. These are single words, not two:

이것
this thing
그것
that thing
저것
that thing

The same happens with other common words in later lessons, such as 곳 (“place”) and 때 (“time”). No need to worry about that now.

With these words, “thing” is not needed in the English translation. Here is why.

“That” is used as the example, but the same applies to “this.”

“That” can go before a noun to describe it, as we saw:

That person
That man
That woman

But it can also be a noun itself. For example:

I like that

Here “that” refers to some thing you like. It is a noun. It is a thing.

So the sentence could just as well be:

I like that thing

A little jargon helps here. In both English and Korean, “that” can be a determiner (“I like that man”) or a pronoun (“I like that”). As a determiner in Korean, put 그 before a noun. As a pronoun, use 그것.

So “이, 그 and 저” mean “this, that and that” and go before nouns, while “이것, 그것 and 저것” are nouns (pronouns). They do not need the redundant word “thing,” though the meaning is the same either way:

I like this
I like this thing

I like that
I like that thing

These nouns can be subjects or objects. Next we use them with “이다.”

6

Using This/That with이다

Remember, 이다 translates to “to be” and is conjugated as “am/is/are” in English. Now that you know how to use 이, 그 and 저 (and 이것, 그것 and 저것), you can make sentences like this:

That person is a doctor

Start by putting the words into the Korean structure:

That person는 doctor is

Then change the English words to Korean:

그 사람은 + 의사 + 이다
그 사람은 의사이다
(그 사람은 의사야 / 그 사람은 의사예요)

More examples:

그 사람은 선생님이다
That person is a teacher

(그 사람은 선생님이야 / 그 사람은 선생님이에요)

이것은 탁자이다
This (thing) is a table

(이것은 탁자야 / 이것은 탁자예요)

저것은 침대이다
That (thing) is a bed

(저것은 침대야 / 저것은 침대예요)

그 사람은 남자이다
That person is a man

(그 사람은 남자야 / 그 사람은 남자예요)

그 사람은 여자이다
That person is a woman

(그 사람은 여자야 / 그 사람은 여자예요)

그것은 차이다
That (thing) is a car

(그것은 차야 / 그것은 차예요)

이것은 나무이다
This (thing) is a tree

(이것은 나무야 / 이것은 나무예요)
