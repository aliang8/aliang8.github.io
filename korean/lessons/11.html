Introduction

This lesson builds on Lesson 10 with a range of time words. You will be able to say “I did X for 2 months” or “I didn’t do Y last week,” and you will learn whether each time word takes pure Korean or Sino-Korean numbers.

The choice between pure Korean and Sino-Korean numbers looks random, but there is a reason. When the time word is of Chinese origin, Sino-Korean numbers are used. “개월” (month) is counted with Sino-Korean numbers because it comes from Chinese and has Chinese (한자) characters. “달” (also month) is a Korean word, so it takes Korean numbers. Many Korean words have Chinese origin and 한자 characters; more on that later.

This lesson is less tidy than earlier ones, because each concept depends on the others. To understand 동안 you need 주, and to understand 주 you need 동안. It is kept as simple as possible, and once you have it, later ‘time’ grammar will come easily.

1

“For” a certain amount of time(동안)

동안 is a very useful word that says how long an action lasts. It usually follows a duration of time, for example:

2분
2 minutes
2분 동안
for 2 minutes
10분
10 minutes
10분 동안
for 10 minutes
이틀
two days
이틀 동안
for two days

동안 can also mean that an action happens “while” another happens. That use comes in Lesson 33, after some key grammar in the lessons ahead.

These go into sentences to say how long one does something. Notice that no extra particle is added to 동안. You might expect 에, but it is not used.

저는 10분 동안 걸었어요
I walked for 10 minutes
저는 30분 동안 공부했어요
I studied for 30 minutes

“동안” usually translates as “for” here, but what you are really saying is “for that period of time.” Keeping that in mind will help with later uses of “동안”. For example:

저는 10분 동안 걸었어요
I walked for (a period of) 10 minutes
저는 30분 동안 공부했어요
I studied for (a period of) 30 minutes
저는 이틀 동안 안 잤어요
I didn’t sleep for two days

Remember:

You can write a number as a word or a numeral. Usually the word is written when a pure Korean number goes with a counter like 개, 명, 번, 시간, 대, 살, etc. Where Sino-Korean numbers are written (as in the sentences above), numeral and word are interchangeable.

Spacing also differs between word and numeral. With the word, put a space between the number and the counter. For example:

저는 십 분 동안 걸었어요

With a numeral, a space is optional. Both are acceptable:

저는 10분 동안 걸었어요
저는 10 분 동안 걸었어요

There are more examples of 동안 to come, and you will want more than “분” (minutes), so on to hours.

2

Korean Hours(시간)

Minutes are easy because you always use 분. For example:

3시 20분 means “3:20”, and
20분 동안 means “for 20 minutes”

For hours, 시 is the hour on a clock, while 시간 counts hours. Compare these two sets:

3시
3:00
세 시
Three o’clock
3시간 동안
for 3 hours
세 시간 동안
for three hours
Remember that pure Korean numbers are used when talking about hours. In the examples below, the Korean word is written out when referring to an amount of hours. For example:
저는 세 시간 동안 잤어요
I slept for 3 hours
저는 한 시간 동안 달렸어요
I ran for one hour
저는 여덟 시간 동안 운전했어요
I drove for eight hours
저는 어제 한 시간 동안 야구를 했어요
I played baseball for an hour yesterday
저는 어제 두 시간 동안 TV를 봤어요*
I watched TV for two hours yesterday

*Notice that 보다 (to see) is used for watching something. 보다 also covers watching a performance, a concert and the like. In English “I saw a TV” and “I watched TV” mean different things, but Koreans simply say “I see TV.”

You are probably thinking “Okay, I can say that I did things for X hours or minutes, but what about:

I will eat in three hours, or
I ate three hours ago

I will go in 10 minutes, or
I came 10 minutes ago”

Those come with 전 and 후 in Lesson 24. This lesson already has plenty, so focus on what is here.

3

Korean Seconds(초)

Seconds take Sino-Korean numbers. Before “초,” word and numeral are interchangeable. For example:

1초
1 second
일 초
one second
2초
2 seconds
이 초
2 seconds
2초 동안
for 2 seconds
이 초 동안
for two seconds

Examples in sentences:

저는 2초 동안 달렸어요
I ran for 2 seconds
저는 그 사람을 10초 동안 만났어요
I met that person for 10 seconds
1분은 60초입니다
One minute is sixty seconds
4

Last/Next(지난/다음)

지난 and 다음 work in many situations, including time. In Lesson 10 you learned 마지막, the “last” or “final” thing in a sequence. Koreans use a different word for a “previous” (last) thing, as in “I saw a movie last (the previous) week.”

For a “previous” thing, use “지난”. The picture below shows the difference between 마지막 and 지난. Imagine a six week trip, and you are in week four. “지난” refers to the previous week, and “마지막” to the final week. (English calls both “last week”.) For example:

지난 can go immediately before some time words (more on “some” below) to mean a “previous” thing, usually translated “last.” For example:

저는 지난 주에 영화를 봤어요
I saw a movie last week
저는 지난 주에 캐나다에 갔어요
I went to Canada last week.
저는 지난 수업을 안 들었어요
I didn’t go to/attend the last/previous class

To “attend a class,” Koreans say “수업을 듣다,” literally “listen to/hear a class.” So ~을 attaches to “수업” because the class is the noun being listened to. Learners sometimes wonder why “~에” is not attached to “수업”, since English says “I didn’t go to the last class”.

This sentence is also correct, but refers to a different class than above:

저는 마지막 수업을 안 들었어요
I didn’t attend the last class

지난 only goes before some time words. You cannot say “지난 분/지난 초.” These would mean “last minute/second” as in the one that just passed, which makes no sense. Nor do they mean “I handed in my paper at the last minute,” which is the last minute in a sequence of minutes; that needs “마지막”.

시간, though, means “time” as well as counting “hours.” So “지난 시간” can mean “last time” (but not “last hour”).

저는 그것을 지난 시간에 배웠어요
I learned that (thing) last time

You can also use 번 for the last “time” something happened.

저는 그것을 지난 번에 배웠어요
I learned that (thing) last time
우리가 지난 번에 계획이 없었어요
We didn’t have plans last time
우리는 지난 번에 돼지고기를 먹었어요
We ate pork last time

다음 works like 지난 but means “next.” For example:

저는 다음 주에 영화를 볼 거예요
I will see a movie next week
저는 다음 주에 캐나다에 갈 거예요
I will go to Canada next week

As above, 다음 with 시간 means “next time,” not “next hour.”

우리는 다음 번에 학교에 갈 거예요
We will go to school next time
우리는 다음 시간에 학교에 갈 거예요
We will go to school next time
나는 너를 다음 번에 방문할 거야
I will visit you next time
나는 너를 다음 시간에 방문할 거야
I will visit you next time
5

Korean Days(일/날/하루)

Days are confusing in Korean because several words are involved. You met the days of the week in an earlier vocabulary section; each ends in 일. 일 means “day” but is never used alone (alone, it means ‘work’). A Korean calendar shows many words ending in “일.” Do not learn these now, just notice “일” in them:

총선
general election
총선일
the day of the general election (election day)
현충일
Memorial day

일 is also the counter for days. Lesson 10 said counting uses pure Korean numbers, but days are counted with Sino-Korean numbers. Before “일,” word and numeral are interchangeable.

나는 3일 동안 공부했어
I studied for 3 days
나는 삼 일 동안 공부했어
I studied for 3 days
나는 3일 동안 학교에 안 갔어
I didn’t go to school for 3 days
나는 삼 일 동안 학교에 안 갔어
I didn’t go to school for 3 days
저는 5일 동안 계획이 없을 거예요
I won’t have plans for 5 days
우리는 6일 동안 만나지 않았어요
We didn’t meet for 6 days

To add confusion, for 1 – 10 days there is a separate word for “one day,” another for “2 days,” another for “3 days,” and so on. The most common is 하루, “one day,” which is used much more than 일 일. But 2일 (이 일)/3일 (삼 일)/4일 (사 일)/5일 (오 일)/etc are used more than their corresponding words.

저는 하루 동안 여행했어요
I traveled for 1 day
저는 사흘 동안 밥을 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat rice for 3 days
저는 3일 동안 밥을 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat rice for 3 days
우리는 이틀 동안 미국에 있었어요
We were in America for two days
우리는 10일 동안 한국에 있을 거예요
We will be in Korea for ten days

A (Sino-Korean) number before 일 can also mean a specific day of the month. The day of a month (3일) and a period of days (3일) look identical, but in sentences they are easy to tell apart:

나는 3일에 수업을 들었어
I went to class on the 3rd (day of the month)
나는 3일 동안  수업을 들었어
I went to class for three days

Give a specific day and month by adding #일 after #월:

3월 2일
March 2nd
5월 25일
May 25th
저는 8월 15일에 도착할 거예요
I will arrive on August 15th
저는 이 여권을 1월 2일에 받았어요
I received my passport on January 2nd

날 is another word for “day.” It stands alone but is not counted, and refers to a specific day by itself. For example:

저는 그 날에 갔어요
I went on that day
저는 그 날에 계획이 있어요
I have plans on that day
저는 그 날에 선생님이 되었어요
I became a teacher on that day
우리는 그 날에 시작할 거예요
We will start on that day

To talk about the first/second/third day, use 번째 (from the previous lesson) with 날. For example:

우리는 두 번째 날에 서울에 갔어요
We went to Seoul on the second day
우리는 둘째 날에 서울에 갔어요
We went to Seoul on the second day
우리는 세 번째 날에 안 만났어요
We didn’t meet on the third day
우리는 셋째 날에 안 만났어요
We didn’t meet on the third day
저는 네 번째 날에 계획이 없어요
I have no plans on the fourth day
저는 넷째 날에 계획이 없어요
I have no plans on the fourth day

첫날 is a word specifically for the first day:

저는 첫날에 명동에 갔어요
I went to 명동 on the first day
저는 첫날에 그 여자를 만났어요
I met that girl on the first day
6

Korean Weeks(주)

Weeks work in sentences just like 일 (day), with no odd extra words. Weeks take Sino-Korean numbers:

저는 다음 주에 미국에 갈 거예요
I will go to the US next week
지난 주에 저는 계획이 많았어요
I had a lot of plans last week

“주” is also the counter for weeks. As with days, Sino-Korean numbers are used, and before “주” word and numeral are interchangeable. For example:

저는 2주 동안 한식을 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat Korean food for 2 weeks
저는 4주 동안 여자친구를 안 만났어요
I didn’t meet my girlfriend for 4 weeks
저는 이 주 동안 한식을 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat Korean food for two weeks
저는 사 주 동안 여자친구를 안 만났어요
I didn’t meet my girlfriend for four weeks

“주일” can also count weeks. It is acceptable, but “주” as above sounds a bit more natural. For example:

저는 2주일 동안 한식을 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat Korean food for two weeks
저는 4주일 동안 여자친구를 안 만났어요
I didn’t meet my girlfriend for four weeks
7

Korean Months(달/개월)

First, the name of each month is its number in the calendar. For example:

January = 1월
February = 2월
December = 12월

Months are counted with either 달 or 개월. 달 takes pure Korean numbers; 개월 takes Sino-Korean numbers. Both are common and mean the same. Before 달 the Korean word is the usual choice; before 개월, word and numeral are interchangeable.

저는 두 달 동안 한국어 수업을 들었어요
I took a Korean class for 2 months
저는 2개월 동안 한국어 수업을 들었어요
I took a Korean class for 2 months
저는 이 개월 동안 한국어 수업을 들었어요
I took a Korean class for 2 months

Some grammar teachers say ‘동안’ is already built into the meaning of 달, so 동안 would not be needed after it. Still, most people use 동안 after 달.

8

Korean Years(년)

Years work like weeks, so Sino-Korean numbers are used. Before “년,” word and numeral are interchangeable:

저는 3년 동안 형을 안 만났어요
I didn’t meet my brother for 3 years
저는 10년 동안 한국어를 공부했어요
I studied Korean for 10 years
저는 삼 년 동안 형을 안 만났어요
I didn’t meet my brother for three years
저는 십 년 동안 한국어를 공부했어요
I studied Korean for ten years

But “last/next year” cannot be “지난/다음 년.” Separate words are used:

작년
last year
내년
next year, for example:
나는 내년에 한국에 갈 거야
I will go to Korea next year
그 사람은 작년에 죽었어요
That person died last year
9

This Week/This Month/This Time(이번)

Finally, 이번 works like 지난/다음 with weeks and months to mean “this week/this month.” You cannot say “이번 년”; the separate word “올해” is used.

저는 이번 주에 계획이 없어요
I have no plans this week
저의 어머니는 올해 한국에 올 것입니다
My mom will come to Korea this year
Note that “에” is commonly omitted from “올해.” As the section above shows, this is not the case with 작년 and 내년.

이번 does not work with minutes/seconds/hours; “I want to go to school this hour” makes no sense. But since 시간 also means “time,” 이번 시간 means “this time,” usually “this time in class.” For example:

우리는 이번 시간에 많이 배웠어
We learned a lot this time

In most other cases, “이번에” is more natural than “이번 시간:”

나는 너를 이번에 안 만날 거야
I won’t meet you this time

번 can also follow 지난/다음 to mean “last/next time”

저는 박물관에 다음 번에 갈 거예요
I will go to the museum next time
저는 박물관에 지난 번에 갔어요
I went to the museum last time
10

“Per”

With each of these units of time, adding ~에 says how much was done within that unit, usually translated “per.” For example:

저는 이 약을 하루에 두 번 먹어요
I eat (take) this medicine twice per day
저는 그 친구를 1주일에 한 번 만나요
I meet that friend once per week
저는 여기에 한 달에 세 번 와요
I come here three times per month
저는 1년에 두 번 미국에 가요
I go to America twice per year

Almost every example here used 동안. There are other ways to use these time words without 동안 (for example, “I will go to Canada 3 days from now”), but this lesson is long enough already, so it ends here.
