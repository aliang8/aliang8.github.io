머무르다 = to stay

머무르다 follows the 르 irregular

Common Usages:

장소에 머무르다
to stay at a place

Notes: 머무르다 can be used as 머물다, which is actually a shortened version of 머무르다.

머무르다 can be used any way and with any grammatical principle, but must be used in accordance with the 르 irregular. For example:

한국에서 온 교환학생이 우리 집에서 1년 동안 머물렀어요

However, 머물다 cannot be used with all grammatical principles.
Any grammatical principle that starts with a consonant (and there is no option other than that one consonant), can be added to 머물다. For example:

머물다 + ~자
머물자 = okay
머물다 + ~고
머물고 = okay
머물다 + 겠다
머물겠다 = okay
머물다 + ~지 않다
머물지 않다 = okay

If a grammatical principle that is added to 머물다 is a vowel – and there is no other option other than a vowel – then that grammatical principle cannot be added to 머물다. The two most common grammatical principles where this occurs is when conjugating in the past or present tenses. For example:

머물다 + ~아/어(요)
머물어요 – this is incorrect
머물다 + ~았/었어요
머물었어요 – this is incorrect

See Lesson 97 for more information.

Example: 우리는 10일 동안 부산에서 머물렀어요
We stayed in Busan for 10 days

Introduction

This lesson introduces your first (of very many) connecting particles in Korean. These particles usually separate two ideas, phrases or sentences. You will learn about 고 and how it is used, including ~고 싶다. You will also see that some words (usually position-like words) follow different rules from most verbs here.

1

Connecting Particle~고

As you study Korean, you will spend a lot of time on connecting particles and their meanings. There are many of them, and their job is to connect two clauses, sentences or ideas. They usually attach to the end (the adjective or verb) of one clause to link it to the next. The simplest is ~고, which attaches to the stem of a verb or adjective.

The most basic use of ~고 is to say that one action happens, and then something else happens. The most common translation of “~고” here is “then.” For example:

저는 밥을 먹고 갈 거예요
I will eat then go
저는 자고 한국어를 공부했어요
I slept then studied Korean
저는 책을 읽고 바로 잤어요
I read a book then slept immediately
저는 10분 동안 쉬고 갈 거예요
I will rest for 10 minutes then go
애기가 1분 동안 울고 배를 먹었어요
The baby cried for 1 minute and then ate a pear
저는 당근을 칼로 자르고 냉장고에 넣었어요
I cut the carrots with the knife then put (them) in the fridge
우리는 서울에서 2일 동안 머무르고 부산에 갈 거예요
We will stay in Seoul for two days, then go to Busan

To stress that you do something after finishing something else, add 나서 after ~고. For example:

저는 숙제를 끝내고 나서 집으로 갈 거예요
I will finish my homework and then go home
저는 친구를 만나고 나서 은행에 갈 거예요
I will meet a friend and then go to the bank

In the sentences so far, ~고 means “then,” but it does not always. It can also simply connect two clauses with a similar idea. For example:

저는 과일도 좋아하고 야채도 좋아해요
I like fruit, and I like vegetables too

*Notice that 도 is added to both 과일 and 야채 here. English would say “I like apples and vegetables too,” with only one “too,” but in Korean 도 can be used twice. The sentence could also be said like this:

저는 과일과 야채도 좋아해요
I like fruit and vegetables too

Essentially, ~고 joins two sentences or clauses. Koreans like to keep sentences as short as possible, and ~고 is one way to do that. Every sentence above could be split into two sentences. For example:

저는 밥을 먹을 거예요. 그리고 저는 갈 거예요
저는 밥을 먹고 갈 거예요
저는 잤어요. 그리고 저는 한국어를 공부했어요
저는 자고 한국어를 공부했어요
저는 과일을 좋아해요. 저는 야채를 좋아해요
저는 과일도 좋아하고 야채도 좋아해요

You can also use ~고 to string adjectives together to describe something. Again, each sentence could be split in two, but ~고 connects two clauses with a similar idea. For example:

그 사람은 착해요. 그 사람은 똑똑해요
That person is nice. That person is smart.

= 그 사람은 착하고 똑똑해요 = That person is kind and smart

저의 여자 친구는 귀여워요. 저의 여자 친구는 예뻐요
My girlfriend is cute. My girlfriend is pretty.

= 저의 여자 친구는 귀엽고 예뻐요 = My girlfriend is cute and pretty

오늘 고객이 많아요. 오늘 분위기가 좋아요
Today there are a lot of customers. Today the atmosphere is good

= 오늘 고객이 많고 분위기가 좋아요 = Today there are a lot of customers, and the atmosphere is good

In many Korean grammar patterns that connect sentences (you will learn a lot of them soon), the verb or adjective in the first clause is not conjugated; the pattern attaches directly to its stem. The tense is usually shown by the final verb or adjective, and the tense of the first one is implied by context.

With ~고, the first verb or adjective can be conjugated. This happens in two cases:

1) When one action happens after another (as at the start of this lesson), but a considerable amount of time has passed between them. Often the first clause made the second one possible. For example:

저는 열심히 공부했고 의사가 되었어요
I studied hard and (then) became a doctor
저는 영어 문법을 열심히 공부했고 시험을 잘 봤어요
I studied English grammar hard and then did well on the test

Notice that Koreans say “시험을 보다” to mean “to take/do a test”

2) When connecting two clauses with a similar idea that both happened in the past. Here there is no real sense of one action following another; the speaker is just saying that both things happened. For example:

저는 방학 동안 영어 문법을 많이 공부했고 영어 신문도 많이 읽었어요
During vacation I studied a lot of English grammar, and I also read a lot of English newspapers

The sentences from the start of the lesson (where one clause happens [right] after the other) would sound awkward with the first clause conjugated. For example:

저는 잤고 한국어를 공부했어요
저는 책을 읽었고 바로 잤어요

You started learning about ~는/은 and ~이/가 in Lesson 2. Their differences are discussed further later in this lesson. First, here is a use of ~는/은 that often appears in sentences with ~고.

Lesson 2 mentioned that ~는/은 has a comparative function. It is often used to say “this thing is one way, and this other thing is another way.” For example:

이 산은 높아요. 저 산은 낮아요.
This mountain is high. That mountain is low.
문법은 어려워요. 단어는 쉬워요.
Grammar is difficult. Words are easy.
이 사람은 부자예요. 저 사람은 가난해요
This person is rich. That person is poor.
이분은 저의 아버지입니다. 이분은 저의 어머니입니다.
This person is my father. This person is my mother

Here it is common to join the two sentences with ~고, since they express a similar idea. ~는/은 attaches to both things being compared. For example:

이 산은 높고 저 산은 낮아요
This mountain is high, but that mountain is low
문법은 어렵고 단어는 쉬워요
Grammar is hard, but words are easy
이 사람은 부자이고 저 사람은 가난해요
That person is rich, but that person is poor
이분은 저의 아버지이고 이분은 저의 어머니입니다
This person is my father, and this person is my mother

~는/은 and ~이/가 are discussed further later in this lesson.

2

Using~아/어서

Another way to say that one action happens after another is to attach ~아/어서 to the verb in the first clause. The verb before ~아/어서 is never conjugated to the past tense. For example:

저는 당근을 칼로 잘라서 냉장고에 넣었어요
I cut carrots with a knife and then put them into the fridge

You might wonder what the difference is between ~고 and ~아/서. For example:

저는 당근을 칼로 잘라서 냉장고에 넣었어요
I cut the carrots with the knife then put them in the fridge
저는 당근을 칼로 자르고 냉장고에 넣었어요
I cut the carrots with the knife then put them in the fridge

Both sentences say effectively the same thing.

However, ~아/어서 tends to be used when the action of the first clause is intrinsically linked to the action of the second.

The best way to see this is to look at why ~아/어서, not ~고, is normally used with 가다 and 오다.

To say that you go somewhere and then do something, you should not say this:

저는 학교에 가고 공부할 거예요

Instead of ~고, you must add ~아/어서 here:

저는 학교에 가서 공부할 거예요
I will go to school and then study

Likewise, to say that you come somewhere and do something, you should not say this:

우리는 집에 오고 바로 잤어요

Instead of ~고, you must add ~아/어서 here:

우리는 집에 와서 바로 잤어요
We came home and went to sleep immediately

The reason is the nature of 오다 and 가다. When ~고 connects clauses with other verbs (for example 먹다), the two actions have no real connection beyond one happening before the other. Take an earlier example:

저는 밥을 먹고 갈 거예요
I will eat and then go

This implies that you eat, finish eating, and then go somewhere. Apart from the order, eating did not affect going.

With “가다” and “오다,” though, the coming or going is directly connected to the next action: to do the second action, you needed to have gone somewhere or come from somewhere.

The picture below shows this idea.

With 가서 … 먹다, the “going” leads up to the “eating.” With 먹고 … 가다, even though “eating” happened before “going,” the two are not related.

In the same way, ~아/어서 can attach to verbs other than 가다 and 오다 to connect two clauses. Some examples:

우리는 맛있는 고기를 골라서 같이 먹었어요
We chose delicious meat then ate together
문을 열어서 밖으로 나갔어요
I opened the door and then went outside

At this point it is important to know how to use “~고,” and “~아/어서” with “가다/오다.” The full nuance of “~아/어서” on other words is beyond your current level. Its use is more complex and is covered in Lesson 70. The connector ~아/어서 also has another meaning, covered in Lesson 37.

You can attach ~고 to “가다” and “오다,” but only when connecting sentences with a similar idea, not when someone goes or comes to a place and does something there.

For example, a teacher telling a current student about all the former students who came to visit him the day before (it was Teacher’s Day) said:

혜원도 오고… 슬기도 오고… 승하도 오고… 지혜도 오고…
혜원 came, 슬기 came, 승하 came, 지혜 came, …

Position verbs
Many position verbs also behave differently from regular verbs (here and in many other situations). The most common are to sit (앉다), to stand (서다), and to lie down (눕다).

They are treated differently because they are verbs of position. For example, if you said:

저는 먹고 공부했어요
I ate and studied

In that sentence, apart from one action following the other, 먹다 and 공부하다 have no relation. But if you said:

저는 눕고 책을 읽었어요 would mean “I lay down, and then read a book,” as in: I lay down, got up again, and then read a book. Technically possible, but nobody does that. What you meant is that you lay down and then, while lying down, read a book. With position verbs, the first action relates to the second (just as 가다 and 오다 relate to the verb that follows). That is why they are treated differently.

To use position verbs this way, add ~아/어서 to them:

저는 누워서 책을 읽었어요
I lied down and read a book
나는 앉아서 쉴 거야
I’m going to sit down and relax
저는 줄에 서서 순서를 기다렸어요
I stood in line and waited for my turn
3

I want to:~고 싶다

As mentioned, ~고 can connect two clauses or sentences. But ~고 also has many other uses when combined with other words. What comes next is extremely common, in Korean and in English; it simply had to wait for some other groundwork first.

Adding ~고 싶다 to the stem of a verb means “I want to ____.” Very easy to use:

저는 한국어를 배우고 싶어요
I want to study Korean
저는 캐나다에 가고 싶어요
I want to go to Canada
저는 여자친구를 위해 편지를 쓰고 싶어요
I want to write a letter for my girlfriend
저는 새로운 차를 사고 싶어요
I want to buy a new car
저는 내일 쉬고 싶어요
I want to rest tomorrow
저는 저의 친구를 파티에 초대하고 싶어요
I want to invite my friend to the party
저는 방학 동안 책 두 권을 읽고 싶어요
I want to read two books during vacation

It works with 되다 to say that you want to be or become something:

저는 선생님이 되고 싶어요
I want to be/become a teacher

It can be used in the past tense too:

저는 선생님이 되고 싶었어요
I wanted to become a teacher
그 여자는 나랑* 결혼하고 싶었어
that girl wanted to marry me

Notice that when talking about “marrying” somebody, Korean uses “with” instead of attaching ‘를/을.’ Because of this difference, many Koreans mistakenly say “I want to marry with you.”

You have actually met ~고 싶다 before without realizing it. Korean has two ways to say “I miss ____.” One is “그립다,” used for missing something other than a person (I miss my hometown). The word for missing a person is 보고 싶다. Notice anything? 보고 – 싶다? Literally, it means “I want to see:”

저는 친구를 보고 싶어요
I miss my friend/I want to see my friend

~고 싶다 is not used with adjectives. English allows “I want to be happy,” but in Korean you need an extra grammar pattern that changes the meaning to “I want to become happy.” You will learn to do this with ~아/어지다 in the next lesson.

Note that 싶다 acts as an adjective and must be conjugated as one. So:

나는 먹고 싶다 (I want to eat) is correct, but
나는 먹고 싶는다 is incorrect

Also notice the difference between 원하다 and ~고 싶다. Use 원하다 when you want an object:

저는 그 책을 원해요
I want that book

and use ~고 싶다 when you want to do a verb:

저는 그 책을 읽고 싶어요
I want to read that book

You can also use “안” or attach “~지 않다” to 싶다 to say that one does not want to do something. For example:

저는 술을 안 마시고 싶어요
I don’t want to drink alcohol
저는 울고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to cry
나는 너를 파티에 초대하고 싶지 않아
I don’t want to invite you to the party
저는 부자가 되고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to become a rich man
방학 동안 저는 학교에 있고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to be at school during vacation

Some grammar patterns you learn are rarely used, but the ones in this lesson are used all the time. Up to now it has been hard to build good example sentences without ~고 and ~고 싶다.

4

~는/은 and~이/가 Revisited

In Lesson 2 you started learning the differences between ~이/가 and ~는/은. Lesson 2 is worth a quick re-read before continuing.

Now that you know more Korean grammar, we can go further into the nuances of ~이/가 and ~는/은. If you have not already, re-read Lesson 2 to refresh what was covered there.

One problem with both particles is that each has many functions. Another function of ~는/은 (besides the comparison function from Lesson 2 and the examples earlier in this lesson) is to state a general fact. For example:

Rocky (로키) 산은 높아요
The Rocky Mountains are high (the Rocky Mountain is high)

This is said as a general fact. Everybody knows the Rocky Mountains are high, so it can be stated generally like this. Contrast ~이/가, which is not used for a general fact about something. Instead it describes a specific situation that the speaker (usually) just realized or observed. For example, if you were hiking with a friend and suddenly caught sight of a nearby mountain, you could say:

와! 산이 높다!
Wow! The mountain is high!
Even though the speaker is talking about this one specific mountain, he or she is not comparing it with something else (for example, another mountain). That would require ~는/은 (in its other usage).

Here are simplified versions of the sentences above:

산은 높다
산이 높다

Remember that the difference between ~이/가 and ~는/은 is about nuance, not a different translation. If we tried to capture the nuance of the two examples above in English, it might be:
산은 높다
In general, mountains are high
산이 높다
This mountain that I’m looking at, which I’ve never seen or noticed before, is high

At the same time (and this is where people really get confused), ~는/은 could be used in this situation with a comparing function. For example:

이 산은 높아요. 하지만, 저 산은 낮아요
This mountain is tall. But that mountain is low (small)

This is exactly what confuses learners about ~이/가 and ~는/은. Both can express nuances that seem to overlap. For example, if you say:
“산은 높다”

What does it mean? Is it…:

“In general, mountains are high”, or
“This mountain, in comparison to that other mountain (or maybe some other thing) is high”

The only way to tell which nuance is meant is to understand the situation in which it is said.

Another example. If you said:

여름 날씨는 좋다
(in general) summer weather is good

This expresses a general fact about summer weather: that summer weather is (in general) good. But if you just walked outside and saw that the weather is good, you would more likely say:

날씨가 좋다
The weather (right now that I am experiencing) is good

Here the speaker is only saying the weather is good right now, not that it is always good.
This does not mean “날씨는 좋다” is wrong. You can certainly use it, but only in a fitting situation. Here is a dialogue with a Korean speaker:

  • Me: When would you be able to say “날씨는 좋다”. It clearly isn’t used as a general statement, because weather (in general) isn’t always good. So when could I say this?
  • Korean person: It sounds like you are comparing it with something. Like, you are saying that the weather is good, but something else might be bad.
  • Me: For example?
  • Korean person: Maybe, “여기 음식이 맛이 없어요. 하지만 날씨는 좋아요.” (The food here isn’t delicious, but the weather is good). Here, you’re saying that the weather is good, but the food is bad.

Let’s look at another example:

다이아몬드는 딱딱하다
(in general) Diamonds are hard

(This statement applies generally. Most people know diamonds are hard; it is not new information)

But if somebody picked up a diamond, felt it, and realized exactly how hard that particular diamond is, they would more likely say:

다이아몬드가 너무 딱딱하다
Diamonds are hard (due to the experience that I have right now with this diamond, I can see/realize that this diamond is very hard!)

(Please ignore the attempt to translate this nuance. It would never actually be translated that way.)

Now, if we changed the noun:

밥은 딱딱하다
The rice is hard

This would not be said as a general statement. Why? Because rice is generally not hard. But if you were about to eat and touched the rice on your plate, you could say:

밥이 딱딱하다
The rice is hard (due to what I am experiencing right now, I can say that this particular rice is hard)

(Again, please ignore this awkward translation)

Now this:

밥은 딱딱하다
The rice is hard

Again, this is not a general statement. So is it wrong?

No, because ~는/은 has other purposes. Maybe the speaker meant the comparison function of ~는/은: the soup is hot (and ready to eat), but the rice is hard. Remember, all of this depends on the situation.

Another example: (빨갛다
red, 노랗다= yellow. These words aren’t introduced until Lesson 23. Sorry.)
사과는 빨갛다
(in general) apples are red, or
사과는 빨갛다. 바나나는 노랗다
Apples are red. Bananas are yellow
사과가 빨갛다
the apple is red (the apple that I am looking at that I just noticed)
사과가 파랗다
the apple is blue (this would not normally be the case and therefore would never be used with ~는/은 to express a general statement meaning. However, ~이/가 is used here to specifically describe the apple that the speaker is experiencing – which may or may not follow what is typical of other apples)
의사들은 똑똑하다
(in general), doctors are smart
그 의사가 아주 똑똑하다
the doctor is smart (the one specific doctor who is in the room, for example)

This is why, to say “it is raining,” you must use this sentence:

비가 와요
It’s raining

Instead of:
비는 와요… (incorrect)

“비는 와요” would suggest that, in general, it rains, which makes no sense. The fact that it is raining, rained, or will rain always falls into the specific-situation use of “이/가.”

As said at the end of Lesson 2: your sense of when to use ~는/은 over ~이/가, and vice versa, will grow with your general understanding of Korean grammar.

Also remember that real sentences (in any language, including Korean) are usually more complicated than those shown here. And sentences are rarely said in isolation; they are usually responses to a question or statement, which is important for understanding the nuances of ~이/가 and ~는/은.
