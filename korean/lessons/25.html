Everything/All

모든

The most common way to say “every _____” is to put ‘모든’ before a noun. 모든 looks, sounds and acts like an adjective, but it cannot predicate a clause or sentence. So you will not see “모든” at the end of a sentence, just as in English. For example, you cannot say:

He is every…

Even though 모든 ends in ~ㄴ (like all adjectives placed before a noun to describe it: 예쁜, 아름다운, 똑똑한), the dictionary form 모드다 does not exist.

But like an adjective, “모든” can go before a noun, where it means “every _____.” For example:

모든 것
everything
모든 사람
all people/every person
모든 과일
all fruits/every fruit
모든 선생님
all teachers/every teacher

Those can now go in sentences very easily:

모든 아시아 사람들은 젓가락을 잘 쓴다
All Asian people use chopsticks well
모든 학생들은 하루 종일 잤어
All students slept all day

다

Another word for similar situations is 다. 다 is used as an adverb to say that “all” of something is done.

One use is in a sentence with an object, to say that an action was completed with nothing left behind. For example:

저는 라면을 다 먹었어요
I ate all the ramen
저는 숙제를 다 했어요
I did all of my homework
저는 소설을 다 읽었어요
I read the whole book – or – I read all the books
저는 월급을 이미 다 썼어요
I already spent all of my paycheque

Here “모든” and “다” create slightly different meanings. ‘다’ is about doing one action to completion and leaving nothing behind. 모든 says the action was performed on all possible nouns after “모든.” For example:

저는 라면을 다 먹었어요
I ate all of the ramen

This sentence says the action of “eating ramen” was completed with nothing left behind. In other words, there is no ramen left in the bowl because it was all eaten.

However, in this sentence:

저는 모든 라면을 먹었어요
I ate every ramen

This sentence is a bit ambiguous and a bit unnatural, since you would rarely say it, but it helps show the difference in nuance. Here there might have been many types of ramen in the house, and the speaker ate or tried “every one of them.” Whether any was finished is unclear, so some could still be left in the bowl or the cupboard.

If you really did eat every one and finished all the ramen in the house, “저는 라면을 다 먹었어요” is more natural, because the action was completed and nothing was left.

This is why this sentence has two translations:

저는 소설을 다 읽었어요
I read the whole book
저는 소설을 다 읽었어요
I read all the books

Depending on the situation, that sentence could mean either one. In the first, the “reading” was completed on one book with no pages left unread. In the second, it was completed on more than one book, with no pages left in any of them.

However, by just saying:

저는 모든 소설을 읽었어요

This one says the speaker has tried reading every book, but says nothing about whether any was finished.

다 can also say that all the members or things making up a subject do some action (or have some quality). For example:

사람들이 다 왔어요
All the people have come
사람들이 다 죽었어요
All the people died
부모님은 다 그래요
All parents are like that
남자들은 다 게을러요
All men are lazy
학생들은 다 공부하고 있어요
All of the students are studying
애기들이 다 낮잠 자고 있어요
All the babies are taking a nap

In these cases there is effectively no difference between 다 and 모든. Each of these sentences would mean the same thing:

모든 사람들이 왔어요
All the people have come
모든 사람들이 죽었어요
All the people died
모든 부모님은 그래요
All parents are like that
모든 남자들은 게을러요
All men are lazy
모든 학생들은 공부하고 있어요
All of the students are studying
모든 애기들이 낮잠을 자고 있어요
All the babies are taking a nap

The only difference is that ‘모든’ treats the subjects (even groups of people) almost as unique individuals within the group, while ‘다’ describes the subjects as a group. You do not need to worry about this now; the precise nuance is a feeling you will develop with time.

Because these uses are so similar, both can appear in the same sentence. For example:

모든 사람들이 다 왔어요
All the people have come
모든 사람들이 다 죽었어요
All the people died
모든 부모님은 다 그래요
All parents are like that
모든 남자들은 다 게을러요
All men are lazy
모든 학생들은 다 공부하고 있어요
All of the students are studying
모든 애기들이 다 낮잠 자고 있어요
All the babies are taking a nap

All of these can also be used in negative sentences, for example:

사람들이 다 안 왔어요
Not all the people have come
저는 라면을 다 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat all of the ramen
저는 숙제를 다 하지 못했어요
I couldn’t do all of my homework

다 can also be a noun. In this form it usually goes before 이다 or 아니다 to say that something is (or is not) “all.” For example:

그게 다야?
Is that all
이게 다가 아니야
This isn’t all

모두

Another word to know is “모두,” meaning “all.” It is generally used in these ways:

As an adverb
Here 모두 means essentially the same as “다.” For example:

선생님들은 모두 똑똑해요
All teachers are smart, which could also be written as:
선생님들은 다 똑똑해요
All teachers are smart, or
모든 선생님들은 똑똑해요
Every teacher is smart

In this adverb form, 다 can also come after 모두 in the same sentence. For example:

선생님들은 모두 다 똑똑해요
All teachers are smart

In this adverb form, no particles are attached to it.

As a (pro)noun
Here 모두 typically means “everybody” or “everything,” and particles are usually attached. For example:

모두가 이해했어요
Everybody understood
모두가 산책하고 있어요
Everybody is going for a walk

Here is the confusing part. When the structure is just Subject – 모두 – Verb, ~를 is often left off 모두. For example:

나는 모두를 이해했어 would be better said as:

나는 모두 이해했어
I understood everything

And

저는 모두를 먹고 싶어요 would be better said as:

저는 모두 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat everything

The best explanation is that in these cases 모두 is acting as an adverb. So it is not really that ~를 is omitted; 모두 is simply doing a different job (and the sentence means the same thing anyway).

Just as you would not see ~를 on 다 in these sentences:

나는 다 이해했어
I understood everything
저는 다 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat everything

Often a word for a group of people comes before 모두. Then ~를 is usually attached to 모두. For example:

저는 가족 모두를 사랑해요
I love all of my family
저는 학생 모두를 가르쳤어요
I taught all of the students

The same structure works when the group of people is the subject. For example:

학생 모두가 기숙사로 갔어요
All of the students went to the dorm
가족 모두가 고향에 갔어요
All of the/my family went to the/our hometown
선생님 모두가 회의에 갔어요
All of the teachers went to a meeting

Other particles can attach as well, for example:

저는 선물을 모두에게 주었어요
I gave a present to everybody

Everywhere/Every time/Everyone

~나 can be added to ‘where,’ ‘when’ and ‘who’ to mean ‘everywhere,’ ‘every time’ and ‘everyone.’ Particles are usually not added to these words. For example:

어디
where
어디나
everywhere
나는 한국에서 어디나 여행하고 싶어
I (would) want to travel everywhere in Korea
우리는 동남아시아에 어디나 갈 거예요
We will go everywhere in South East Asia
밥은 어디나 맛이 똑같아요
Rice tastes the same everywhere
언제
when
언제나
every time/always
그녀는 언제나 늦게 와요
She comes late every time
저는 언제나 똑같은 메뉴를 먹어요
I always eat the same menu
저는 언제나 똑같은 시간에 일어나요
I always wake up at the same time
누구
who
누구나
everyone
누구나 그 여자를 알아요
Everybody knows that girl
누구나 우리 애기를 보고 싶어요
Everybody wants to see our baby
누구나 한국을 좋아해요
Everybody likes Korea

Something/Somewhere/Sometime/Somebody

~ㄴ가 can be added to ‘what,’ ‘where,’ ‘when’ and ‘who’ to mean ‘something,’ ‘somewhere,’ ‘sometime’ and ‘somebody.’ For example:

뭐
what
뭔가
something

Subject and object particles can be omitted from any sentence (though it is best not to omit particles until you know Korean very well), but they are especially often omitted in these sentences. Many examples:

나는 방금 뭔가(를) 봤어
I just saw something a minute ago
등에 뭔가(가) 있어요
There is something on your back
등에 뭔가(가) 있나요?
Is there something on my back?
저는 팔에 뭔가(가)느껴져요
I feel something on my arm
저는 뭔가(를) 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat something
저는 뭔가(를) 말하고 싶어요
I want to say something
어디
where
어딘가
somewhere

Given what “somewhere” means, you will most often use ~에 or ~에서 in these sentences. Omitting ~에 from 어딘가 is acceptable; omitting ~에서 is less so. Many examples:

열쇠를 어딘가(에) 뒀어
I left my keys somewhere
전쟁이 아시아 어딘가에서 났어요
A war broke out somewhere in Asia
피가 어딘가에서 나오고 있어요
Blood is coming out of somewhere
저는 그릇을 어딘가(에) 두었어요
I put the bowl in some place
선생님들이 회의를 어딘가에서 하고 있어요
The teachers are having a meeting somewhere
저는 휴일에 어딘가에 가고 싶어요
I want to go somewhere on the holiday
저는 어딘가에서 커피를 마시고 싶어요
I want to drink a cup of coffee somewhere
언제
when
언젠가
sometime/someday

~에 is typically not attached to 언젠가. Many examples:

그 날이 언젠가 올 거예요
The day will come sometime
구름이 언젠가 걷힐 거예요
The clouds will clear eventually/sometime
저는 언젠가 경찰관이 되고 싶어요
I want to become a police officer someday
저는 언젠가 중국어도 배우고 싶어요
I want to learn Chinese as well some day
저는 언젠가 고향에 돌아가고 싶어요
I want to return (go back) to my hometown some day

You might wonder whether particles like ~까지 or ~부터 can attach to 언젠가, since it represents a time. In practice Koreans cannot produce such sentences: in both English and Korean, “until someday” or “from someday” is not logical.

누구
who
누군가
somebody
누군가(는) 너를 찾고 있어
Somebody is looking for you
저는 누군가의 열쇠를 찾았어요
I found somebody’s keys
경찰관들은 누군가와 얘기하고 있어요
The policemen are talking with somebody
누군가가 지갑을 잃어버렸어요
Somebody lost their wallet
누군가가 그 파일을 저에게 보냈어요
Somebody sent that file to me

In Lesson 22 you learned about 어느. Another use of 어느 is to refer to a vague place or point in time. It is included here because of its similarity to the sentences above.

You often see this in stories, poetry or news reports where the exact location need not be given. It is like the opening of a story in English:

A long time ago in a far-away castle, or
A long time ago in a galaxy far, far away

In this use, 어느 commonly goes before a broad indication of time or place. For example:

어느 날
some day (an ambiguous day)
어느 밤
some night (an ambiguous night)
어느 겨울
some winter (an ambiguous winter)
어느 여름
some summer (an ambiguous summer)
어느 마을
some village (an ambiguous village)
어느 시장
some market (an ambiguous market)

The speaker or writer uses this because the time does not need to be given specifically. Some example sentences:

어느 겨울 꽃이 다 사라졌다
Some winter, all the flowers disappeared
어느 마을에서 애기 두 명이 태어났다
Two babies were born in some village
그 다음 날, 할머니가 어느 시장을 구경하고 있었다
The next day, the grandmother was browsing around some market

Again, you will most likely see these sentences in stories, often setting the scene.

A question word can also refer to an ambiguous place, thing or person. This is most common with these words:

어디

Person 1: 엄마가 집에 있어?
Is mom at home
Person 2: 아니요. 어디 갔어.
No, she went somewhere

뭐

우리가 이미 뭐 먹었어요
We already ate something

누구

나는 내일 누구 만날 거야
Tomorrow I’m going to meet somebody

This use is quite advanced and usually takes years of exposure to pick up. What makes it harder is that the same words appear in sentences that ask a question. For example:

뭐 먹었어요? Could mean both:
– What did you eat? or
– Did you eat something?

어디 갔어요? Could mean both:
– Where did you go? or
– Did you go somewhere

누구(를) 만났어요? Could mean both:
– Who did you meet? Or
– Did you meet somebody

You can tell them apart fairly easily once your ear is trained. When “뭐, 어디 or 누구” is the question word, the stress falls on the question word itself. Read these aloud, stressing the question word:

뭐 먹었어요?
What did you eat?
어디 갔어요?
Where did you go?
누구 만났어요?
Who did you meet?

When “뭐, 어디 or 누구” refers to an ambiguous thing, place or person, the stress falls on the verb instead. Read these aloud and notice the difference from the examples above:

뭐 먹었어요?
Did you eat something?
어디 갔어요?
Did you go somewhere?
누구 만났어요?
Did you meet somebody?

As mentioned, this is quite advanced. You may not hear the difference yet, but it is good to be aware of as you progress.

아무 Anybody/Nobody

아무 is a pronoun referring to “any” person. It signals that the person is not a specific “set” person, but could be “anybody.”

It is most commonly used with ~나 attached. This particle (attached to other nouns, as you will learn in Lesson 58) often carries the same indifference that “아무” itself has.

Together they can almost be seen as one unit meaning “anybody.” Since ~나 is itself a particle, when it is the subject or object of a sentence the particles ~는 or ~가 are not usually stacked on top of ~나. For example:

아무나 그 책을 좋아할 거예요
Anybody will/would like that book
이렇게 좋은 학교에서는 아무나 공부를 잘 할 거예요
Anybody can study well at this school

Particles can be added before ~나, though, when the word has a role other than subject or object. Since 아무 refers to a person here, the most common are the particles for “to” (~에게/한테) and “with” (~와/하고). For example:

나는 아무와나 사귀고 싶어
I want to go out with anybody
저는 이 선물을 아무에게나 주고 싶어요
I want to give this present to anybody
저는 아무하고나 축구를 하고 싶어요
I want to play soccer with anybody

When ~도 replaces ~나, the meaning flips. 아무도 can be the subject or object of a sentence meaning “nobody.” As with 아무나, particles are not usually attached to 아무도, since “~도” is itself the particle.

A sentence with 아무도 must be conjugated negatively or end with a negative word like 없다. This feels counterintuitive to English speakers, because it looks like a double negative. For example:

집에 아무도 있어요 looks like it should mean “there is nobody at home.” But a sentence with “아무도” must end negatively, so this is correct:

집에 아무도 없어요
There is nobody at home/There isn’t anybody at home

The Korean double negative makes the English translation awkward. In English we can say either “anybody” with a negative verb or “nobody” with a positive one.

For example:

저는 아무도 못 봤어요
I didn’t see anybody/I saw nobody
저는 아무도 만나고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to meet anybody/I want to meet nobody
아무도 집에 가지 않았어요
Nobody went home
아무도 나를 좋아하지 않아
Nobody likes me

Particles can be added before ~도 when the word has a role other than subject or object. Since 아무 refers to a person here, the most common are the particles for “to” (~에게/한테) and “with” (~와/하고). For example:

저는 그 말을 아무에게도 안 했어요
I didn’t say that to anybody/I said that to nobody
저는 아무하고도 얘기하고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to talk with anybody/I want to talk with nobody

These double negatives are easy to mix up at first. To keep it simple, use “아무나” when the sentence ends positively and “아무도” when it ends negatively. In theory, 아무나 and 아무도 are the same word with different particles; one just goes with positive sentences and the other with negative ones.

아무 Used Before Other Nouns

아무 can also go before a thing, place or time to describe it. The most common nouns after 아무 are:

거
short form of 것, meaning “thing”
데
meaning “place”
때
meaning “time”

With ~나 attached to these nouns, the speaker signals that the thing, place or time is not specific but could be “anything,” “anywhere” or “anytime.” For example:

저는 아무 거나 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat anything (I’d eat anything)
아무 때나 좋아요
Anytime is good
저는 아무 데나 가고 싶어요
I want to go anywhere (I’d go anywhere)

In the example above, “데” refers to a place. When the verb would require “~에” on that place, “~에” is omitted. But when the verb requires “~에서,” “~에서” should be added before ~나.

For example, the same place appears in both sentences below, but because of the verbs 가다 and 먹다, ~에 is used in one and ~에서 in the other.

저는 공원에 가고 싶어요
I want to go to the park
저는 밥을 공원에서 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat in the park

The difference between these two sentences is the same as between these two:

저는 아무 데나 가고 싶어요
I want to go anywhere
저는 아무 데서나 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat (at) anywhere

~도 can replace ~나 here to mean “nothing” or “nowhere.” For example:

저는 아무 것도 먹고 싶지 않아요
I don’t want to eat anything/I want to eat nothing
아기는 아무 데도 가지 않았어
The baby didn’t go anywhere/The baby went nowhere

“아무 때도” would mean “no time?”, which makes no sense. Use 전혀 (introduced in Lesson 34) instead.

Other nouns can follow 아무 too. For example:

저는 아무 버스나 타고 갈 거예요
I’m going to take any bus and leave
저는 아무 말도 하지 않았어요
I didn’t say anything/I said nothing
저는 엄마랑 아무 관계도 없어요
I don’t have any relationship with my mother/I have no relationship with my mother

But by far the three most common words after 아무 are 거, 데 and 때.

Many more examples with 아무 (and the words it forms) would need grammar you have not learned yet. The two most common missing pieces are:

Making a command (introduced in Lesson 40), and
One can/cannot do (Introduced in Lesson 45)

Here are some quick examples using those two patterns, in case you have already met them by looking ahead or from another resource.

Making a command

가방을 아무 데나 두세요
Put your bag down anywhere
아무 거나 고르세요!
Choose anything!
아무 거나 먹자!
Let’s eat anything!

One can/cannot do

아무나 그것을 할 수 있어요
Anybody can do that

It may also help to peek at Lesson 58 to see how ~나 is used outside these specific cases of 아무나, 아무 거나, 아무 때나, etc. In theory these words belong after ~나 is taught, but the use of ~나 in this lesson is more common (and so more important) than the general use introduced later.

3

Each(마다)

마다 means “each” and attaches directly to the end of a noun:

날마다
each day
학생마다
each student
금요일마다
each/every Friday
30분마다
each/every 30 minutes

A natural question here:

“What is the difference between ‘모든 학생’, and 학생마다?”

The difference is subtle, but it exists. The only way to put it is this:
If you can explain the difference between “every student” and “each student” in English, then you fully understand the difference between “모든 학생” and “학생마다.”

Just remember that 마다 means “each” and “모든” means “every.” That is more than enough.

그 버스는 10분마다 와요
That bus comes each/every 10 minutes
학생마다 달라요
Each student is different

With that, you have finished Unit 1. You have come a really long way from the first lessons, where you were learning sentences like “나는 선생님이다.”
