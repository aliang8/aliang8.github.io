Vocabulary

The vocabulary is split into nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs to keep things simple.

Nouns:

점원 = store assistant

The pronunciation of this word is closer to “저뭔”

Common Usages:

점원 모집
Clerk Wanted (you might see this on a sign outside a store looking for applicants)

Example:

저는 점원한테 질문을 물어봤어요
I asked the clerk a question
이 가게는 점원 한  명만 있어요
This store only has one person working here
배우 = actor

Common Usages:

주연배우
leading actor/actress
조연배우
supporting actor/actress
배우상
acting award

Example:

배우들은 돈을 많이 벌어요
Actors earn a lot of money
배우들은 그들의 영화를 보통 좋아하지 않아
Actors usually don’t like their movies
목 = neck/throat

Common Usages:

목소리
voice (literally, throat sound)
목 마르다
to be thirsty (literally, a dry throat)
손목
wrist (literally, hand neck)
Example: 목이 아파요
I have a sore throat
소리 = noise/sound

Common Usages:

목소리
voice (literally, throat sound)
소리가 나다
for a sound to be make
소리가 들리다
for a sound to be heard
소리를 지르다
to scream
무슨 소리야?
What is that sound?

This phrase literally means “what is that sound?” and it can be used to have that meaning.  However, it is commonly used to show disagreement to what somebody says. In English, this would be similar to saying “What are you talking about!?”

소리가 안 들려요
I can’t hear the sound

The Korean sentence above is predicated by an intransitive verb (들리다), which means it cannot act on an object. The English translation is predicated by a transitive verb (to hear), which means it can act on an object. A more direct translation of the Korean sentence would be “the sound cannot be heard.” However, this phrase is often used to indicate that one cannot hear something.

Example:

무슨 소리예요?
What is that sound?
소리를 많이 질러도 그는 제 말을 듣지 못했어요
Even though I screamed a lot, he didn’t hear my voice

This word appears in my Korean Sign Explanation Video #1.

목소리 = voice

Example:

저는 선생님의 목소리를 못 들었어요
I couldn’t hear the teacher’s voice
목소리가 잘 들려요
I hear you (your voice) well

The Korean sentence above is predicated by an intransitive verb (들리다), which means it cannot act on an object. The English translation is predicated by a transitive verb (to hear), which means it can act on an object. A more direct translation of the Korean sentence would be “Your voice can be heard well/clearly.” However, this phrase is often used to indicate that one cannot hear another person’s voice.

닭 = chicken

The pronunciation of this word is closer to “닥”
Typically, the pronunciation of words with four-letter syllables merge with the pronunciation of an attaching grammatical principle. For example, “값” is pronounced as “kap” but 값을 is pronounced as “갑슬.” However, the ㄹ in 닭 is usually not pronounced in these cases. For example, 닭을 is usually pronounced as “닥을” or “다글” and 닭이 is usually pronounced as “다기” or “닥이.”

Common Usages

닭갈비
chicken ribs
닭살
goose-bumps

Notes: When eating Western style chicken (like in KFC), Korean people use the word “치킨”

Example: 그 농장은 닭을 키워요
That farm raises chickens

넘어지다 = to fall

Common Usages:

넘어질 뻔하다

Example:

길이 미끄러워서 저는 넘어졌어요

네가 넘어질 것처럼 보였어
It looked like you were going to fall
늙은 아주머니는 넘어졌어요
The old lady fell over
만약 엄마의 손을 안 잡았더라면 넘어졌을 거예요
If I didn’t grab mom’s hand, I would have fallen
독서하다 = to read a book

Common Usages:

독서실
reading room

Notes: This word less common and more difficult than “읽다.” When using 독서하다, within the meaning of the verb is that one is reading a book. You don’t need to include the object “책” if using this verb. It is common in its noun form “독서” to refer to the noun of reading a book.

Example:

심심할 때 독서를 하는 것이 좋아요
When bored, reading books is good
내일 공부하러 독서실에 갈래요?
Shall we go to the reading room tomorrow to study?

Example:

우리는 언제 출발할 거예요?
When are we going to leave/depart?
비행기가 아직 출발할 준비가 안 됐습니까?

다음 버스는 저 정류장에서 출발할 거예요

우리는 집에서 출발할 거예요
We will depart from home
비행기가 9시에 출발할 예정이지만 눈이 많이 와서 못 출발할 것 같아요
The plane is scheduled to depart at 9:00, but it probably won’t because it is snowing a lot

나오다 = to come out

Common Usages: There are many different ways this word can be used

집에서 나오다
to come out of a house
영화에 나오다
to be/appear in a movie (to come out of a movie)
펜이 안 나오다
the pen isn’t working (ink isn’t coming out of the pen)
회사에 나오다
to show up for work
말이 안 나오다
for words to not come out (of one’s mouth)

Notes: Aside from literally meaning “to come out” Korean people use this word to say that somebody is “in” a movie or TV show. In English, I would say “Will Smith is in Men in Black.” In Korean, they would say “Will Smith은 Men in Black에 나왔어요.”

Example:

학생들은 교실에서 나왔어요
The students came out of the classroom
결과가 아직 안 나왔어요
The results haven’t come out yet

쪽 = side/direction

Notes: 쪽 is a noun, but a special noun. I refer to these sometimes as “pseudo-nouns” in my lessons. Other nouns like this are 적 (Lesson 32) and 수 (Lesson 45). 쪽 can only ever be used when some sort of context is given to it.

You can see that if you describe it using the concepts in Lesson 26:

저는 다른 사람들이 가는 쪽으로 갈 거예요
I will go in the direction that other people are going

Or, you can place a noun immediately before it:

저는 학교 쪽으로 갈 거예요
I will go in the direction of school

Common things that 쪽 is placed after are words on their own. Similar how 이 것, 그 것 and 저 것 are actually words as 이것, 그것 and 저것. For example:

이쪽
towards this direction
그쪽
towards that direction
저쪽
towards that direction
오른쪽
to the right
왼쪽
to the left
안쪽
towards the inside

I talk about the use of 쪽 in a Korean sign in this Youtube Video.

Examples:

우리가 저쪽으로 가야 돼요
We have to go that way (in that direction)
오른쪽으로 가세요
Go right (in the direction of right)
왼쪽으로 가세요
Go left (in the direction of left)

열심히 공부한 후에 실력은 빨리 늘었어요
After studying hard, my skills have increased
저는 회사를 위해 열심히 일할 거예요
I will work hard for the company
저는 정신과의사가 되려고 열심히 공부하고 있어요
I am studying hard to become a psychiatrist
완전히 = perfectly, completely

Example:

저는 완전히 이해해요
I completely understand
그 강은 완전히 말랐어요
That river has completely dried up
한국 문법은 영어 문법과 완전히 달라요
Korean grammar is completely different from English grammar
문이 완전히 열릴 때까지 버스에서 내리거나 문에 기대지 마세요
Until the door is completely open, don’t get off the bus or lean on the door
몇 = some ____, how many (used with a counter)

Common Usages:

몇 명
some people OR how many people
몇 개
some things OR how many things
몇 시예요?
What time is it?
몇 살이에요?
How old are you?

Notes: 몇 is placed before a counter to ask “how many” of something. For example:

펜 몇 개 있어요?
How many pens do you have (see Lesson 22 for more information)

몇 can also be used to mean “some.” For example:

저는 사과 몇 개를 샀어요
I bought some apples (see Lesson 12 for more information)

Examples:

저는 친구 몇 명을 만났어요
I met some friends
친구가 몇 명 있어요?
How many friends do you have?
층 = floor

Common Usages

3층
third floor
4층
fourth floor
지하층
basement floor

Notes: Placed after a number to indicate the “third floor,” fourth floor,” etc…

Example:

저는 2층에서 살아요
I live on the second floor

.

Introduction

So far you have learned the particles 는/은, 이/가, 를/을, 의 and 도. There are many more, and this lesson covers a lot of them: ~들, ~만, ~에서, ~부터, ~까지 and ~(으)로.

An Important Note: Eliminating the Subject

One thing not yet mentioned: Koreans often omit the topic/subject of a sentence, especially when it is ‘I.’ Koreans like their sentences as short as possible, and this is one more way to do it. Whenever the subject is clear from the situation, it is usually dropped. For example, instead of:

저는 아침식사를 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat breakfast

They would say:

아침식사를 안 먹었어요
I didn’t eat breakfast

Both are perfect sentences and both are used, but be aware that Koreans often drop the subject/topic entirely when speaking. These lessons will do the same from time to time.

.

Korean Particle~들 and using몇 with a counter

You have probably wondered how to make something plural. Nothing has been said about plurals so far because Koreans rarely distinguish singular from plural. For example:

나는 사과를 샀어

This could mean “I bought an apple” OR “I bought apples.” That seems crazy to English speakers, but it is how Korean works. Context usually makes it clear. To make clear that you bought one apple, you could say:

나는 사과 한 개를 샀어
I bought one apple

To handle this ambiguity you can also use 몇, which replaces a number in examples like 두 개/두 명/두 번. When 몇 replaces the number (몇 번/몇 명/몇 개), it means “some ____.” For example, instead of “나는 사과 한 개를 샀어”, you could say:

나는 사과 몇 개를 샀어
I bought SOME apples

More examples (remember that 펜 한 개 and 한 개의 펜 mean the same thing):

나는 몇 개의 펜을 샀어
I bought some pens
나는 몇 명의 사람을 만났어
I met some people
나는 학교에 몇 번 갔어
I went to school a few/some times (not sometimes)

(Remember, 번 acts as an adverb, so it takes no particle.)

Back to plurals. ~들 attaches to a noun to make it plural. But adding ~들 to a noun that is not a person is usually unnatural. So this would be unnatural:

저는 사과들을 샀어요

Instead, ~들 is usually only attached to “person” (사람) or other words meaning people (actors, workers, doctors, etc…).

의사들은 돈이 많아
doctors have a lot of money
선생님은 내일 학생들을 만날 거야
The teacher will meet the students tomorrow
배우들은 그들의* 영화를 보통 좋아하지 않아
actors usually don’t like their movies

*Adding the possessive particle 의 to 그들 (them) gives 그들의 (their)

1

Korean Particle~만 (only)

The particle ~만 is very common and means “only.” It attaches directly to the end of a noun to mean “only (that noun).” For example:

나는 물만 마셔
I only drink water

It can attach to the subject or the object, and in each case it replaces the particle normally found there (~은/는 or ~을/를). For example:

나만 그 여자를 좋아해
Only I like that girl
나는 그 여자만 좋아해
I only like that girl
나만 사과를 샀어
Only I bought apples
나는 사과만 샀어
I only bought apples

You can also stress that you bought only one apple (or any number of things) by putting ~만 on a counter:

나는 사과 한 개만 샀어
I only bought 1 apple
저는 차 두 대만 있어요
I only have two cars
저는 친구 한 명만 만났어요
I only met one friend

Lesson 3 mentioned this, but learners often forget: when a verb ends in 하다, the part before 하다 is usually the noun form of that verb. The earlier examples were:

성공하다
succeed
성공
success
말하다
speak
말
speech/words
성취하다
achieve
성취
achievement
With these verbs, the part before 하다 can be separated from 하다 to make a noun form of that verb. Then, “하다,” meaning “do” can act on that noun (I do study
I study). It is hard to explain, but look at the following example:
나는 공부했어
I studied
나는 공부를 했어
I studied

Those two mean exactly the same, even though in the second 공부 stands alone as a noun. Why does this matter? Because you can now treat 공부 as a regular noun and attach 만 to it:

저는 공부만 했어요
I only studied
저는 어제 일만 했어요
Yesterday, I only worked
Just because a word ends in 하다 does not mean you can do this. Many adjectives end in 하다 (for example, 행복하다: happy), but this:
저는 행복만 해요
I am only happy – doesn’t really make a lot of sense

Many verbs do not end in 하다, just 다 (for example 가다, 먹다, 보내다). How to change those into a form that can take ~만 is covered in Lesson 29.

The examples above attach ~만 to nouns, but it can attach to a wide variety of things, including grammatical principles and other particles. That comes after a few more lessons on basics.

When ~만 attaches to more complicated things, it usually does not replace anything but sits alongside. On a subject or object, ~을/를 or ~은/는 are removed; attached to something else, everything usually stays in place.

Here is how it attaches to the ~에 particle you already know. Other particles follow in later lessons.

우리는 학교에만 갔어요
We only went to school
학생들은 교실 안에만 있어요
The students are only in their classrooms
저는 밤에만 운동해요
I exercise only at night
저는 커피를 낮에만 마셔요
I drink coffee only during the day
저는 그 여자를 그때만 사랑했어요
I loved her only at that time

With 그때, ~에 is often omitted because it can be assumed.

~만 shows up constantly on Korean signs.

2

Korean Particle~에서

Learners are often confused about when to use ~에서 instead of ~에, since both mark places. ~에서 marks the location where the subject is doing something.

For example:

저는 학교에서 공부할 거예요
I will study at school
저는 저의 친구를 병원에서 봤어요
I saw my friend at the hospital
저는 남편을 공원에서 만날 거예요
I will meet my husband at the park
저는 한국어를 한국에서 배웠어요
I learned Korean in Korea

To make the purpose of ~에서 clear, here is the distinction from ~에. Again, ~에서 marks the location in which the subject is doing something.

This does not mean the location he/she is going to
This does not mean the location he/she is looking at
This does not mean the location he/she places something on
This does not mean the location he/she places something in

All of those locations would take the particle “~에”.

~에서, on the other hand, is where the subject (the acting agent of the sentence) is while doing the action. Look at this example:

저는 건물에 간판을 봤어요

Where is the subject (저) when doing the action (보다)? ~에서 is not used, so the sentence does not say where the subject was when seeing the sign. Context might tell, but this sentence does not. The person saw the sign “on the building”, as if walking by and seeing the sign attached to it. The action did not occur at/on/in the building; that is just where he/she was looking.

Compare this sentence:
저는 건물에서 간판을 봤어요

Where is the subject (저) when doing the action (보다)? ~에서 is attached to “건물,” so the subject was in the building and saw the sign.

Another example:

저는 병을 탁자에 놓았어요

Where is the subject (저) when doing the action (놓다)? ~에서 is not used, so the sentence does not say where the subject was when putting the bottle on the table. Context might tell, but this sentence does not. The person put the bottle “on the table.”

Compare this sentence:
저는 병을 탁자에서 놓았어요

This sentence is nonsense. It says the action occurred on/in the table: the subject, somehow inside the table, placed the bottle somewhere. It is so nonsensical that it does not even say where the bottle went. Roughly, “(While I was) in the table, I placed the bottle.” Do not dwell on the translation; a sentence that makes no sense is hard to translate.

But because ~에서 marks where the subject acts and ~에 marks where the bottle is placed, both ~에 and ~에서 can appear in one sentence. For example:

저는 방에서 탁자에 병을 놓았어요
I placed the bottle on the table in the room

This is also why ~에 goes on the place a person is going to. For example:

저는 한국에서 갈 거예요
(This sentence is correct, but it says the person left from Korea, because the action of “going” (가다) occurs at/in Korea. This function is covered a little later.)

To mark the place you are going to (and so are not currently in), use ~에. For example:

저는 한국에 갈 거예요
I will go to Korea

~에서 can also attach to the location where an adjective “occurs.” “Occurs” is an awkward word here, since adjectives do not really occur, but there is no better one. Just as a verb can be used with a subject…:

저는 잤어요
I slept

…and a location can say where that action occurred:

저는 집에서 잤어요
I slept at home

…adjectives can be used with a subject…:

과일은 비싸요
Fruit is expensive

…and a location can say where that adjective “occurs”:

과일은 한국에서 비싸요
Fruit is expensive in Korea

Here are some other examples:

저는 학교에서 추웠어요
I was cold at school
고등학교는 한국에서 어려워요
High school is difficult in Korea
녹차는 한국에서 유명해요
Green Tea is famous in Korea

Not many examples here, because perfectly natural sentences of this kind need more complicated grammar you have not met yet. For now, understand this function of ~에서: marking where a verb or adjective “occurs.”

Also, when you say where something is using 있다, use ~에, not ~에서. For example:

저는 집에 있어요
I’m at home
저는 차 안에 있어요
I’m in the car

The other main use of ~에서 is “from.” Most basically, it marks the place the subject departs from. This is the use mentioned earlier. For example:

저는 한국에서 갈 거예요
I will go from Korea
다음 버스는 저 정류장에서 출발할 거예요
The next bus will depart from that station

The same use extends to situations similar to “departing.” For example:

When getting off something (bus/train):

저는 서울역에서 내릴 거예요
I will get off at (from) Seoul station

When something or someone comes, goes or is taken out of something:

학생은 교실에서 나왔어요
the student came out of the classroom

It also marks the country (or any place) you come from. English says “I come from Canada/I’m from Canada,” but Korean uses the past tense of “come”:

저는 캐나다에서 왔어요
I come from Canada

Many textbooks teach “저는 ___에서 왔어요” in the first or second lesson, before any of the grammar inside it. Nothing explains why 저 rather than 나, why 는, what 에서 means, what 오다 means, how 오다 becomes 왔다, or how 왔다 becomes 왔어요. By now you can see every piece.

When ~에서 is added to 여기/거기/저기 (here, there, there), it is common to write and say:

여기서 instead of 여기에서
거기서 instead of 거기에서
저기서 instead of 저기에서

There are still more ways ~에서 means “from.” Below is a preview using some grammar not yet introduced. Noting these sentence types now may help when you meet similar ones later.

저는 학교에서 멀리 살고 있어요
I live far from school
1에서 10까지 센다
Count from 1 to 10
그들은 많은 후보자들 중에서 저를 뽑았어요
They chose me from many candidates
1시에서 2시까지 오세요
Please come from 1:00 to 2:00
10에서 5를 뺀다
Subtract 5 from 10

As you can see, English ‘from’ has many uses too. When a Korean word and its English counterpart both have many meanings, mastering the usage is challenging but rewarding when it comes together.

3

Korean Particles~부터 and~까지

Two more important particles are ~부터 and ~까지.

~까지 is used with or without ~에서 to mean “to/until a place/time.” For example:

3시까지 기다릴 거예요
I will wait until 3:00
그 여자를 지금까지 좋아했어요
I liked that girl until now
저는 그 회사에서 5월까지 일할 거예요
I will work at that company until May
저는 그때까지 김치를 먹지 않았어요
I hadn’t eaten Kimchi until that time
저는 오늘 이 책을 여기까지 읽었어요
Today, I read up to here in this book
저는 한강까지 달렸어요
I ran until (I reached) the Han River

~부터 is often confused with ~에서, because both can translate as “from” and seem to overlap. You learned that one use of ~에서 is the location an action departs from. For example:

우리는 집에서 출발할 거예요
We will depart from home

~부터 is very similar, but specifically marks the place (or time) something starts from. Take this sentence:

나는 인천에서 서울까지 갈 거야
I will depart from Incheon and go to (until) Seoul

~에서 says the person departed from 인천. In theory, that is also the starting point, so the sentence could also be written:

나는 인천부터 서울까지 갈 거야
I will go from Incheon to Seoul

Apart from the slight nuance of “departing” versus “starting,” these two are essentially the same: the subject goes from Incheon to Seoul. Both are correct, but most Koreans prefer ~에서 for the location where something starts or departs.

A native speaker was shown these two sentences and asked to explain the difference:

다음 버스는 저 정류장에서 출발할 거예요
The next bus will depart from that station
다음 버스는 저 정류장부터 출발할 거예요
The next bus will leave from that stop

He said: “The first one sounds more natural. The second sounds as if the bus is leaving from the bus garage, like the absolute starting point of the bus. In most situations the first sentence is the natural one.”

Instead, ~부터 is commonly attached to a time to say when something starts. For example:

저는 어제부터 아팠어요
I have been sick since (from) yesterday
저는 내일부터 한국어를 공부할 거예요
I’m going to study Korean from tomorrow
내년부터 우리는 서울에서 살 거예요
From next year, we will be living in Seoul
저는 3시부터 학교에 있을 거예요
I will be at school from 3:00
저는 작년부터 한국어를 배웠어요
I have been learning Korean since last year

~까지 very often appears in the same sentence as ~부터: ~부터 marks the start point and ~까지 the end point. For example:

저는 아침부터 밤까지 공부만 했어요
From morning to night I only studied
나는 캐나다에 1일부터 8일까지 있을 거야
I will be in Canada from the 1st to the 8th

“부터” is often attached to 처음 to mean “from the start” or “from the beginning.” For example:

그들은 저를 처음부터 싫어했어요
They didn’t like me from the start
우리는 그 일을 처음부터 시작할 거예요
We will start that job/task from the beginning

For “from start to finish,” the word “끝” is often used for “finish.” For example:

저는 그 상황을 처음부터 끝까지 몰랐어요
I didn’t know that situation from start to finish
저는 그것을 처음부터 끝까지 복습했어요
I reviewed that from start to finish
저는 그 책을 처음부터 끝까지 읽었어요
I read that book from start to finish
4

Korean Particle~(으)로

The particle ~(으)로 attaches to nouns with a few different meanings. One main meaning is the tool, device, method or material something is done with. The English equivalent varies:

Write with a pen
Go to the store by car
Go to school on foot
Make a house out of wood

This meaning of ~(으)로 applies in so many situations that listing them all is impossible. Get comfortable with the basic examples and you will gradually sense when to use it elsewhere.

~로 is added to words ending in a vowel, ~으로 to words ending in a consonant. ~로 is also added to words ending in ㄹ. The only reason is ease of pronunciation: in “것로” your tongue cannot go straight from 것 to ~로, so it becomes 것으로.

나는 우리 집을 나무로 지었어
I built our house out of wood
배로 제주에 갈 거야
I will go to Je-ju by boat
저는 그것을 손으로 만들었어요
I built that with my hands

In the same way, ~(으)로 marks the language something is spoken in. As in some examples above, the language is the “tool” of communication. For example:

저는 그 문장을 한국어로 말했어요
I said that sentence (using) in Korean
저는 그것을 영어로 할 거예요
I will say that (using) in English

It also marks what you ate for a specific meal:

저는 아침식사로 밥을 먹었어요
I ate rice for breakfast
저는 보통 점심식사로 과일만 먹었어요
I usually only ate fruit for lunch

If someone does an action in a line of people, ~(으)로 on a number + 번째 gives the order it was done in. For example:

저는 그것을 두 번째로 했어요
I did that second (I was the second person to do that)
저는 학교에 두 번째로 왔어요
I came to school second (I was the second person to come to school)
저는 그것을 첫 번째로 할 거예요
I will go (do it) first

The other main meaning of ~(으)로 is the direction something happens in. This sometimes matches “에.” For example:

저는 집으로 갈 거예요
I will go in the direction of home (simply ‘I will go home’), which would be the same as:
저는 집에 갈 거예요
I will go home

~(으)로 is often added after ~쪽 to make “~쪽으로”. ~쪽 attaches to some nouns and direction words (above/below/East/West/etc) to mean “the direction of ___.”

그쪽
that way/direction
위쪽
upper direction
사람 쪽
the direction of the people, etc..

To make sentences like:

저의 친구는 저 쪽으로 갔어요
My friend went that way
학생들은 교실 쪽으로 걸어요
Students walk towards/in the direction of their class

Notice the difference between these two:

저는 집 안 쪽으로 달렸어요
I ran inside the house
저는 집 안에서 달렸어요
I ran inside the house

In the first example, you run into the house, in the direction of ‘inside the house.’ In the second, you run around inside the house.

A common sign on trains warns passengers that the door opens “in the direction of inside” with “안쪽으로”.

Another common sign tells people not to go “in the direction” of below/downwards with “아래로.”
