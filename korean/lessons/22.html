Notes: Incredibly common word that is used in very important situations. 자리 is some area of space, but not really 3-dimensional space. It is more space on the ground or something similar to that. Introduction

In the previous lesson you learned how to form questions in Korean. This lesson builds on Lesson 21 with more question words: how, what, which and how many.

1

How(어떻게)

어떻게 is the easiest word in this lesson. It is actually 어떻다 (a word you do not know yet) turned into an adverb by adding ~게 to the stem (어떻 + 게). Although 어떻다 and 어떻게 are technically the same word, do not think of them that way. Just remember that 어떻게 means “how.” You can use 어떻게 to ask how somebody does a verb, but it cannot go before an adjective or adverb to mean “how (adjective/adverb).” For example:

“Learn” is a verb, so you can use 어떻게 in this sentence:
How did you learn?

“Beautiful” is an adjective. “Often” is an adverb. So you can not use 어떻게 in these sentences:
How beautiful is your girlfriend?
How often do you go to school?

Later in this lesson you will learn another word (얼마나) for those sentences.

어떻게 is an adverb, so it can be placed quite freely. It works in past, present and future questions. For example:

그것을 어떻게 해요?
How do you do that?
한국어를 어떻게 배웠어요?
How did you learn Korean?
그 자리를 어떻게 찾았어요?
How did you find that seat?
그 파일을 어떻게 보낼 거예요?
How are you going to send that file?

One difference between English and Korean: to ask “what do you think about…,” Korean uses the equivalent of “how do you think about…”:

그 여자에 대해 어떻게 생각해요?
What do you think about that girl?

That is nearly all you need to know about ‘어떻게,’ but there is more to know about the word 어떻다.

어때?
어떻다 is rarely used as 어떻다 in sentences. In fact, if you asked Korean people whether ‘어떻다’ and 어떻게’ are the same word, they would probably say they are completely different.

Besides 어떻게, there is another way 어떻다 appears in sentences. Through conjugation, 어떻다 becomes ‘어때.’ You do not need to worry about how this change happens (only a few other Korean words transform in a similar way).

  1. 어떻다 is an adjective
  2. ~아/어하다 can be added to some adjectives (You already know about 좋다 + ~아/어하다 = 좋아하다)
  3. The addition of ~아/어하다 causes the ㅎ to drop (you will learn more about this in the next lesson)
  4. 어떠하다 can conjugate to 어떠해
  5. Say 어떠해 fast enough and it comes out as “어때”

어때 is used to ask “how is/was the…?” or “what do/did you think about…?”, that is, to ask for somebody's opinion or evaluation of something. It sounds more natural for the thing you ask about with “어때” to have no particle. For example:

남자 친구 어때?
How is your boyfriend? (is he good/bad/handsome/etc..?)

It is also very common in the present tense to say “how about… ?/what do you think of…?” For example:

점심 어때?
How about lunch? / What do you think about lunch?

Imagine you and a friend are searching through pictures for the best profile picture. You find one you like and want to ask “what do you think about this picture/how about this picture?” You can say:

이 사진(이) 어때?
How about this picture?

In fact, 어때 is probably the most commonly mistranslated word by Korean speakers learning English. Ask a Korean what ‘어때’ means and they will say “how about.” Sometimes that is true, but 어때 only works as “how about” in limited cases. In the picture example it does translate to “how about.” But what about the boyfriend example?:

How about your boyfriend?

That sounds odd, and most English speakers probably could not even understand it.

If you have a Korean friend whose English is not perfect, ask how to say “한국 어때?” in English. They will almost certainly say “How about Korea?” But it really means “what do you think about Korea?/How is Korea?”

~요 can be added to 어때 to make it more formal. 어때 can also be put in the past tense to ask about the past, though even then the past tense is not strictly necessary:

시험 어땠어요?
How was the exam?/What did you think about the exam? (was it hard/easy?)
점심 어땠어요?
How was the lunch?/What did you think about the lunch? (was it delicious?)
2

What(뭐/무엇/무슨)

Now that you have the easy ways to ask questions, on to the hard ones. Asking “what” in Korean is probably the hardest grammar you will meet for a while. Essentially there are three ways to say “what:”

뭐 – which is a pronoun
무엇 – which is a pronoun
무슨 – which is a word that can be placed before nouns to describe them

Let me explain how each one is used.

3

뭐 and무엇

These two have essentially the same meaning and function. First, 뭐.

뭐 works like 언제, 어디 and 누구: it stands for an unknown thing in a sentence. For example:

밥을 먹었어
I ate rice
뭐 먹었어요?
What did you eat?
계란을 샀어요
I bought eggs
뭐 샀어요?
What did you buy?

Like 누구 (from the previous lesson), “뭐” and “what” act as pronouns in their sentences. More examples:

파일을 첨부했어요
I attached the file
뭐 첨부했어요?
What did you attach?
치마를 입었어요
I put on a skirt
뭐 입었어요?
What did you put on/wear?

Particles are not usually attached to 뭐 in questions like this. These would be unnatural:

뭐를 먹었어요?
뭐를 첨부했어요?
뭐를 입었어요?

One time a particle can attach to 뭐 is when asking what somebody is referring to in a previous sentence where a noun was omitted. Look at this dialogue:

Person 1: 저는 많이 먹었어요
I ate a lot
Person 2: 뭐를?
what? (you ate a lot of what?)

The first person did not say what he or she ate. The second person asks for clarification and can use “뭐를.” Here “뭐” stands in for the noun omitted in the previous sentence.

무엇 is very similar to 뭐, but it is more common to attach ~을 to 무엇 than to 뭐. For example:

점심을 먹었어?
Did you eat lunch?
무엇을 먹었어?
What did you eat?
무엇을 입었어요?
What did you put on/wear?
무엇을 샀어요?
What did you buy?

Before 이다, to ask what something “is,” 뭐 is more common than 무엇. For example:

이것이 뭐야?
What is this?
이름이 뭐예요?
What is your name?
점심이 뭐야?
What is (for) lunch?

Instead of:
이것이 무엇이야?
이름이 무엇이야?
점심이 무엇이야?

4

무슨

무슨 also translates to “what,” but it is placed before nouns to describe them.

For example:

그 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Do you want to see that movie?
무서운 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Do you want to see a scary movie?
무슨 영화를 보고 싶어요?
What movie do you want to see?

The dictionary form of 무슨 is 무슨. It looks like an adjective with ~ㄴ/은 attached, but the word itself is 무슨, so it is not actually an adjective. It looks and feels like one because of how it is used in sentences.

무슨 is used when the speaker does not know what an object is and is asking what it might be. The most common situations are these:

그것이 무슨 냄새야?
What is that smell?
무슨 생각(을) 해?
What are you thinking?
그것이 무슨 소리야?
What is that sound?
무슨 말이야?
What do you mean?

(The last two examples are often used to ask “what do you mean” or “I can’t understand what you are trying to say”)

One way to separate 무슨 from 어떤 and 어느 (coming next) is this: 무슨 is used when the speaker has no idea what the answer will be. The answer could be almost anything. For example:

무슨 냄새야?
What is that smell?

You might say this on walking into a room and smelling something for the first time. You do not know the source, so you have no idea what the answer might be. It could be anything from “my fart” to “the toaster.”

Likewise, if I ask:

무슨 생각(을) 해?
What are you thinking?

You might say this on seeing somebody staring into space. You cannot even guess what they are thinking, so you have no idea what the answer might be. It could be anything from “my future” to “eating pizza.”

Simple enough. Now for the other words often confused with 무슨.

5

Which(어떤/어느)

6

어떤

어떤 is another word that, although not an adjective, goes before a noun to describe it. For example:

그 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Do you want to see that movie?
무서운 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Do you want to see a scary movie?
무슨 영화를 보고 싶어요?
What movie do you want to see?
어떤 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Which movie do you want to see?

Or

그 차를 사고 싶어요?
Do you want to buy that car?
비싼 차를 사고 싶어요?
Do you want to buy an expensive car?
무슨 차를 사고 싶어요?
What car do you want to buy?
어떤 차를 사고 싶어요?
Which car do you want to buy?

The first question every learner asks about 어떤 is how it differs from 무슨. 어떤 usually translates to “which” and 무슨 to “what.” Their meanings are very similar, and the English translations give little hint of the difference.

The difference is subtle, and you do not need to fully grasp it yet. Even Koreans find it hard to explain, and swapping one for the other usually produces a sentence with essentially the same meaning. Here is a brief introduction to how they differ.

In questions, 어떤 is used for two main reasons.

  • To choose from a selection of options
    For example, in: “어떤 차를 사고 싶어요?” Maybe you are selling cars and giving a person a selection of cars to choose from. Here, although you technically don’t know the answer to the question, you can assume that it will be from a limited number of choices given.
  • To ask about the type of properties or characteristics related to a person/object
    For example, in: “어떤 차를 사고 싶어요?” Maybe you are asking your friend about the type of car he wants to buy based on the characteristics of it. Here, you could be asking if he wants to buy a van, an SUV, a truck, etc…

From those two uses you can see why 어떤 should not replace 무슨 in most situations. For example, the 무슨 examples earlier included this sentence:

무슨 냄새야?
What is that smell?

“무슨 냄새야?” is used when the person does not know the source of the smell and has no idea what it could be. The answer would likely name the source, anything from “farts” to “the toaster.”

Saying “어떤 냄새야?” would be odd in most situations. It only really works if you are asking someone to describe the characteristics of a smell. Imagine holding a bouquet of flowers to your friend’s nose: you want them to describe the smell. “Is it a nice smell? A fresh smell? A rosy smell?” The answer would describe the smell's characteristics.

Despite this distinction, 어떤 and 무슨 are basically interchangeable in many situations. Ask a Korean to describe the difference between these two sentences:

무슨 영화를 보고 싶어요?
What movie do you want to see?

(In theory, the speaker would have no idea what the answer to the question would be)

어떤 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Which movie do you want to see?

(In theory, the speaker would have given the listener a choice to choose from, or would be asking about the type of movie the listener wants to see [for example, a comedy, a horror movie, or a love story]).

Another use of 어떤, unrelated to questions, appears in this sentence:

어떤 남자는 어제 여기에 왔어
Some man came here yesterday

Here 어떤 shows that the speaker knows a man came but is unsure exactly who he was. This works with other nouns too, when the speaker is aware of some object but unsure which specific one it is. For example

저는 어떤 책을 읽고 있었어요
I was reading some book

(the speaker doesn’t know exactly what book he was reading)

저는 어떤 건물에 들어갔어요
I went into some building

(the speaker doesn’t know exactly what building he went into)

7

어느

Another way to ask this type of question is with 어느. Like 무슨 and 어떤, 어느 goes immediately before a noun.

Unfortunately, 어느 also translates to “which.” For example:

그 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Do you want to see that movie?
무서운 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Do you want to see a scary movie?
무슨 영화를 보고 싶어요?
What movie do you want to see?
어떤 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Which movie do you want to see?
어느 영화를 보고 싶어요?
Which movie do you want to see?

In questions, 어느 is used like the first use of 어떤 above: “to choose from a selection of options.” It is not used to ask about the characteristics or properties of an object, and not used to refer to something unknown.

So the uses of 무슨, 어떤 and 어느 overlap on several levels. This overlap is confusing, but it also means the words are interchangeable in most cases, which makes them easier to use than you might think. It is just as important to know how not to use each word in a question. In brief:

무슨:
Don’t use this when asking about the type of characteristics or properties of something
Don’t use this when giving somebody options to choose from

However,
Do use this when you can’t expect what the answer will be at all

어떤:
Don’t use this when asking about something that you can’t expect the answer for

However,
Do use this when asking about the type of characteristics or properties of something
Do use this when giving somebody options to choose from

어느:
Don’t use this when asking about the type of characteristics or properties of something
Don’t use this when asking about something that you can’t expect the answer for

However,
Do use this when giving somebody options to choose from

Here are similar examples with each word:

무슨 집에서 살아요?
What house do you live in?
어떤 집에서 살아요?
Which house do you live in?
어느 집에서 살아요?
Which house do you live in?
무슨 대학교를 다녀요?
What university do you go to?
어떤 대학교를 다녀요?
Which university do you go to?
어느 대학교를 다녀요?
Which university do you go to?

무슨 쪽으로 가고 싶어요? – This would generally not be said in Korean. You would say this standing on a street (or somewhere similar) with a selection of options to choose from, so one of the following would be used instead:

어떤 쪽으로 가고 싶어요?
Which way do you want to go?
어느 쪽으로 가고 싶어요?
Which way do you want to go?
무슨 치마를 샀어요?
What skirt did you buy?
어떤 치마를 샀어요?
Which skirt did you buy?
어느 치마를 샀어요?
Which skirt did you buy?

8

How many(몇)___ (words with counters)

Before asking “how many ____?” (as in “how many cars do you have?”), review how to say “I have # cars.” Remember that these sentences need counters:

저는 차 두 대가 있어요
I have 2 cars
나는 어제 친구 다섯 명을 만났어
I met five friends yesterday
저는 그 영화를 다섯 번 봤어요
I saw that movie five times

To ask “how many ___?”, put “몇” before the counter:

차가 몇 대 있어요?
How many cars do you have?
친구를 몇 명 만났어요?
How many friends did you meet?
어제 학교에 몇 번 갔어요?
How many times did you go to school yesterday?
파일을 몇 개 보냈어요?
How many files did you send?
치마를 몇 개 샀어요?
How many skirts did you buy?
그 종이를 몇 번 찢었어요?
How many times did you rip that paper?

Notice where the particles go. “몇 번,” “몇 대” or “몇 명” essentially form a question word, and like most question words they usually take no particles. Putting particles after “몇 ___” would still be understood, but it is more natural to put them after the noun you are asking about, not after the counter. These would be understandable, and it mostly depends on the speaker:

차 몇 대가 있어요?
How many cars do you have?
친구 몇 명을 만났어요?
How many friends did you meet?

Do not confuse these with the similar sentences you learned in an earlier lesson:

(저는) 차 몇 대가 있어요
I have some cars
(저는) 어제 학교에 몇 번 갔어요
I went to school some times (a few times) yesterday
(저는) 친구를 몇 명 만났어요
I met some friends

The first three are questions; the last three are statements. Remember that a question needs rising intonation at the end.

With 몇 ___ you can also ask “how old are you?” and “what time is it?”:

몇 시예요?
What time is it?
몇 살이에요?
How old are you?
너의 남동생은 몇 살이야?
How old is your younger brother?
9

How much, How many:얼마나

“얼마나” goes before adjectives and adverbs to mean “how.” The speaker is asking to what extent something occurs. For example:

Adjectives

얼마나 짧다
how short
얼마나 예쁘다
how pretty
얼마나 많다
how much/many

Adverbs

얼마나 자주
how often
얼마나 빨리
how quickly
얼마나 잘
how well

For example:

한국어를 얼마나 자주 공부해요?
How often do you study Korean?
여자 친구는 얼마나 예뻐요?
How pretty is your girlfriend?
축구를 얼마나 잘 해요?
How well do you play soccer?

It can also go before verbs when the speaker is not distinguishing countable objects; the question falls on an uncountable continuum. Notice the difference between these two questions:

How many pieces did you eat?
Speaker is making a distinction between countable objects

How much did you eat?
Speaker is not making a distinction between countable objects. Here, the speaker’s inquiry falls within an uncountable continuum.

Used this way with a verb, “얼마나” usually translates to “how much.” For example:

빵을 얼마나 먹었어요?
How much bread did you eat?
그 여자를 얼마나 사랑해요?
How much do you love that girl?
돈을 얼마나 가져갈 거예요?
How much money will you take?
물을 얼마나 마셨어요?
How much water did you drink?
그 책상은 자리를 얼마나 차지해요?
How much space does that desk take up?

The adverb 많이 is sometimes added to these sentences with essentially the same meaning; the translation usually does not change. For example:

빵을 얼마나 많이 먹었어요?
How much bread did you eat?
그 여자를 얼마나 많이 사랑해요?
How much do you love that girl?
돈을 얼마나 많이 가져갈 거예요?
How much money will you take?
물을 얼마나 많이 마셨어요?
How much water did you drink?
그 책상은 자리를 얼마나 많이 차지해요?
How much space does that desk take up?

Adding 많이 just stresses that the speaker knows the answer is “a lot” (remember, 많이 means “a lot” or “many”) and is asking “okay, I know it is a lot, but how much a lot?”

—————–

많이 (the adverb) can also appear as 많다 (the adjective) in these sentences. The meaning is the same, but the structure changes so that 많다 describes the noun in question. For example:

얼마나 많은 빵을 먹었어요?
How much bread did you eat?
얼마나 많은 돈을 가져갈 거예요?
How much money will you take?
얼마나 많은 물을 마셨어요?
How much water did you drink?

Although correct and understandable, avoid this style. “빵을 얼마나 먹었어요?” or “빵을 얼마나 많이 먹었어요?” sounds much more natural than “얼마나 많은 빵을 먹었어요?”

—————–

When the speaker is distinguishing countable objects, use the counter as in the previous section. For example:

빵을 몇 개 먹었어요?
How many pieces of bread did you eat?
몇 명의 여자를 사랑해요?
How many girls do you love?
Although “얼마나” translates to “how” in all of these usages, this is different from 어떻게, which also translates to “how” in English.

Notice the difference in the use of “how” in the sentences below:

한국어를 얼마나 자주 공부해요?
How often do you study Korean?
한국어를 어떻게 배웠어요?
How did you learn Korean?

“얼마” is used to ask how much something costs.
얼마나 and 얼마 are technically not the same word, but 얼마 is presented here because it is very common and takes the form of a question.

For example:

그 로션이 얼마예요?
How much is that lotion?
저 바지가 얼마예요?
How much are those pants?
벌금은 얼마였어요?
How much was the fine?
이게 얼마예요?
How much is this?
Remember, 이게, 그게 and 저게 are contractions of 이것이, 그것이 and 저것이 respectively.

———————-

Most of these question words can also be used on their own to ask for more information. If somebody is talking and you want to know who, what, when, where, why or how something happened, you can use 누구, 뭐, 언제, 어디, 왜 or 어떻게 by themselves as questions. In more formal situations you can add “요” after any of them. For example:

왜
In Lesson 21 you saw this used when somebody calls you, for example:

Person 1: 슬기야!
Seulgi!
Person 2: 왜?
Why/what do you want?

It can also be used alone to ask “why” something happens. For example:

Person 1: 저는 내일 캐나다에 갈 거예요
Tomorrow, I will go to Canada
Person 2: 왜?
Why (will you go to Canada tomorrow)?

언제

Person 1: 저는 캐나다에 갈 거예요
I will go to Canada
Person 2: 언제?
When (will you go to Canada)?

어디

Person 1: 저는 내일 갈 거예요
I will go tomorrow
Person 2: 어디?
Where (will you go tomorrow)?

누구

Person 1: 내일 그 사람을 만날 거예요
I will meet that person tomorrow
Person 2: 누구(를)?
Who(m) (will you meet)?

You can use 누가 alone to ask about the acting agent of a sentence. For example:

Person 1: 내일 어떤 사람이 여기 올 거예요
Tomorrow, some person will come here
Person 2: 누가?
Who (will come here)?

어떻게

Person 1: 저는 공항에 갈 거예요
I will go to the airport
Person 2: 어떻게?
How (will you go to the airport)?

뭐
You saw this already in this lesson:

Person 1: 저는 많이 먹었어요
I ate a lot
Person 2: 뭐(를)?
what? (you ate a lot of what?)

10

~는/은 and~이/가 Revisited (again)

Now that you know more grammar, and in particular how to ask questions, we can go further into the nuances of ~이/가 and ~는/은.

Unless the situation sets up a comparison between you and somebody else, it would be very awkward to say:

내가 밥을 먹었어

If you walked into a room with no prior conversation or back-story and simply said “내가 밥을 먹었어,” Koreans would understand you perfectly but find it very awkward.

Using ~이/가 instead of ~는/은 puts enormous stress on the fact that it was that particular subject who did the action. The speaker is not comparing anything, but specifically stressing that the subject does the action (or has the property described by the adjective, or “is” the thing attached to 이다). This is just a nuance and cannot be shown in an English translation; it can only be described.

“내가 밥을 먹었어” sounds strange because it is like walking into a room and saying:

“I am the one who ate rice!”
Note that “내가 밥을 먹었어” would still be translated “I ate,” not “I am the one who ate rice.” The latter is used here only to show how the stress falls on the subject.
Nobody would say that unprompted. Instead, you would just say:

나는 밥을 먹었어
I ate rice
Here, nothing is being compared, and no general fact is being stated. ~는/은 is simply marking the subject of the sentence. How can you tell that nothing is being compared, that this is not a general fact, and that ~는/은 is just a plain subject marker?

To repeat what Lesson 17 said:
This is exactly what confuses learners about ~이/가 and ~는/은. Both can express more than one nuance. The only way to tell which nuance is meant is to understand the situation in which they are used.

Telling ~이/가 from ~는/은 is confusing. Keep this phrase in mind as you learn these particles:
“It’s not about understanding them – it’s about understanding when to use them.”

Here is a closer look at this use of stressing the subject. As noted, it needs some back-story explaining why the speaker would need to stress the subject.

In most situations it would be odd to say this as a one-off sentence:

내가 학생이야
… this is like walking into a room and saying “It is me who is a/the student!”

But imagine two students arguing about who gets to sit in the front row (the best seats). After a few minutes, the class president can walk in and say:

내가 반장이야!
I am the class president! (반장 = class president)

Here the speaker is stressing that it is he or she who is the class president, and so has the power to settle the argument.

This use waited until Lesson 22 because now you know how to ask questions. ~이/가, as a subject stressor, is used when somebody specifically asks “who” did an action.

For example, in the following dialogue:

누가 밥을 먹었어?
Who ate the rice?
내가 밥을 먹었어
I ate the rice

This could also be shortened to just the subject:

내가
“I did”, or “me”

Another example:

누가 피자를 만들었어?
Who made the pizza?
내가 만들었어
I made it (me)

Note that a “who” question in English does not automatically require ~이/가 on the subject. It only applies when the question asks “who” the subject was, not who the object was (that is, when “who” translates to 누가 and not 누구”).

누가 그녀를 좋아해?
Who likes her?
내가 그녀를 좋아해
I like her

Above, the question asks who the subject is

너는 누구를 좋아해?
Who do you like?
나는 그녀를 좋아해
I like her

Above, the question asks who the object is. “는” on the subject takes the stress off the subject; “는” is just acting as a subject marker.

The same works with other question words, as long as the question asks for the subject. For example:

어떤 것이 더 좋아요?
Which one is better?
이것이 더 좋아요
This one is better

All of these nuances are situational.
A sentence with ~는/은 can make sense in one situation and be awkward in another. The same sentence with ~이/가 might be awkward in one situation and fine in another. In some situations they are exactly the same. Ask a Korean to distinguish these:

내 친구가 밥을 먹었다
내 친구는 밥을 먹었다

They will tell you that, depending on the situation, they could be exactly the same. It depends entirely on the nuance the speaker intends.

To finish, here is an attempt to sum up the uses of ~는/은 and ~이/가 very briefly. “Attempt,” because it is almost impossible to do in a few sentences; Koreans write doctoral theses on this topic. Nonetheless:

~는/은:

  • 1)    To denote a subject of a sentence. For example:
    나는 밥을 먹었다 = I ate rice
    내 친구는 밥을 먹었다 = My friend ate rice
    나는 그녀를 좋아해 = I like her
    This may or may not have an overlapped meaning with the following purposes:
  • 2)    To compare something. For example:
    이 산은 높다 = This mountain is high (but maybe another mountain is low)
    사과는 빨갛다. 바나나는 노랗다 = Apples are red. Bananas are yellow.
  • 3)    To state a general fact. For example:
    로키산은 높다 = The Rocky Mountains are high
    여름 날씨는 좋다 = Summer weather is good (nice)
    다이아몬드는 딱딱하다 = Diamonds are hard
    사과는 빨갛다 = Apples are red

~이/가:

  • 1)    To denote a subject of a sentence. For example:
    고양이가 집 뒤에 있다 = The cat is behind the house
    This may or may not have an overlapped meaning with the following purposes:
  • 2)    To indicate something based on a recent experience/observation. For example:
    날씨가 좋다 = The weather is nice!
    비가 와요 = It’s raining!
    사과가 파랗다 = The apple is blue
  • 3)    To stress that the subject does the action (or is the adjective). For example:
    누가 그녀를 좋아해? = Who likes her?
    – 내가 그녀를 좋아해 = I like her
    내가 반장이야! = I am the class president!
  • 4)    Placed on objects in sentences that are predicated by adjectives. For example:
    나는 학교가 싫다 = I don’t like school
    나는 그것이 좋다 = I like that
    나는 펜이 있다 = I have a pen
  • 5)    Placed on the object before 아니다 to indicate what something is not. For example:
    나는 학생이 아니다 = I am not a student
    나는 의사가 아니다 = I am not a doctor
  • 6)    Placed on the object predicated by “되다” to indicate what something becomes. For example:
    나는 의사가 되고 싶다 = I want to become a doctor
    나는 선생님이 되고 싶다 = I want to become a teacher

In Lesson 14 you also learned passive sentences with verbs that describe the “state” of something. For example:

문이 열려 있다
The door is open

That lesson said to use ~이/가 on the object that is in the state. The reason is most likely point 2) above: “the door being open” is not a general statement, since obviously not all doors are open. The speaker means a particular door in a particular situation, which calls for ~이/가. Adding ~는/은 to “문” (or any other object in that situation) would only be acceptable in a comparison.

Later lessons will keep showing you when ~는/은 or ~이/가 is more appropriate. For now, focus on what you have learned so far.
