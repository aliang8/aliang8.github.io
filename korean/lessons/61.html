Introduction

This lesson covers adding ~았/었으면 좋겠다 to the end of a sentence to mean “hope,” and the word “바라다,” which can also mean “hope.”

1

To hope:바라다

Let’s start with the peculiar word “바라다.” 바라다 indicates that one “hopes” something happens. In its simplest form it follows a noun, to say one “hopes” for that noun to occur. For example:

행복을 바랍니다
건강을 바랍니다
성공을 바랍니다
행운을 바랍니다

These constructions are hard to translate. Note that you would rarely say them aloud with 바라다. Outside of scripted, formal speech, you mostly see them as a kind of greeting in writing. Writing to someone to “wish them the best of luck,” you could say “행운을 바랍니다.” A greeting card that wants to say “I wish you happiness” could say “행복을 바랍니다.”

That said, it can be used in full sentences to say that one wants to “possess” or “gain” something. For example:

그 회사가 일본통화만 바라요
That company only wants Japanese currency

Native speakers often pronounce “바라요” as “바래요.”

There is no need for many examples of 바라다 used this way. It is more commonly used in a different way, shown below. These sentences also look odd to Korean speakers, who often pronounce “바라요” differently.

When ~아/어 (or a form built on it) is added to 바라다, it is often pronounced (and spelt) 바래. For example:

바래
바래요
바랬어
바랬어요

The governing body for the Korean language states that when a verb stem ends in ㅏ (as 바라다 does), 아 should be added (and usually merged) in these cases. It makes no exception for 바라다. So although “바래” and “바래요” are common in speech, writing, and even Korean songs, 바라 and 바라요 are correct.

To hope for a whole clause to happen (something ending in a verb or adjective), it is very common to attach ~기(를) to the clause’s final verb or adjective, turning it into a noun. For example:

아버님이 항상 행복하시기 바랍니다
I hope you (father) are always happy
저는 그 일을 잘 하기를 바라요
I hope I do that job well
숙제가 많지 않기를 바라요
I hope there isn’t a lot of homework
많은 사람들이 오시기를 바랍니다
I hope many people (many of you) come
제가 야간을 안 하기를 바라요
I hope I don’t work nights
정부가 그 정책을 수정하기를 바라요
I hope the government amends that policy
실내에서 신발을 안 신기를 바라요
I hope you don’t wear your shoes indoors (inside)
그 백조가 다시 헤엄치기를 바라요
I hope that swan can swim again
지금 만날 여자가 예쁘기를 바라요
I hope the girl I am meeting now is pretty
아빠가 곧 오기를 바라요
I hope dad comes home soon
그가 나에게 돈을 많이 주기를 바라
I hope he gives me a lot of money
지방을 많이 먹지 말고 단백질을 많이 먹기를 바랍니다
I hope you don’t eat a lot of fat, and (instead) eat a lot of protein

The sentences above with “바라요” and “바라” look unnatural to Korean people, who would pronounce them “바래요” and “바래.” So in speech, say “바래” or “바래요.” The recordings use “바래” and “바래요” because the speaker couldn’t make herself say “바라” and “바라요.”

The translations above use “hope” only because there is no good English equivalent for 바라다. As noted, 바라다 lets the speaker say that he or she wants the clause to happen. “Hope” isn’t required. Depending on the situation, the sentences above could be translated as a request from speaker to listener. For example:

많은 사람들이 오시기를 바랍니다
I hope many people (many of you) come

Imagine you are announcing a party to the people at your office. You want lots of people to come, so you could end with “많은 사람들이 오시기를 바랍니다.” This could be translated as “(Lots of people), please come (to my party).”

In Lesson 40 you learned to ask for something by adding ~아/어 주세요 to a verb. For example:

그 일을 해 주세요
Please do that for me
그 종이를 저한테 건네 주세요
Please hand that paper over to me

~기 바라다 can create essentially the same meaning. For example:

그 일을 하기 바랍니다
Please do that for me
그 종이를 저한테 건네기를 바랍니다
Please hand that paper over to me

Again, don’t be married to “hope” as the translation of 바라다. “Hope” would work above (“I hope that you do this for me”), but the translations match the ~아/어 주세요 versions to show that ~기 바라다 can be used to ask for something to be done.

Adding just ~기 바랍니다 to a “command” or “request” like this makes the speaker sound like an authority. A boss telling workers to finish their work might say “그 일을 하기 바랍니다.” The implication is that the work should be done, and the boss is ordering it.

You can remove that “authoritative” feel by adding 아/어 주다 after the verb you hope will be done. For example:

그 일을 해 주기 바랍니다
그 종이를 저한테 건네 주기를 바랍니다

To make those sentences more formal, the honorific “시” (Lesson 39) is often added to 주다:

그 일을 해 주시기 바랍니다
Please do that/I hope that you do that
그 종이를 저한테 건네 주시기 바랍니다
Please hand that paper over to me/I hope that you hand that paper over to me

A command given this way is very formal, and most often heard in overhead announcements telling large crowds what to do. On the Seoul subway, for example, the announcement might say:

지금은 서울 역. 서울역입니다. 공항철도나 KTX를 타시고 싶은 승객은 이 역에서 갈아타시기 바랍니다
This is Seoul Station. Riders wanting to take the Airport Railroad or the KTX, please transfer at this station.

If you need to tell somebody what to do, the ladder of politeness looks roughly like this:

지금 나가
지금 나가라
지금 나가세요
지금 나가기 바랍니다
지금 나가 주기 바랍니다
지금 나가십시오
지금 나가 주시기 바랍니다

All of the above could be translated as “Please go now.”

Here are more examples:

실내에서 운동을 하지 말아 주시기 바랍니다
Please don’t exercise indoors
이번 시험에 최선을 다해 주시기를 바랍니다
Please try your best on this exam
모든 학생들이 딱 12시에 도착해 주시기 바랍니다
All students please arrive at exactly 12:00
새로운 정책을 설명할 때 모두 다 잘 들어 주시기 바랍니다
When I’m explaining the new policy, everybody please listen well

Star Wars gives another good example of how hard “바라다” is to translate. Remember, “바라다” lets the speaker say that he or she wants the clause to happen. You probably know the line “May the force be with you.” With Korean subtitles, it becomes:

포스가 함께 하기를 바랍니다

Here “hope” isn’t in the English at all. But Obi-Wan uses this phrase to say he “wants” the force to be with Luke.

There is another way to say “I hope” in Korean, covered next.

2

I hope/I wish:~았/었으면 좋겠다

Adding “~았/었으면 좋겠다” to the end of a verb or adjective creates the meaning “I hope” or “I wish.” Treat the whole thing as one unit rather than breaking it into pieces. One simple example, then some discussion:

비가 안 왔으면 좋겠어요
I hope it doesn’t rain

Notice the past tense conjugation before “~면” and the future tense conjugation on “좋다.” Despite those tenses, the speaker is hoping right now for that situation to occur, whether in the present or the future. More examples:

양이 많았으면 좋겠어요
I hope there is a lot (I hope the amount is a lot)
내일 일이 없었으면 좋겠어요
I wish I didn’t have work tomorrow
그 말을 사전에 했으면 좋겠어요
I wish you had told me that beforehand
나를 무조건 사랑했으면 좋겠어
I wish you loved me unconditionally (no matter what)
삶을 한가롭게 살았으면 좋겠어
I wish I could live life freely/leisurely
집에 가서 집이 청결했으면 좋겠어요
I hope the house will be clean when I go home
엄마가 저를 데리러 왔으면 좋겠어요
I wish mom would come to pick me up
우리는 그 여자랑 같이 갔으면 좋겠어요
I hope we go together with that girl
그것 때문에 나를 해고하지 않았으면 좋겠다
I wish/hope you don’t fire me because of that
저를 의도적으로 피하려고 안 했으면 좋겠어요
I wish you didn’t deliberately try to avoid me
내일 경기에서 우리 팀 모두가 최선을 다하면 좋겠어
Tomorrow, I hope everybody on our team tries their best

You may also see other words (usually 하다 or 바라다) in place of 좋다. For example:

우리가 곧 만났으면 해

우리가 곧 만났으면 바래요
I wish we could meet soon, it would be nice if we could meet soon

~(으)면 can also attach to a word without the past tense conjugation. For example:

비가 안 왔으면 좋겠어요

비가 안 오면 좋겠어요
I hope it doesn’t rain

Ask a Korean speaker and they will say the two examples above mean the same thing: both express a “hope” or “wish” in the present. There is no concrete published explanation of why. Korean speakers simply tend to use the first form (“~았/었으면”) for hopes and wishes.

———————–

What follows is one way of thinking about it, based on feel rather than any rule. Korean grammar teachers say both examples are identical, and that they would be more likely to say “ ~았/었으면”)

Think of “~았/었으면 좋겠다” as a grammatical principle in its own right, one that can’t be split into pieces. The whole construction expresses a “wish.” For example:

비가 안 왔으면 좋겠어요
I hope it doesn’t rain

~(으)면 좋겠다, on the other hand, can be split into its grammatical pieces: ~(으)면 meaning “if” or “when” (from Lesson 43), followed by “좋겠다” meaning “it will be good.” These examples can be read as the sum of their parts. For example:

비가 안 오면 좋겠어요
It would be good if it doesn’t rain

Either way, both sentences (in English and Korean) express the same meaning, whether you treat “~았/었으면” as a single unit or read “~(으)면 좋겠다” as separate pieces.

Anyways, that is just my observation.
