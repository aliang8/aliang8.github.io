Introduction

In this lesson you will learn how to end sentences with ~잖아 or ~잖아요. It is a very common ending in spoken Korean, but English speakers often struggle with it because it is hard to translate directly. The many examples here describe the specific situations where it fits.

1

The meaning of~잖아(요)

Adding ~잖아 or ~잖아요 to the word at the end of a sentence is very common in Korean. Its meaning is hard to translate but easier to describe, so as you read the examples, pay more attention to the descriptions after each sentence than to the translations.

~잖아 or ~잖아요 attaches to verbs, adjectives or 이다 at the end of a sentence. It loosely means “as you know/you know/don’t forget that…” You use it when the listener already knows (or should know) the fact you are stating, and you are pointing out that they know (or should know) it. A common word you will see with ~잖아 or ~잖아요 is 맞다. For example:

맞잖아요!
I told you it was correct! You should have known that this was correct!

Description of when this would be used:

Imagine you are with a friend and somebody asks you both what the capital of Canada is. You say Ottawa; your friend says Vancouver. After some debate, the person who asked confirms it is Ottawa. You could say to your friend: 오타와가 맞잖아! = I told you Ottawa was correct/You should have known that Ottawa was correct!

Similarly, if you know the capital of Canada is not Vancouver, and your friend should have known that too, you could say:

밴쿠버 아니잖아!
It’s not Vancouver! You should know that it is not Vancouver!

————————-

Here are more examples of ~잖아(요). To help you get the hang of it, each sentence comes with a detailed description of the situation where it would be used:

내일은 토요일이잖아요! 그래서 우리가 일찍 가야 될 것 같아요
You should know that tomorrow is Saturday, so we should probably go early!

Description of when this would be used:

You and a friend are deciding what time to go to the park tomorrow. Tomorrow is Saturday, so you know the park will probably be crowded. Your friend should know this too, so you use ~잖아(요) to mark it as information they already have: “don’t forget/you should know that tomorrow is Saturday! So we should go early.”

 ————————-

나는 돈이 없잖아!
Don’t forget that I don’t have any money/you should know that I don’t have any money

Description of when this would be used:

You are out with your girlfriend but have no money because you forgot your wallet, and you have told her so. Later she asks you to buy her a coffee. You can answer “Don’t forget that I don’t have any money/you should know that I don’t have any money!” Since you already told her, she should know.

————————-

윌리가 배고프잖아!
Willy is hungry!/You should know that Willy is hungry/Don’t forget that Willy is hungry!

Description of when this would be used:

A real example: at his in-laws’ house, a man named Willy was discussing lunch with his wife’s parents. His mother-in-law suggested making something small, and his father-in-law replied “윌리가 배고프잖아!”

Again, the key point is that the listener should already know the fact being stated. Willy is always hungry, and the father-in-law was reminding his wife: “don’t forget, Willy eats a lot and will be hungry if we cook something small!”

 ————————-

~잖아(요) is also common when telling a longer story. In any language, people often give some back-story that is common knowledge before getting to the actual story. For example:

제가 어제 회사에서 조금 아팠잖아요…… 그래서 집에 가서 밥을 먹고 갑자기 토했어요~ 그래서 병원에 갔어요.

You are at work the day after being sick. A co-worker heard you went to the hospital last night and asks why. You answer “remember/don’t forget/as you know I was sick at work yesterday… Then I went home and ate, suddenly threw up, and went to the hospital.” Here too, the listener should know you were sick yesterday.

————————-

선생님이 왜 화가 났냐고? 선생님에게 말대답을 했잖아! 선생님에게 그렇게 맞서면 당연히 선생님이 화가 나실 거예요
Did you just ask why the teacher was mad? You should know that (it is because) you talked back to him! If you oppose the teacher like that, obviously he is going to get mad

Description of when this would be used:

Your friend tells you he got detention at school and says he does not understand why. You can tell him “you talked back to the teacher!” He should know this, since he did it. You point that out by saying “선생님에게 말대답을 했잖아.”

————————-

수료증을 받고 싶으면 수료를 해야 돼요. 근데 당신이 수료를 아직 안 했잖아요
If you want to get the class certification, you need to finish the class. But, you should know that you didn’t finish the class yet

Description of when this would be used:

You are asking for a certificate for a class. The person in the administration office is explaining that you need to finish the class first. They can say “you should know that you didn’t finish the class.”

————————-

그거를 거기다가 버리지 마! 플라스틱을 재활용해야 되잖아
Don’t throw that (out) there! You should know that we have to recycle plastics.

Description of when this would be used:

Your son throws a plastic bottle in the regular garbage. You have taught him many times that recycling matters, so he should know to use the recycling bin. You could say “you should know that we/you should recycle plastic.”

————————-

A few more things to know before we finish:

No further grammatical principles are ever attached after 잖아(요), so you will always find it at the very end of a sentence.

Native speakers often feel that ~잖아(요) can sound rude or sharp, almost like “I TOLD you it was like that!” In practice you will hear people use ~잖아(요) all the time without sounding angry. Still, be cautious about using it with an elder or someone who deserves high respect.

In a recent lesson you learned ~알다시피, meaning “as you know.” ~알다시피 and ~잖아(요) are used differently, but their meanings are quite similar. So if you want to use “~잖아(요)” with an elder, consider ~알다시피 first. For example, if your boss asked why you were late yesterday, you could say:

제가 어제 아팠잖아요!
Don’t forget/You should know that I was sick yesterday

But that sentence asserts, in a way, that your boss should know you were sick, which could sound very rude. Instead, you could say:

부장님도 아시다시피 저는 어제 아파서 회사에 늦게 왔어요
As you know, I was sick yesterday, so I was late

This version sounds much more polite and probably would not get you in trouble.

————————

~잖아(요) is also commonly attached to 있다. Do not read this as the meanings of “있다” and “잖아(요)” combined. Treat “있잖아” or “있잖아요” as one unit. “있잖아” or “ 있잖아요” often opens a sentence when somebody is gathering their thoughts or signaling that they have something to say. For example:

있잖아… 오늘은 재활용을 해야 되는 날이야
So, you know,… Today is the day to do recycling
