Introduction

In this lesson you will learn the uses of ~다가, which usually sits between two clauses. It has several meanings and is easy to confuse with other grammatical principles that mean something similar.

1

For one action to halt, and for another action to begin:~다가

If you have met ~다가 before, you probably found it hard to pin down. The difficulty comes from two sides: it looks like it does the same job as other grammatical principles, and ~다가 itself can create different meanings.

The good news is that ~다가 is usually easy to understand when you hear or read it, so you can get a feel for its meaning before you start using it yourself. Rather than building your own sentences with ~다가 right away, listen for it in conversation and look for it in your reading first.

Good explanations of the specific meaning(s) of ~다가 are hard to find, so this lesson tries to lay them out as clearly as possible.

~다가 expresses that one action happens after another. On the surface that sounds like many other grammatical principles (for example ~고, ~아/어서 or ~는데). Two simple examples will start to show how ~다가 is different:

학생은 열심히 공부하다가 잠이 들었어요
The student studied hard and then fell asleep
요리를 하다가 주방에서 불이 나서 급하게 가스레인지를 껐어요
I was cooking, and then a fire started in the kitchen so I urgently turned off the stove

When the verb before ~다가 is in the present tense (as in the two examples above), the first action was halted, or cut short, and the subject then does the action in the next clause. Look at those two sentences again:

학생은 열심히 공부하다가 잠이 들었어요
Here, the student was studying. The studying was cut short because he fell asleep.

요리를 하다가 주방에서 불이 나서 급하게 가스레인지를 껐어요
Here, I was cooking. The cooking was cut short because a fire started in the kitchen and I turned off the stove.

The examples above attach ~다가 to verbs, but it can attach to adjectives too. For example:

날씨가 춥다가 갑자기 더워졌어요
The weather was cold, and then suddenly became hot

When the first clause is in the present tense (as above), the first action is halted and so does not finish. The result of completing that action is therefore usually not stated in the next clause. Instead, the next clause usually states the action that caused the first action to halt.

A common English translation for these sentences is “while.” Be careful: “while” has several meanings in English, and one of them is two actions moving along together (that is the meaning of ~(으)면서 from Lesson 62). With ~다가, “while” one action is going on, something else happens that stops it. Here are many more examples:

침실에서 자다가 갑자기 돌아가셨어요
While sleeping in the bedroom, he suddenly passed away

돌아가시다 is a formal way to say somebody dies, similar to “passed away” in English.

열심히 운동하다가 갑자기 쓰러졌어요
While exercising hard, suddenly I collapsed
제가 집을 청소하다가 잃어버린 열쇠를 찾았어요
While I was cleaning the house, I found the key that I lost
과학 천재가 실험을 하다가 실수로 집을 폭발시켰어요
While the science genius was doing an experiment, he accidently blew up his house
재시험을 준비하다가 몸이 안 좋아져서 시험을 포기했어요
While studying for the retest, my body/health became bad so I gave up (studying for) the exam
학생은 열심히 공부하다가 친구가 놀러 와서 친구랑 수다를 떨었어요
While the student was studying hard, his friend came (to play) so (he stopped studying and) they started chatting
회장이 직원들을 월급을 지급하다가 시스템이 고장이 나서 다 못 했어요
While the chairman was paying the employees, the system broke so he couldn’t do it all
사무실에서 일하다가 회장님이 들어와서 모든 직원들이 인사하려고 일어났어요
While working at the office, the chairman/boss came in so all of the workers (stopped working and) got up to greet him

You can add ~았/었 to the word before ~다가. Then the action before ~았/었다가 finished before the action in the next clause started, and the next clause usually states the end result of completing that action. Let’s take four actions from the ~다가 sentences above and build sentences with ~았/었다가 using the same actions:

학생이 열심히 공부하다가 잠이 들었어요
The student studied hard and then fell asleep

Here, the student was studying. The studying was cut short because he fell asleep.

학생이 열심히 공부했다가 친구를 잘 못 만나서 나쁜 학생이 되었어요
The student studied hard, and then met bad friends and then became a bad student

Here, the student finished studying before the next action, “becoming a bad student,” takes place. “The student studying hard” was completed, and “becoming a bad student” is the end result.

I was cooking, and then a fire started in the kitchen, so I urgently turned off the stove

Here, I was cooking. The cooking was cut short because a fire started in the kitchen and I turned off the stove.

요리를 했다가 먹을 사람이 없어서 다 버렸어요

Here, I finished cooking before the next action, “throwing it away,” takes place. “Cooking” was completed, and “throwing away the food” is the end result.

————————

To see the difference between ~다가 and ~았/었다가, let’s look at them with 가다 and 오다. Compare these two sentences:

1) 저는 학교에 가다가 친구 집에 갔어요
2) 저는 학교에 갔다가 친구 집에 갔어요

In only one of these sentences did I go to school.

1) In the first sentence, “going to school” did not finish. While I was going to school, the action was cut short and I went to a friend’s house.

2) In the second sentence, “going to school” finished. I went to school, and after that went to my friend’s house.

We can do the same with 오다. Notice how the endings of these sentences differ because of the context that 오다가 or 왔다가 creates:

그 사람들이 가게에 오다가 버스가 고장 나서 돌아갔어요
While those people were coming to the store (while they were on their way), their bus broke down so they went back
그 사람들이 가게에 아침에 왔다가 급히 떠났어요
Those people came to the store in the morning and left quickly

————————

~다가 is commonly attached to 있다. “있다가” is almost the same as “later,” because it literally implies “after we are here” or “after this.” It can stand alone or appear inside a sentence. 조금 often comes before 있다가 to mean “just a little bit later.” For example:

Person 1: 언제 가고 싶어요?
When do you want to go?
Person 2: 있다가
Later

저는 조금 있다가 해산물을 먹으러 갈 거예요

A time frame can also go before “있다가” to say how much later. For example:

10분 있다가
10 minutes later
1년 있다가
1 year later

These can be used inside sentences. For example:

10분 있다가 나가자!
Let’s leave in 10 minutes!
우리는 아마 1년 있다가 결혼할 것 같아요
We will probably get married in a year

———————————-

To sharpen your understanding of ~다가, let’s look at some common mistakes with it.

When ~다가 is attached to a present-tense verb, that action is halted (not finished) because something unexpected comes up. So ~다가 is wrong if the action finishes as planned. For example, these would be incorrect:

재료를 다 섞다가 드세요
재료를 다 섞다가 10분 동안 비비세요

In these sentences the speaker gives a command to do something after the ingredients have been properly mixed. In both cases ~고 or ~아/어서 would be more appropriate. For example:

재료를 다 섞고 드세요
재료를 다 섞어서 드세요
재료를 다 섞고 10분 동안 비비세요
재료를 다 섞어서 10분 동안 비비세요

To use the first clause “재료를 다 섞다가” correctly, we need some action that halts or interrupts the mixing. For example:

재료를 다 섞다가 거품이 생겼어요
While mixing all the ingredients, bubbles formed
재료를 다 섞다가 떡의 형태가 변했어요
While mixing all the ingredients, the shape/form of the rice cakes changed

The two sentences above could be correct, but they would most likely be answers to a question like “why did you stop mixing the ingredients?” When you use ~다가, the final clause usually says what the subject does that interrupts the first action. In these two sentences, context lets us assume the speaker stopped mixing, so it does not have to be stated.

But if you were simply telling a story with no prior context, it would be better to say what the subject does that interrupts the first action. For example:

재료를 다 섞다가 거품이 생겨서 멈췄어요
While mixing all the ingredients, bubbles formed so I stopped
재료를 다 섞다가 떡의 형태가 변하면 드시면 됩니다
While mixing all the ingredients, if the shape/form of the rice cakes changes, you can eat it

Notice that the underlined actions are done by the same person doing the action attached to ~다가. Sentences with ~다가 usually state what the subject does that interrupts the first action. With context you can leave this out, but without context it is usually included.

Let’s move on to another usage of ~다가.

2

The second action is caused by the first action:~다가

This usage of ~다가 is very close to the grammatical principles that mean “because,” such as ~아/어서, ~기 때문에 or ~(으)니까.

The difference is that with ~다가 there is often no direct link explaining why the first clause causes the second. Instead, the idea is that while the subject was doing the first action, something about that action made the second action happen.

For example:

제가 빨리 걷다가 발가락을 부딪혔어요
I was walking fast and then (because I was walking fast) stubbed my toe

Notice the very subtle difference between that sentence and this one:

제가 빨리 걸어서 발가락을 부딪혔어요
Because I was walking fast I stubbed my toe

In the second example, the only reason you stubbed your toe is that you were walking too fast; the two are directly connected. In the first example, you were walking fast, and while you were doing that, something about it caused you to stub your toe. We will come back to this ambiguity below, but first some more examples:

눈길을 걷다가 넘어졌어요
I was walking on a snowy road, and then – something about walking on a snowy road caused me to fall
친구만 믿다가 결국 가족을 잃었어요
I only trusted my friend, and then – something about only trusting my friend caused me to eventually lose my family
해물만 먹다가 식중독에 걸려서 병원에 갔어요
I only ate seafood, and then – something about only eating seafood caused me to get food poisoning and go to the hospital
우리가 계속 얘기하다가 선생님의 말씀을 못 들었어요
We were talking, and then – something about us continually talking caused us to not hear what the teacher said

——————————

Here is the tricky part of ~다가: it can be hard to tell which meaning is intended. Take the example from the start of the lesson:

학생은 열심히 공부하다가 잠이 들었어요

This could have two meanings. Based on what you have learned, they would be:

학생은 열심히 공부하다가 잠이 들었어요
The student studied hard and then – something about him studying hard caused him to fall asleep

Or

학생은 열심히 공부하다가 잠이 들었어요
While the student was studying hard, he fell asleep

How do we tell them apart? Look at both English sentences. The translations differ slightly, but is the meaning really that different? In the first, the student studies hard and then falls asleep. In the second, the student studies hard and, while studying, falls asleep.

Are they really that different? Learners try to separate these cases, but they do not need to be separated. As you see more and more in your studies, meaning depends heavily on context. Use the context of the sentence to clarify the meaning.

3

Adding~다가 to~에

Besides the two meanings above, you will often hear “~다가” attached to “~에” when referring to a place. For example:

밥을 냉장고에 넣어
Put the rice in the fridge
밥을 냉장고에다가 넣어
Put the rice in the fridge
유리에 붙여 주세요
Please stick it on the glass
유리에다가 붙여 주세요
Please stick it on the glass
재료를 그릇에 섞어
Mix the ingredients in the bowl
재료를 그릇에다가 섞어
Mix the ingredients in the bowl
돈을 탁자에 놓았어요
I put the money on the table
돈을 탁자에다가 놓았어요
I put the money on the table
펜을 어디에 두었어요?
Where did you put/leave the pen?
펜을 어디에다가 두었어요?
Where did you put/leave the pen?
이불을 다 침실에 넣었어요
I put the blankets in the bedroom
이불을 다 침실에다가 넣었어요
I put the blankets in the bedroom
팔에 로션을 살짝 발랐어요
I applied a little bit of lotion (slightly) to my arm
팔에다가 로션을 살짝 발랐어요
I applied a little bit of lotion (slightly) to my arm
의자를 방 한가운데에 두고 와요
Put the chair in the middle of the room and come (back)
의자를 방 한가운데에다가 두고 와요
Put the chair in the middle of the room and come (back)

Korean speakers would say the sentences above with ~에 and ~에다가 sound identical and mean the same thing.

———————————-

Since ~에 is often dropped from words like “여기,” “저기,” “거기” and “어디,” ~다가 is often added directly to those words. Here they are in sentences:

밥을 여기다가 넣어
Put the rice here
거기다가 붙여 주세요
Please stick it there
이불을 다 저기다가 넣었어요
I put the blankets there
펜을 어디다가 두었어요?
Where did you put/leave the pen?

———————————-

~에다가 is not used when somebody goes to a place. For example:

“나는 집에 갔어요” is natural, but “나는 집에다가 갔어요” is unnatural.

———————————-

Although ~에 and ~에다가 can mean the same thing, ~에다가 is more likely when one thing is being added to something else. For example:

저는 샌드위치에다가 김치를 넣었어요
I put kimchi in the sandwich

(~에다가 implies that there is already other stuff in the sandwich, and you are adding more to it.)

커피에다가 설탕을 넣었어요
I put sugar in my coffee

(~에다가 implies that something was already there [the coffee], and you are putting more [the sugar] into it.)

A real-life example: at a school cafeteria, an 아주머니 puts fruit on each tray. If your tray is already full, you might ask her to put the fruit next to your rice, where there is a little space left. You could say:

여기에다가 두세요
Please, put it here

(implying that she is putting the fruit where there is already something)
