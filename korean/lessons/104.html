Introduction

This lesson is about the particle ~는/은. Yes, the same ~는/은 from Lesson 1, the one you have used in almost every Korean sentence since you started. So why is it back in Lesson 104?

Because ~는/은 is quite complicated. Here you will revisit it and learn how to apply it in more complicated sentences.

1

Using~는/은 to state that a comparison has taken place

In Lesson 1, you learned to attach ~는/은 to a noun to mark it as the subject/topic of the sentence. Later, in Lesson 2, 17 22 and 24, you learned that ~는/은 can also be used to:

  • Indicate a subject of a sentence
  • Indicate a general fact
  • Indicate a comparison

This comparison function of ~는/은 is often added to other particles or grammatical principles to bring in that subtle meaning. Some examples will make this clear.

Adding ~는/은 to ~에 and ~에서
~는/은 can be added to the particles ~에 and ~에서 to indicate a comparison with some other situation. Look at this example:

한국에서는 사람들이 김치를 많이 먹어요
In Korea, people eat a lot of Kimchi

This sentence says that in Korea people eat a lot of kimchi. Built into ~는/은 here is a comparison with some other situation. Perhaps somebody had just said that people in some other country do not eat much kimchi. That prompts the speaker to say: (while people in other countries don’t eat a lot of kimchi) people in Korea do.

The role of ~는/은 is the part in parentheses. It compares something in the sentence to something else. That “something else” can be mentioned earlier in the conversation, or just implied.

Let’s look at another example:

이 가방에는 귀중품이 없어요
There are no valuables in this bag

This sentence says that in this bag there are no valuables. Again, ~는/은 carries a comparison with some other situation. Perhaps someone at the airport asked whether there were valuables in the bag about to be loaded onto the plane. You might answer “no, not in this bag, (but there are valuables in another bag).”

The role of ~는/은 is again the part in parentheses. It compares something in the sentence to something else, which can be stated earlier or implied.

Often the context is given in the same sentence, with ~는/은 attached to the situation being compared. For example:

그 가방에는 귀중품이 있는데 이 가방에는 귀중품이 없어요
There are valuables in this bag, but not in this bag

Let’s look at many more examples:

미국에는 일거리가 없어요
There are no jobs in America (compared to another country)
티켓을 매표소에서는 살 수 있어요
You can purchase tickets at the ticket office (compared to another place)
놀이동산에 여름에는 사람이 많아요
There are many people at the amusement park in the summer (compared to another season)
공원에서는 사람들이 축구를 할 수 없다
People can’t play soccer in the park
우리학교에서는 영어원어민 선생님 10분이 있습니다
At our school, there are 10 native English speakers
요즘에는 일거리가 없어 손을 놓고 있는 사람도 적지 않다
These days (compared to sometime before), there aren’t many places to work, so there are many people who don’t have a job
몇 달 전에 서른 살이 되어서 우울했는데 이제는 전혀 우울하지 않아요
A few months ago, I was depressed that I was going to be thirty years old, but now (compared to that time), I’m not depressed at all

Sometimes, though, the situation being compared is stated right inside the sentence. Here is a sentence from one of our Korean short stories for learners (Set 1, Story 4):

5년 전에 잡지 사는 게 돈 낭비라고 생각했는데, 요즘에는 잡지 읽을 때가 얼마나 좋은지 모른다

I would translate the sentence to:

5 years ago, I thought buying a magazine was a waste of money, but these days (compared to before) I can’t explain how good it is when I read a magazine.

Another scene, from a different story, is about a little girl who went to the zoo with her father.

She had a lot of fun, but wished her mother had come too. When she gets home, she says to her mother:

“나중에는 꼭 엄마도 같이 동물원에 가자! 나랑 약속해!”

This translates to: Next time (compared to this past time when you didn’t come), let’s go to the zoo together! Promise me!

Adding ~는/은 to 수
In Lesson 45, you learned about the noun 수 and how it is used with 있다 and 없다 to say that one is able or not able to do something. For example:

밥을 먹을 수 있어요
I can eat rice
밥을 먹을 수 없어요
I can’t eat rice

You can attach ~는 to 수 to indicate that the situation that “can happen” is being compared with some other situation that “cannot happen” (or vice versa). For example:

밥을 먹을 수 있어요
I can eat rice

This is a simple sentence; nothing is being compared.

밥을 먹을 수는 있어요
I can eat rice

Here the speaker can eat rice, but not something else. What that other food is stays ambiguous because no context is given, but in a real conversation the speaker likely described some other food he or she cannot eat. Likewise:

밥을 먹을 수는 없어요
I cannot eat rice

Here the speaker cannot eat rice, but can eat something else. Again the other food is ambiguous without context, but in a real conversation the speaker likely mentioned some food he or she can eat. Here are some other examples:

선풍기를 틀 수 있어요
I can turn on the fan
(에어컨을 틀 수 없지만) 선풍기를 틀 수는 있어요
I can turn on the fan (but not the air conditioner)
이 쪽으로 들어갈 수 있어요
You can go in through this way
(문으로 들어갈 수 없지만) 이 쪽으로 들어갈 수는 있어요
You can’t go in through the door, but you can go in through this way)

In the two examples above, the first “수” is theoretically being compared with the second “수.”

So it is also acceptable to attach ~는 to the first 수 in the sentence. For example:

에어컨을 틀 수는 없지만 선풍기를 틀 수는 있어요
You can’t turn the air conditioner on, but you can turn the fan on
문으로 들어갈 수는 없지만 이 쪽으로 들어갈 수는 있어요
You can’t go out that door, but you can go out this way

Knowing how versatile ~는/은 is, you can see how it attaches to other nouns like 수 that have a special place in sentences. Below are the resulting sentences and meanings when it is added to 적, 필요 and 줄:

망가진 컴퓨터를 고치려고 컴퓨터 전문가에 맡긴 적은 없어요
I haven’t yet taken my broken computer to a specialist to get it fixed
그 고기 부위를 사려고 고기 전문점을 갈 필요는 없어요
In order to purchase that type of meat, you don’t need to go to a specialty store
이력서를 작성을 했는데 온라인으로 제출을 할 줄은 몰라요
I wrote up a resume, but I don’t know how to apply online

The same versatility lets ~는/은 attach to many other grammatical principles. Below are the resulting sentences and meanings when it is added to:

~(로)부터
~까지
~(으)로는
~(으)면
~지는 말다

일이 다 끝나고 어머니로부터는 연락이 없었다
After work, there was no contact (I wasn’t contacted) by my mother (implied here is that the person was contacted by other people, but not his mother)
시험 전 날까지는 밤을 샜는데 당일에는 일찍 잤어요
I stayed up all night until the day of the test, but on the day of the test I went to sleep early
정부에서 주는 장학금으로는 대학교를 졸업할 수 없었어요

2

Adding~는 to~지 않다 (~지는 않다/~진 않다)

This usage of ~는/은 is often placed after “~지” in “~지 않다.” For example, instead of saying:

시간이 오래 걸리지 않을 것이에요
It won’t take a long time
시간이 오래 걸리지는 않을 것이에요
It won’t take a long time

~는/은 here indicates that the situation differs from some expectation. In the sentence above, the listener for some reason expects it to take a long time. By using ~는/은, the speaker says “(despite what you expect,) it won’t take long.”

~지는 is almost always contracted to ~진, as taught in Lesson 101.

Another example:
저는 요리를 잘 하진 않습니다

Imagine you are in a cooking class, so the listener expects you to be good at cooking. By saying “저는 요리를 잘 하진 않습니다,” you are saying “(despite what you expect,) I am not good at cooking.”

This usage of ~는/은 is best understood through many examples, so here are more.

Picture this: a patient has had a weird feeling in his esophagus for a few weeks and goes to the doctor. The doctor says it is nothing to worry about and that he knows exactly what it is. The patient, still skeptical, asks:

“Do a lot of people complain of this feeling?”

He asked because the doctor made it seem like he had seen this a thousand times before. The answer was:

그런 사람이 많진 않아요

Again, ~지는 (or ~진) marks that the fact goes against expectations. The doctor had made the patient assume many people had this feeling, so when asked “do many people complain of this feeling?” he responded with:

그런 사람이 많진 않아요
(despite what you expect,) there aren’t that many people

Here are more examples:

그 가수가 인기가 많은데 티켓이 아직 매진되진 않았어요
That singer is very popular but the tickets still haven’t haven’t sold out
일반 자리가 없다고 노약자 좌석에 앉는 사람이 많진 않아요
There aren’t many people who would sit in the seats for the old and weak when there are no regular seats available
네, 그 상황을 직접 목격했는데 10년 전이라서 잘 기억나진 않아요
Yes, I witnessed that situation/event directly, but it was ten years ago so I don’t remember it well
그 사람의 전문이 화학이라서 생물도 잘 아실 줄 알았는데 그렇진 않아요
That person’s specialty is Chemistry so I thought he would be good at Biology as well but he is not
3

Downplaying a Fact:~긴 하다

Attaching ~는 to ~기 after a verb or adjective creates a special meaning that is hard to pin down. The speaker is downplaying the situation stated in the clause. How it is downplayed depends on context; sometimes the speaker is simply being modest.

After ~는, you can finish the sentence either with 하다 or by repeating the same verb or adjective that was attached to ~기. For example:

~기는 is almost always contracted to ~긴, as taught in Lesson 101.

생각이 있긴 있어
I have an idea, or
생각이 있긴 해
I have an idea

An example from a TV show: a group of people were discussing how to get their toy back from the neighbor’s yard after it flew over the fence. One of them said “생각이 있긴 있어”. Compared to plain “생각이 있어”, this suggests the person thinks his idea is not very good, or is too shy to share it. Either way, it downplays what he is about to say.

Another example:

하고 싶은 말이 있긴 있어요, or

하고 싶은 말이 있긴 해요
I have something I want to say (but it might not be that good, or there is some other reason that is preventing me from wanting to say what I want to say)

날씨가 춥긴 추워요, or

날씨가 춥긴 해요
It’s cold (but it’s not that cold… it’s just a little bit cold)
공부를 열심히 하긴 했어
I studied hard (but not that hard)

Because this principle downplays what you are saying, it is very common to connect the next clause with ~지만 to show what happens as a result. For example:

돈이 있긴 하지만 조금 부족해요
I have money, but just not enough
재미있긴 하지만 조금 이상해요
It’s funny, but a little bit strange
계획이 있긴 있었지만 갑자기 취소되었어요
I had plans, but they got cancelled
하고 싶은 말이 있긴 하지만 조금 부끄러워요
I have something I want to say, but I’m a little bit shy
우리 학교 애들이 똑똑하긴 한데 노력을 안 해요
The kids at our school are smart, but they don’t try hard
우리 도시에 거지가 조금 있긴 한데 많진 않아요
There are some homeless people in our city, but not many
아이디어가 있긴 있지만 그 아이디어가 좋은지 모르겠어요
I have an idea, but I don’t know if it is good
일을 다 하긴 했지만 정신없이 해서 한번 더 확인해야 될 것 같아요
I did all the work, but I did it without really thinking, so I’ll probably have to check it again

Here are more examples of the same concept:

정신과 의사도 의사이긴 하지만 보통 인간의 삶을 살릴 수 없는 의사다
Psychiatrists are doctors too, but usually doctors that can’t save people’s lives
물론 수업 시간에 그 주제에 대해 배우기는 했지만 너무 짧게 배우고 시험 전날 하룻밤만 공부했기 때문에 금방 잊어버렸다
Of course, during class we learned about that subject, but it was very short and I only studied before the night of the exam so I forgot everything immediately
4

Comparing by Using~는/은 Twice in the Same Sentence

You saw this in earlier lessons, but it deserves another look. ~는/은 is sometimes used twice in one sentence to compare how two things differ. The two things are usually in separate clauses, split by “~고” or another clause-splitting principle. For example:

사과는 맛있고 바나나는 맛이 없어요
The apples are delicious, but the bananas are not
오늘은 비가 오고 내일은 비가 안 와요
Today it is raining, but tomorrow it won’t
5

What about X?

Finally, one more small usage of ~는/은. By placing ~는/은 after a noun and saying only that noun (with ~는/은), you can ask for clarification about that specific noun.

For example, imagine a teacher asks you what you did over winter vacation. After answering, you could ask the question back by saying:

선생님은?
Or, more formally:
선생님은요?

A common one that you will hear is:

(Conversation Audio)

Person 1: 집에 도착했어요
I arrived home
Person 2: 밥은?
Have you eaten yet (What about rice/food?)
Person 1: 아직 안 먹었어요
I haven’t eaten yet

A translation that fits every example is hard to find. The purpose of ~는/은 here is to ask about the thing just mentioned. For example, if you were describing all the furniture in your house, somebody could say:

“침대는?”

That translates to “what about your bed? Tell me about your bed.”

Another example:

(Conversation Audio)

Person 1: 저는 내일 엄마와 오빠랑 같이 밥을 먹을 거예요
Tomorrow, I am going to eat with my mom and brother
Person 2: 아빠는?
What about your dad? Why isn’t your dad going?Person 1: 아빠는 일을 해야 돼서 못 가요 = Dad has to work, so he can’t go
