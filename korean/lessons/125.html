Introduction

In this lesson you will learn a grammatical principle that makes your Korean sound a little sassy. Use it when you are a bit annoyed at somebody and want to show your frustration.

1

I said that…:~니까

~니까 can attach to a verb at the end of a sentence when the speaker wants to express frustration or anger at having to repeat something already said. In the present tense with a verb, ~니까 attaches directly to the plain/diary form conjugation. For example:

한다니까

This is actually a contraction of:

한다고 하니까

This is another compounded quoted grammatical principle, introduced in Lesson 120. Recall from Lesson 52 that to make a quoted sentence you first conjugate in the plain/diary form and then attach ~고. The patterns in this lesson are quoted sentences, followed by 하다, followed by ~(으)니까 (Lesson 81). For example, in the present tense:

하다 + ~ㄴ/는다+ ~고 + 하니까
한다니까
가다 + ~ㄴ/는다 + ~고 + 하니까
간다니까
먹다 + ~ㄴ/는다 + ~고+ 하니까
먹는다니까

In its simplest sense, you can see the meaning in the following exchange:

Person 1: 그것을 진짜 하고 싶지 않아
I really don’t want to do that
Person 2: 아~ 같이 하자!
Ah… (come on…) let’s do it together!
Person 1: 안 한다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) I’m not doing it!

~니까 is always used like this in the middle of a conversation (in response to something), never at the start, because the speaker is frustrated at having to say something again.

With most grammatical principles you can attach “요” to make the construction more formal. In theory you can do that here, but ~ㄴ/는다니까 is quite rude in itself, so avoid using it with anybody in a more superior position than you. With friends it is acceptable, but always remember its negative “frustrated or angry” connotation.

You can also attach this to a word in the past or future tense. As with the present tense above, ~니까 attaches directly to the plain/diary form conjugation, following the same quoting pattern for the past and future tenses. For example:

Past Tense:

하다 + ~았/었다 + ~고 + 하니까
했다니까
가다 + ~았/었다 + ~고 + 하니까
갔다니까
먹다 + ~았/었다 + ~고 + 하니까
먹었다니까
These are contractions of ~았/었다고 하니까. For example:
Person 1: 그 일을 했어?
Did you do it (that job/work/task)?
Person 1: 했다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) I did it!

Future Tense:

하다 + ~겠다 + ~고 + 하니까
하겠다니까
가다 + ~겠다 + ~고 + 하니까
가겠다니까
먹다 + ~겠다 + ~고 + 하니까
먹겠다니까
These are contractions of ~겠다고 하니까. For example:
Person 1: 돈을 언제 줄 거야?
When are you going to give me the money?
Person 2: 내일 주겠다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) I will give it (to you) tomorrow!

It is usually used in a very short sentence. Often the sentence is only the word that ~ㄴ/는다니까 is attached to, since the rest can be implied from the first time the speaker said it, or from the sentence being responded to.

Person 1: 재판이 언제 시작돼?
When does the case/trial start?
Person 2: 내일 시작된다니까!
I told you it starts tomorrow!

(Ugh, I already said that) I have to go to work!
아직 안 익었다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) they haven’t ripened yet!
거스름돈을 안 줬다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) you didn’t give me my change!

With Adjectives

~니까 also works with adjectives. Remember that the present tense plain/diary form of an adjective is identical to its dictionary form, so in the present tense ~니까 goes right after the dictionary form with no changes. This again follows the quoting pattern for adjectives. For example:

(Remember that 있다, 없다 and 싶다 are all conjugated as adjectives):

Person 1: 영어를 잠깐 해 봐
Try speaking in English
Person 2: 싫어~
No/I don’t want to/I don’t like it
Person 1: 아~ 그냥 해 봐!
Common, just try it
Person 2: 싫다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) I don’t want to!
Person 1: 내일 도서관에 갈까?
Shall we go to the library tomorrow?
Person 2: 아니. 난 진짜 가고 싶지 않아
No, I really don’t want to go
Person 1: 왜? 재미있을 건데.
Why? It’ll be fun?
Person 2: 가고 싶지 않다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) I don’t want to go
Person 1: 이 걸 버릴게
I’m going to throw this out!
Person 2: 그 걸 먹고 싶다니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) I wanted to eat that!
Person 1: 내일 가고 싶은 사람이 있어?
Is there anybody who wants to go tomorrow?
Person 2: 있어!
There is (me)!
Person 1: 없어?
Nobody? (in this situation this person probably ignored Person 2’s response)
Person 2: 있다니까
(Ugh, I already said that there is somebody who wants to go!)

Below are more examples without the preceding context, but you can imagine what sentence came before each:

이런 빵은 너무 두껍다니까
I told you that this kind of bread is too thick
그 사람은 보면 볼수록 예쁘다니까
I told you that the more you look at that person, the more pretty she is
사고가 날 것을 직감하고 미리 피했다고 해서 엄청 신기했다니까
I told you it was really weird/crazy that he was able to see in advance that there was going to be an accident and avoided it

With 이다:

~니까 can also attach to 이다. Here 이다 changes to (이)라, just as when quoting with 이다. As a reminder, this is how we quote with 이다:

이게 내 거라고 했어요
I said that this is mine
이게 내 것이라고 했어요
I said that this is mine

The same applies when adding ~니까 to 이다. ~니까 added to ~이다 creates a contraction that works like this:

이다 + ~고 + 하니까
이라니까
This is a contraction of ~(이)라고 하니까
이게 내 것이라니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) this is mine
Person 1: 이게 한국어로 뭐라고 불러요?
What do you call this in Korean?
Person 2: 부채라니까!
(Ugh, I already said that) it is a “부채”

In the same way, you can attach ~니까 to 이다 in the future ~ㄹ/을 것이다 conjugation. For example:

Person 1: 내일 갈 거야? 안 갈 거야?
Are you going tomorrow or not?
Person 2: 안 갈 거라니까
(Ugh, I already said that) I’m not going

Below are more examples without the preceding context, but you can imagine what sentence came before each:

이 운동은 건강한 몸매를 위한 운동이라니까 모두가 해 보는 것을 추천해
I said that this exercise is exercise that is good for a good figure, so I recommend that everybody try it
이게 그냥 속담이라니까 그러니 이 문장의 문법을 그렇게까지 공부 안 해도 돼
I told you it’s just a proverb, so you don’t need to study the grammar of this sentence to that extent

With other Grammatical Principles:

In Lesson 48 you learned to add ~자 to the end of a sentence to mean “let’s…”. For example:

오늘 복근 운동을 하자!
