잃어버리다 = to lose

Sometimes you will find two verbs connected by “~아/어”. This grammatical principle is fairly complex and isn’t discussed in great detail until Lesson 70.

A basic explanation for now is that “~아/어” essentially has no real meaning, and that the meaning of both verbs comes out. “버리다” is probably the most common verb that you will find after a verb and ~아/어. Constructions in this format “(verb + ~아/어 + 버리다) add emotion to a sentence. The emotion is as if something was “thrown away” or done without any real thinking of the consequences. Some quick examples:

끄다
to turn off
꺼 버리다
to turn off (with that added emotion)
가다
to go
가 버리다
to go (with that added emotion)
잊다
to forget
잊어버리다
to forget (with that added emotion)

Notice that 잃어버리다 and 잊어버리다 are actually independent words – whereas the other examples include a space (two verbs with a grammatical principle separating them)

Examples:

저는 열쇠를 잃어버렸어요
I lost my keys
저는 아이를 잃어버려서 걱정이 되었어요
I was worried because I lost the baby)

Conjugating이다

Exactly three things can be conjugated in Korean: verbs, adjectives and 이다. 이다 conjugates differently from verbs and adjectives, and in fact behaves differently in many ways you will meet in later lessons. This lesson shows how to conjugate 이다 and how it differs. The sentence below is the plain form, present tense of 이다, which you have seen so far:

나는 선생님이다
I am a teacher

If the noun before 이다 ends in a vowel, you can drop 이. For example:

나는 의사다
I am a doctor
나는 의사이다
I am a doctor

Both are correct. The pronunciation of “이” merges with the vowel in the noun; say the two sentences aloud and you will hear very little difference.

If the noun before 이다 ends in a consonant, this merging cannot happen. For example:

나는 선생님이다
I am a teacher (correct)

나는 선생님다 – incorrect

There is nothing for 이다 to merge with. And “선생님다” just does not flow; it is hard to move from the “ㅁ” sound straight to the “ㄷ” sound. The same principle appears in other conjugations of 이다, a little more complexly.

In almost every case, 이다 conjugates differently depending on whether the noun ends in a vowel or a consonant, for the same reason as 의사다 vs. 의사이다: the “이” sound merges with something and can disappear. Each conjugation is covered below, but here is one example to prepare you. Ignore the meanings and focus on the pattern.

For the past tense plain form, “었다” is added to the stem of “이다” (이). This is easy to follow, because every other verb and adjective does the same. For example:

의사이었다
선생님이었다

But 이었다 can merge to “였다” when the noun ends in a vowel. Both of these are correct:

의사이었다
의사였다

Say both aloud and notice how little difference there is. Both are easy to pronounce and flow well.

이 and 었다 cannot merge when the noun ends in a consonant. For example:

선생님이었다 – correct
선생님였다 – incorrect

Say both and hear the difference. ‘선생님였다’ is hard to pronounce; moving from the ㅁ sound straight to 여 is awkward. It is much easier as 나는 선생님-이-었-다.

This example is only the past tense plain form, but the same rule applies in many situations. Keep it in mind and the conjugations in this lesson will be much easier.

2

이다 Present Tense

The present tense of 이다 is more confusing than the past, because new syllables are added with no real logic. The past tense simply joins the stem “이” to the usual “었다”, but the present tense uses additions found with no other verb or adjective. Let’s take those first.

3

Informal Low Respect

Add ~이야 to a word ending in a consonant, or ~(이)야 to a word ending in a vowel:

나는 좋은 학생이야
I am a good student
그것은 책이야
That thing is a book
나는 선생님이야
I am a teacher
이것은 여권이야
This is a passport
그것은 사과야
That thing is an apple
나는 의사야
I am a doctor
야구는 좋은 스포츠야
Baseball is a good sport

For “아니다” in this form, simply add “~야” to “아니다:”

나는 학생이 아니야
I am not a student
그것은 책이 아니야
That thing is not a book
4

Informal High Respect

Add ~이에요 to a word ending in a consonant, or ~예요 to a word ending in a vowel:

그것은 사진이에요
That thing is a picture
저는 선생님이에요
I am a teacher
저는 좋은 학생이에요
I am a good student
이 사람은 저의 누나예요
This (person) is my sister
저는 의사예요
I am a doctor
저것은 사과예요
That thing is an apple

For “아니다” in this form, simply add ~에요 to 아니다:

저는 학생이 아니에요
I am not a student
Korean people are often unsure whether to add “~예요” or “~에요” to 아니다, so it is not uncommon to see “아니예요.”
5

Formal High Respect

Add ~입니다 (~이 + ~ㅂ니다) to words ending in a vowel or consonant:

저는 의사입니다
I am a doctor
그 사람은 저의 형입니다
That person is my brother
저는 선생님입니다
I am a teacher
저는 좋은 학생입니다
I am a good student
이 고기는 돼지고기입니다
This meat is pork

With words ending in a vowel, you can drop ~이 and attach ~ㅂ니다 directly. This is more common in conversation and not usually written.

For “아니다” in this form, add “~ㅂ니다” directly to “아니다.” For example:

저는 의사가 아닙니다
I am not a doctor
저는 학생이 아닙니다
I am not a student
그것은 저의 직업이 아닙니다
That (thing) is not my job
그것은 저의 여권이 아닙니다
That (thing) is not my passport
그 건물은 극장이 아닙니다
That building is not a theater
6

이다 Past Tense

The past tense of 이다 is simple: join ~이 to ~었~. When the word ends in a vowel, ~이 + ~었 can combine into ~였.

7

Informal Low Respect

Add ~이었어 to all words. If the word ends in a vowel, ~이었어 can contract to ~였어.

나는 바쁜 선생님이었어
I was a busy teacher
나는 학생이었어
I was a student
나는 선생님이었어
I was a teacher
나는 나쁜 애기였어
I was a bad baby
나는 나쁜 의사였어
I was a bad doctor
8

Informal High Respect

Same as Informal Low Respect above, with “~요” added after ~이었 or ~였. That is, add ~이었어요 to all words. If the word ends in a vowel, ~이었어요 can contract to ~였어요.

그것은 큰 비밀이었어요
That was a big secret
저는 선생님이었어요
I was a teacher
저는 의사였어요
I was a doctor
저는 나쁜 애기였어요
I was a bad baby
9

Plain Form

Same as above, but with the regular “~다” ending instead of “~어요.” That is, add ~이었다 to all words. If the word ends in a vowel, ~이었다 can contract to ~였다.

나는 선생님이었다
I was a teacher
나는 의사였다
I was a doctor
10

Formal High Respect

Add ~이었습니다 to all words. If the word ends in a vowel, ~이었습니다 can contract to ~였습니다.

저는 선생님이었습니다
I was a teacher
저는 의사였습니다
I was a doctor

In the past tense, 아니다 is conjugated like any other word. One example per respect level:

나는 학생이 아니었어
나는 학생이 아니었다
저는 학생이 아니었어요
저는 학생이 아니었습니다

Oddly, Korean speakers sometimes use these:

나는 학생이 아니였어
나는 학생이 아니였다
저는 학생이 아니였어요
저는 학생이 아니였습니다

Going by the rules of the language, the first set should be the correct one. No other word takes “~였~” on its stem; “~였~” comes from “이 + 었” and is never added on its own.

11

How to actually conjugate verbs/adjectives to the Future Tense

In Lesson 6 you conjugated the future tense by adding 겠어/겠어요/겠다/겠습니다 to the stem. ~겠~ is one way, but there is a more common one.

You needed more grammar first, namely how to conjugate 이다. ~겠~ is still used in Korean, just less than the method you are about to learn.

For verbs and adjectives in the future tense, first add ~ㄹ/을 to the stem.

~ㄹ attaches directly to stems ending in a vowel, and ~을 to stems ending in a consonant. For example:

가다 ends in a vowel, so

가다 + ㄹ
갈

먹다 ends in a consonant, so

먹다 + 을
먹을

——————-

This will sound complicated (it is): adding ~ㄹ/을 to an adjective stem turns it into a word that describes a noun in the future tense. For example:

행복한 사람
happy person
행복할 사람
a person that will be happy

Similarly (and this is the complicated part), adding ~ㄹ/~을 to a verb stem turns it into a word that describes a noun in the future.

먹을 음식
the food that will be eaten

In practice, whole clauses ending in verbs commonly describe the noun this way. For example:

제가 먹을 음식
the food that will be eaten by me (the food that I will eat)

——————-

If the explanation between the lines does not make sense, do not worry. That grammar is very hard at this stage and is covered in depth in Lessons 26 – 29. You may jump ahead if you like, but the mechanics are not important yet. For now, keep three points in mind:

  1. Adding ~ㄹ/~을 to the stem of an adjective allows that adjective to describe a noun in the future tense
  2. Adding ~ㄹ/~을 to the stem of a verb allows that verb to describe a noun in the future tense
  3. Because these newly formed words can describe nouns, they must be followed by a noun

What does all this have to do with the future tense?

Koreans usually form the future by adding ~ㄹ/~을 to a verb or adjective.
This is essentially the same as adding ㄴ/은 to an adjective stem, which you already know: (좋다 -> 좋은).
But you cannot end a sentence like this:

나는 좋은

Because 좋은 is an adjective modifying a noun, a noun must follow 좋은:

나는 좋은 사람

To end the sentence, add 이다 to the noun:

나는 좋은 사람이다
I am a good person.

So again, Koreans usually form the future of verbs and adjectives by adding ~ㄹ/~을 to the stem:

나는 행복할
나는 먹을
나는 공부할

But this turns verbs and adjectives into adjectives that describe nouns, so (just like 좋은) a noun must follow. The noun always used here is 것 (thing):

나는 행복할 것
나는 먹을 것
나는 공부할 것

To end those sentences, add 이다 to the noun:

나는 행복할 것이다
나는 먹을 것이다
나는 공부할 것이다

Translated word for word, these mean:

I am a thing who will be happy
I am a thing who will eat
I am a thing who will study

But their actual meanings are:

나는 행복할 것이다
I will be happy
나는 먹을 것이다
I will eat
나는 공부할 것이다
I will study

The 이다 is then conjugated for politeness or formality. Even though the sentence is future, 이다 stays in the present tense: the ~ㄹ/을 already makes the sentence future, so 이다 does not need to be.

것 is also often shortened to 거, simply because it is easier to say and shorter. These two are exactly the same:

저는 밥을 먹을 것이에요
I will eat rice
저는 밥을 먹을 거예요
I will eat rice

Notice that ~이에요 is added with 것 (which ends in a consonant) and ~예요 with 거 (which ends in a vowel). This is the same rule from earlier in the lesson for conjugating 이다 after a consonant or a vowel.

Korean people are often unsure whether to add “~이에요,” or “~예요” or “~에요” to 거 in these cases, so it is not uncommon to see “할 거에요.”

Other examples:

나는 내일 친구를 만날 것이야
I will meet my friend tomorrow
나는 내일 친구를 만날 거야
I will meet my friend tomorrow
저는 내일 학교에 갈 것입니다
I will go to school tomorrow
저는 영어를 공부할 거예요
I will study English

In Lesson 7 you learned how irregular words change under different additions. This is your first time adding ~ㄹ/을, so here is how irregulars change with it.

  • The ㅅ irregular, ㄷ irregular and ㅂ irregular all follow the same rules that were introduced in Lesson 7. The addition of the vowel causes a change (or elimination) of the last letter of the stem.
  • The ㅡ and 르 irregular are not affected by this addition.
  • Adding ~ㄹ/을 to a word that follows the ㄹ irregular brings about a change you are not familiar with. Normally, you would add ~ㄹ to the stem of a word ending in a vowel, and ~을 to the stem of a word ending in a consonant.
    • For example: 잃다 + ~ㄹ/을 = 잃을
      예쁘다 + ~ㄹ/을 = 예쁠
    • However, when you add ~ㄹ/을 to a stem of a word that ends in ㄹ, the ㄹ is dropped and ㄹ is attached directly to the stem. In effect, you removed something and replace it with exactly the same thing. For example:갈다 + ㄹ/을 = 갈
      빨다 + ㄹ/을 = 빨
      열다 + ㄹ/을 = 열
      저는 문을 열 거예요 = I will open the door (열 + 을 = 열)

The table below shows the changes from adding ~ㄹ/을 to a word.

Irregular Word Does this apply + ~ㄹ/을 것이다
ㅅ Irregular 짓다 (to build) YES 지을 것이다
ㄷ Irregular 걷다 (to walk) YES 걸을 것이다
ㅂ Irregular 쉽다 (to be easy) YES 쉬울 것이다
ㅂ Irregular 돕다 (to help) YES 도울 것이다
ㅡ Irregular 잠그다 (to lock) NO 잠글 것이다
르 Irregular 다르다 (to be different) NO 다를 것이다
ㄹIrregular 길다 (to be long) YES 길 것이다

The word 돕다 is included too. As you learned in Lesson 7, ㅂ changes to 오 when ~아/어 (or a derivative) is added. When any other vowel is added, ㅂ changes to 우, as shown above.

You will see these same changes whenever you add something beginning with ~ㄹ/을. For example:

~ㄹ/을래(요) – Lesson 44
~ㄹ/을까(요) – Lesson 63
~ㄹ/을게(요) – Lesson 63
~ㄹ/을걸(요) – Lesson 115

Future이다 – Using되다

The future tense of 이다 works as above, but another verb can also be used: 되다. 되다 is one of the hardest words in Korean because it has so many meanings, which you will meet as you progress. Its first meaning is “to become”, slightly different from “to be”. Here is “되다” in the past tense:

(Note how 되다 is used: ~이/가 attaches to the noun the subject “becomes”, not ~를/을)

저는 선생님이 되었어요
I became a teacher

Which is slightly different than:

저는 선생님이었어요
I was a teacher

Very similar, but “become” (versus “was”) implies that before that time the situation was different. In English:

I became a teacher last year
I was a teacher last year

“I became a teacher last year” means that before last year you were not a teacher, and last year you became one.

“I was a teacher last year” says nothing about whether you were a teacher before, or whether you still are. It only says you were a teacher last year.

되다 also works in the present tense (again slightly differently from 이다); examples come later in the course once more grammar is in place. It is mentioned here for its use in the future tense. First, conjugating 이다 to the future with ~겠다 is awkward.

나는 선생님이겠다

To say something “will be” something in the future, the nature of “되다” means there is no real difference between 되다 and 이다. For example:

저는 곧 선생님이 될 것입니다
I will become a teacher soon
저는 곧 선생님일 거예요
I will be a teacher soon

Other examples:

나는 미래에 의사가 될 거야
I will become a doctor in the future
나는 미래에 의사일 거야
I will be a doctor in the future
한국이 곧 좋은 나라가 될 것이다
Korea will become a good country soon
한국이 곧 좋은 나라일 것이다
Korea will be a good country soon
이 장소는 공원이 될 것이다
This place will become a park
이 장소는 공원일 것이다
This place will be a park

Note that the “일” above is not the word “일“. It is the future conjugation of 이다 using this lesson’s method. 선생님이다 becomes 선생님 + 이다 + ~ㄹ/을 것이다.

As your vocabulary grows, the same pattern lets you make sentences like:

This place will become a park next year
I will become a doctor in a few months

Lesson 11 gives the vocabulary for sentences like those.

The future tense sentences with 이다 and 되다 can also be made negative. For a 되다 sentence, add 안 or ~지 않다 as with any verb or adjective. For an 이다 sentence, use 아니다. Each pair of sentences above can be made negative. For example:

나는 미래에 의사가 되지 않을 거야
I won’t become a doctor in the future
나는 미래에 의사가 아닐 거야
I won’t be a doctor in the future
한국이 곧 좋은 나라가 되지 않을 거야
Korea won’t become a good country soon
한국이 곧 좋은 나라가 아닐 거야
Korea won’t be a good country soon
이 장소는 공원이 되지 않을 거야
This place won’t become a park
이 장소는 공원이 아닐 거야
This place won’t be a park

Those sentences are a bit ridiculous but grammatically correct. You would rarely want to say them, and usually there is a better way. For example, instead of:

나는 미래에 의사가 되지 않을 거야
I won’t become a doctor in the future

it would be more natural to say something like “I don’t want to become a doctor in the future.” You will learn that and other ways to sound natural as you progress. For now, focus on the grammar, not on when you would use such a sentence.

One more quick thing, since the future conjugation of 이다 is introduced here. Using ~ㄹ/을 것이다 on 이다 can also make a sentence where the speaker is guessing about a present situation. Some examples first:

그 사람이 의사일 거예요
That person is probably/most likely a doctor
그것은 여권일 거예요
That thing is probably/most likely a passport
문제는 돈일 거예요
The problem is probably/most likely money
Remember, you are not adding 일 to these nouns. You are adding ~이다 and then ~ㄹ 것이다 to 이다. That is where this “일” comes from.

These can also be said with 아니다 instead of 이다:

그 사람이 의사가 아닐 거야
That person is probably/most likely not a doctor
그것은 여권이 아닐 거야
That thing is probably/most likely not a passport
문제는 돈이 아닐 거야
The problem is probably/most likely not money

In these cases the speaker is not talking about themselves. And although the sentence is in the future tense, the speaker is guessing that something is the case now. So time words (“next year”, “in a few months”) would be strange here, since that is not the intended meaning.

So how do you tell a “guessing” sentence from “something will become something”? As you will keep seeing, understanding Korean is all about context, and the situation almost always makes the speaker’s meaning clear.

For now, rather than the guessing nuance, focus on using ~ㄹ/을 것이다 to put verbs and adjectives in the future tense, and remember that 되다 can replace 이다 in the future.
