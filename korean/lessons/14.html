Vocabulary

The vocabulary is split into nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs to keep things simple.

Nouns:

냄새 (나다) = smell

Common Usages:

냄새가 나다

냄새가 지독하다

냄새를 맡다

Notes: It is hard for an English speaker to understand this word. “냄새” is a noun that means “smell.” If you want to say that something is smellY (adjective), you need to use the verb 나다, which is very confusing. 나다 acts as a passive verb which is introduced in Lesson 14.

The pronunciation of this word is closer to “숨따”

Common Usages:

저는 큰 박스 뒤에 숨었어요

Introduction

If you looked at the vocabulary list before reading this, you may have noticed that many of the words look very similar to each other. That is because this lesson is about passive verbs. They are not very difficult, but they are rarely taught well, so take your time here.

A quick look at what passive verbs are in English will help you understand their Korean equivalents.

An active verb is used when a subject does an action. For example:

In the examples below, the subject is “I”

I eat
I learn
I open

Active verbs often act on an object. For example:

“Object” here means the part of speech that a verb acts on. Learners sometimes think an object has to be “a thing,” like “a pencil” or “a door.” In fact any noun can be an object, including people and abstract ideas. The objects are underlined in the following sentences:

I love my father
He wants respect

I eat rice
I learn Korean
I open the door

Some active verbs by their nature cannot act on an object. The active verbs are underlined in the examples below:

I went to the park
I arrived at the park

“The park” in the examples above is not an object. The verb is not acting on it; it just indicates a place.

In all of the examples above, with or without an object, the subject performs the action. These are all active sentences.

Passive sentences indicate that an action is performed on the subject. For example:

I was kicked
The door was opened
The hamburger was eaten

In English, passive verbs feel like adjectives because the sentence structures are similar. For example:

I was handsome
The door was big
The hamburger was delicious

Compare an active verb and a passive verb in similar sentences:

1) I opened the door
2) The door was opened

In the first example “I” am the subject and I performed the action (opening) on the object (the door).
In the second example, “the door” is the subject and the action (opening) was performed on it.

Here are more active-passive pairs so you can see how they differ:

All of the examples below are in the present tense. In the present tense these passive sentences (like most plain present-tense sentences) sound unnatural; nobody says “I turn the computer on.” The present tense is used here only because it is the basic “raw” form. They could just as well be past, future, or something more complex.

I turn the computer on
The computer is turned on

I lock the door
The door is locked

I respect my friend
My friend is respected

I cooked the rice
The rice is cooked

Passive verbs (like adjectives) cannot act on an object. For example:

I opened the door (active verb) – correct
The door was opened me (passive verb) – incorrect
The door was big me (adjective) – incorrect

However, a passive sentence can add information about by whom (or by what) the action was performed. For example:

The door was opened by me
The door was opened by the wind
The door was opened by the guard

The same applies to the passive sentences from earlier:

The computer was turned on by me
The door was locked by the teacher
My friend is respected by many people
The rice was cooked by my mother

Because passive verbs cannot act on an object, you will never see ~을/를 in a Korean sentence whose verb is passive. ~을/를 marks objects, so it cannot appear with a passive verb.

This is probably the most important paragraph in the lesson: passive verbs are usually unnatural in Korean. In almost every situation the active form is more natural. Instead of “the house is built,” say “somebody built the house” (which implies that the house is now built).

Still, you need to understand how passive verbs work in Korean to keep progressing.

There are several ways to make a passive sentence in Korean. This lesson covers each of them.

Remember too that passive verbs are verbs, not adjectives. Learners often think they are adjectives because they look, sound and feel like adjectives and never act on objects. Treat them as verbs; this matters because you must conjugate them as verbs, not as adjectives.

Alright, let’s get started.

1

Korean Passive Verbs –하다 to되다

You have seen before that verbs ending in 하다 can usually be split from 하다 to give the noun form of the verb. 하다 then means “do”:

나는 일했어
I worked

which has the same meaning of:

나는 일을 했어
I did work/I worked

With 하다 verbs, most of the time you can simply swap 하다 for 되다 to make the verb passive. For example:

포함하다
to include
포함되다
to be included
제공하다
to provide
제공되다
to be provided
대체하다
to replace
대체되다
to be replaced

Passive verbs let you build sentences with a meaning similar to the active version. The structure differs, though, because the passive verb cannot act on an object. For example:

이 값은 세금을 포함해요
This price includes tax
세금은 포함돼요
The tax is included
저는 점심을 준비했어요
I prepared (the) lunch
점심이 준비되었어요
(The) lunch was prepared
회사는 기계를 대체했어
The company replaced the machine
기계는 대체되었어
The machine was replaced

Adding 어 to 되 gives either 되어 or 돼, with no difference in meaning. (For example, 되다 in the present tense can be 돼 or 되어. In the past tense it can be 되었다 or 됐다.

Often the speaker wants to say by whom/what the lunch was prepared, or by whom/what the machine was replaced. For example:

– The lunch was prepared by the school
– The machine was replaced by the company

These sentences still use a passive verb, but add information about how the action happened. This is exactly where Korean strongly prefers the active form. In Korean (and probably in English too), “I prepared the lunch” is far more natural than “The lunch was prepared by me.” The grammar is still important, so we cover it here.

To build these sentences, remember that a sentence with a passive verb can NOT have an object, so you CANNOT attach ~를/을 to “the school” or “the company.” The sentence below would make no sense and probably would not be understood:

점심이 학교를 준비되었어요

Instead, to show how or by what means the passive action happened, you need different particles. If that part of the sentence is a person, you can attach “에게” to the noun. For example:

그것은 이해되었어
It was understood
그것은 학생들에게 이해되었어
It was understood by the students
집은 청소되었어
The house was cleaned
집은 아버지에게 청소되었어
The house was cleaned by my dad

Again, avoid using this passive voice in Korean. You need to understand it here so later lessons can build on it. The two sentences above would be better said as:

학생들은 그것을 이해했어요
The students understood that
아버지는 집을 청소했어요
My dad cleaned the house

The particle ~에 is used when this part of the sentence is a non-person. For example:

점심이 학교에 준비되었어요
The lunch was prepared by the school

The particle “~에 의해” can also attach to non-person nouns here, but the difference between ~에 and ~에 의해 is not introduced until Lesson 78. Since this passive form is best avoided anyway, stay with what is presented here.

Also remember ~(으)로 from Lesson 12: it indicates the tool, device, method or material something is done with. So you can say:

저는 집을 청소기로 청소했어요
I cleaned the house with a vacuum cleaner

To say the same thing with the passive verb (to be cleaned), you again attach ~(으)로 to 청소기, because it was the method/tool used to clean:

집은 청소기로 청소되었어
the house was cleaned by a vacuum cleaner, whereas:
집은 청소기에 청소되었어
is not correct, although most Korean people would probably understand you.

Wow. Confusing.

This is confusing, even for Korean people. Do not get hung up on the difference between ~에/에게 /~(으)로 here, because Koreans rarely use passive verbs compared to active ones.

What should you take from all of this? Here are the main points:

  1. Clauses ending in a passive verb can never have a word with an object marker (를/을) within the clause.
  2. Passive verbs are conjugated just like active verbs, even though they feel like adjectives
  3. Though clauses ending in a passive verb cannot have an object in the clause, other particles can be attached to nouns to indicate how the passive action occurred. These particles are usually:
    1. 에 – to indicate that something occurred due to a non-person
    2. 에 의해 – to indicate that something occurred due to a non-person, but we haven’t studied this specifically yet
    3. 에게 – to indicate that something occurred due to a person
    4. ~(으)로 – to indicate the tool/method by which something occurred

One more thing. A noun with ~에 (or ~에게, or ~에 의해) in a passive sentence is not necessarily the thing that caused the action. ~에 can also have its familiar job of marking a place. We saw this sentence before:

세금은 포함돼요
the tax is included

You can add the noun “값” to show where the tax is included.

세금은 값에 포함된다
The tax is included in the price

Here “~에” does not mean the tax is included by the price; it is included in the price. Recognizing this takes practice and will get easier as you progress. For now, focus on the big picture rather than the tiny details.

2

Korean Passive Verbs –하다 to받다

Besides swapping 하다 for 되다, there is another similar way to make these verbs passive. It only works with certain verbs (usually ones acting on people): swap 하다 for 받다 (to receive). For example:

저는 저의 형을 존경해요
I respect my brother
저의 형은 존경 받아요
My brother is respected (literally-my brother receives respect)

The same rules for ~에 and ~에게 apply as before:

교장선생님은 선생님들에게 존경받아요
The principal is respected by the teachers
나는 너의 말에* 감동받았어
I was impressed with what you said

*Remember that 말하다 means “to speak.” Removing 하다, 말 becomes the noun form of “speak”: “words/the thing you said/what you said/etc.”

3

Korean Passive Verbs – Non-하다 verbs

So far you have only changed ~하다 verbs into the passive. Many Korean verbs do not end in ~하다. For words not ending in 하다, there is often a separate (but very similar) word for the passive voice. These are always listed separately in the vocabulary lists. Some examples:

켜다
to turn on
켜지다
to be turned on
끄다
to turn off
꺼지다
to be turned off
닫다
to close
닫히다
to be closed

These are just a few active-passive pairs. There are many more, and unfortunately there is no way to know instinctively which one is active and which is passive. In all the examples above, the longer word (one more syllable) is the passive verb. Sometimes, though, the longer word is the active verb. For example:

붙다
to be attached
붙이다
to attach
끓다
to be boiling
끓이다
to boil

You might guess a pattern: “if “이” is the difference between the two, the longer word is the active verb.” It is not that simple. Here are two pairs that show the opposite:

쓰다
to write
쓰이다
to be written
보다
to see
보이다
to be seen

There are many such pairs in Korean, and the only way to know which is active and which is passive is to memorize each one as you meet it. The helpful part is that the active verb usually looks very similar to the passive verb, with just one syllable added or removed.

Passive verbs like 줄다 (to be decreased) and 줄이다 (to decrease) follow the same pattern.

The same goes for 열리다.

You can treat these passive verbs just like the 되다 and 받다 verbs from the previous two sections. Make sure you use the passive verb, not the active one (for example, 닫히다 instead of 닫다 in a passive sentence):

밥은 학교에 의해 제공된다
food is provided by the school
밥은 학교에 의해 제공되었다
food was provided by the school
문은 바람에 의해 닫혔어요
The door was closed by the wind

With these passive verbs, though, you need to think about whether the verb describes the state of something. None of the passive verbs in the previous two sections (하다 to 되다 and 하다 to 받다) described a state. For example, again:

나는 너의 말에 감동받았어
I was impressed with what you said

This simply means you were impressed, not that you are in the state of being impressed. But in these sentences:

The computer is on.
The TV is off
The door is locked

All of those nouns are in the state of something: the computer is in the state of being on, the TV off, the door locked. To say that something “is in the state” of something in Korean, add ~아/어 있다 to the passive verb. For example:

As you know, 있다 has many uses in Korean. Sometimes 있다 is a verb and sometimes an adjective. In ~아/어 있다 as described here, 있다 is a verb. For now this only matters when conjugating in the plain form; later it will matter in other places too.

저는 컴퓨터를 켰어요
I turned the computer on
컴퓨터가 켜져 있어요
The computer is (in the state of) on
저는 TV를 껐어요
I turned the TV off
TV가 꺼져 있어요
The TV is (in the state of) off
저는 문을 잠갔어요
I locked the door
문이 잠겨 있어요
The door is (in the state of) locked

Notice that ~이/가 is used in the passive sentences above. It is usually more natural in this kind of sentence. Do not worry about that yet (it is covered in Lesson 17); focus on how the verbs, active and passive, are used.

This “state” idea confuses many learners, so here is a full comparison of a passive verb describing a state and the same passive verb not describing a state. For example:

저는 문을 닫았어요
I closed the door

This is an active sentence. It has an active verb and an object. No problem.

문이 닫혔어요
The door was closed

This is a passive sentence. It says that at some point in the past the door was closed by something or someone. It is the exact passive equivalent of “저는 문을 닫았어요,” except that we have no idea who or what closed the door. You might assume the door was then in the state of “closed,” but the sentence does not actually say so.

문이 바람에 닫혔어요
The door was closed by the wind

This is essentially the same sentence, but now we know how the door was closed. Again, you might assume the door was then in the state of “closed,” but the sentence does not say so.

문이 닫혀 있었어요
The door was closed

This is another passive sentence, but notice the difference from the two above. Here the sentence specifically says the door was in the state of “closed.” We have no idea whether anyone had opened it recently; all we know is that, at that time, the door was not open.

저는 문을 닫아요
I close the door

This is another active sentence. It makes sense, but it probably would not be said on its own without an adverb or other information (for example, “I close the door every day at 9:00pm”).

문이 닫혀요
The door closes

Technically correct, but it needs extra information to sound natural (for example, “the door closes every day at 9:00pm”). It is the exact passive equivalent of “저는 문을 닫아요,” except that nothing says who or what will close the door. It does not describe the door being in the state of “closed”; it says the door somehow closes.

문이 닫혀 있어요
The door is closed

Notice the difference from the example above. This sentence does not refer to the door closing. It only says the door is currently in the state of “closed.”

문이 닫힐 거예요
The door will close

Again, this refers to the door somehow closing. It is the exact passive equivalent of “저는 문을 닫을 거예요,” except that nothing says who or what will close the door.

문이 닫혀 있을 거예요
The door will be closed

This passive sentence says the door will be in the state of “closed” in the future. It does not say who or what will close it; all we know is that, at that future time, the door will be closed.

Just because an active verb has a passive equivalent does not mean you can attach ~아/어 있다 to it to describe a “state.” Typically this is only done for words like on, off, open, closed, and so on.

A good example of where this does not work is 속이다 and 속다.

속이다
to trick somebody
속다
to be tricked
나는 친구를 속였어
I tricked my friend

This is an active sentence.

나는 친구에게 속았어
I was tricked by my friend

This is a passive sentence

나는 속아 있다…
… I am… in the state of being tricked.
This does not make sense. Once you are tricked, you are tricked. You do not stay in the state of “tricked” the way a door stays open.

펜이 탁자에 놓여 있었어요
The pen was (in the state of) laying on the table
4

Korean Passive Verbs –내다 and나다

Quite a few Korean words can end in either 나다 or 내다.

These two work like 되다 and 하다: a word ending in 나다 is passive, a word ending in 내다 is active. For example:

끝내다
to finish
끝나다
to be finished

나다 and 내다 can also be used as standalone verbs, not just attached to other words. Their meanings are complex and depend heavily on the situation, but in general:

나다
for something to come up/arise/occur
내다
to make something come up/arise/occur

Not every word ending in 나다 has a matching 내다 verb (and vice versa). For example, 어긋나다 is a word (to be out of step with something), but 어긋내다 is not. The two most common words ending in 나다/내다 are:

끝내다
to finish
끝나다
to be finished
고장 내다
to break
고장 나다
to be broken

It is very common to use the past tense of both of these words in the passive voice even when the thing is currently broken or finished. In English we would use the present tense, but in theory the thing was broken or finished in the past. For example:

숙제는 끝났어요
My homework is finished
컴퓨터는 고장 났어요
The computer is broken

You can express these in the active voice, but “나다” (as above) is more common than 내다 in these cases. Still, the following are acceptable:

저는 숙제를 끝냈어요
I finished my homework
저는 컴퓨터를 고장냈어요
I broke the computer

As mentioned, 나다 itself means “for something to come up/arise/occur,” so it appears in many sentences to say that some noun “comes up.” Three common uses are with 기억 (a memory), 생각 (a thought) and 냄새 (a smell):

아! 그것이 기억났다!
Ah! I remember that! (Literally – my memory came up)
좋은 생각이 났어요!
I have a good idea (Literally – a good idea came up)
그 가방에서 냄새가 났어요
That bag is smelly (A smell is coming out of that bag)
그 방에서 냄새가 났어요
That room is smelly (A smell is coming out of that room)
Remember that 나다 is a passive verb and cannot act on an object, so the particle ~이/가 must be used on “것.” You will learn more about this structure in the next lesson.

Another common use of “나다” is this phrase:

큰 일 났어요!

Literally, this means “a big thing/task/work came up!” It is used like “Oh no! Something bad just happened!” or, more commonly, “Oh crap!”

You will meet “나다” with many other words as you study. The most common uses of 나다 (and its active form 내다) are the ones above. Other common examples you will learn soon are listed below. They are not in this lesson's vocabulary list, so you do not need to memorize them now; they are here because they relate to this topic.

소리
sound
소리가 나다
the verb of a sound happening (for a sound to “come up”)
화
anger, rage
화가 나다
to be angry (for anger to “come up”)
사고
accident
사고가 나다
to get into an accident (for an accident to “arise”)
땀
sweat
땀이 나다
to be sweating (for sweat to “come up”)
전쟁
war
전쟁이 나다
for a war to start (for a war to “come up”)
멀미
motion sickness
멀미가 나다
for motion sickness to “come up”

In many of these cases 나다 is conjugated in the past tense where English speakers would think of the situation in the present. Take an earlier example:

아! 그것이 기억났다!
Ah! I remember that!

Here 나다 is in the past tense but the English translation is present. If you picture a memory as something that can “come up,” then in theory it had already come up before you spoke, hence the past tense. Context tells you whether the speaker means past or present.

You can use these words in the present tense, but that means the noun is currently coming up. A good example from the list above:

땀이 났어요
I’m sweating

When 나다 is in the present tense here, it suggests the thing is currently “coming up.” In most situations the difference is negligible and really splitting hairs.

However, let’s split some hairs:

땀이 났어요
Sweat was coming out of my body… which means that there is currently sweat on my body… which means that I am wet from the sweat on my body that had previously come out of my body.
땀이 나요
Sweat is literally currently coming out of my body
저는 그것이 기억났어요
I remembered that… which means that I also currently remember that fact… which means that I know that fact that I had previously remembered
저는 그것이 기억나요
That memory is literally currently just coming to my mind

This past/present pattern does not appear in negative sentences. For example, if you say:

그것이 기억이 안 났어

Does your memory “not coming up” mean it is currently not up as you speak? That barely makes sense even in English.

A past-tense negative sentence with 나다 is only used to say that you did not remember something at some time in the past. To say you cannot currently remember something, use the present tense. For example:

나는 그때 기억이 안 났어
I didn’t remember (that) at that time
나는 그것이 기억 안 나
I don’t remember that
5

Korean Passive Verbs – Normal verbs

So far you have seen 하다/되다/받다 verbs, verbs that can be in a state (닫다/닫히다), and 나다/내다 verbs. Sometimes a verb that fits none of these can still be made passive, simply by adding ~아/어지다 to the verb stem. This does not work with every verb, but some common examples are:

주다
to give
주어지다
to be given

(Note that the word is 주어지다 and not 줘지다)

짓다
to build
지어지다
to be built (짓 + 어 = 지어) + 지다 = 지어지다
기회가 주어졌어요
I was given a chance
그 집은 한국에서 지어졌어요
that house was built in Korea

That’s it!

There was a lot of grammar in this lesson. All of it matters and you need it to keep progressing, but remember: active sentences are always more natural than passive ones in Korean.
