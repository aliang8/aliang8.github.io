Introduction

In this lesson you will learn to connect two clauses with ~다니. This is your first real introduction to what we will call compounded quoted grammatical principles.

1

Indicating an Emotion after hearing what somebody says:~다니 How is this grammatical principle formed?

As you move into more advanced grammar, you will often meet a grammatical principle that looks like one you already know, but placed after ~다~ (or after ~(이)라~ in the case of nouns).

Take this lesson’s topic, ~다니. ~(으)니 is the ending you learned in Lesson 81, and here it is placed after ~다~. Usually in this situation the clause before ~다~ is a quoted sentence, and that whole quoted sentence is attached to whatever grammatical principle comes next. The ending after ~다~ (in our case, ~(으)니) compounds its meaning with the quote.

From here on we will call these compounded quoted grammatical principles, because that is exactly what they are.

Let’s look at the following example:

네가 떠나다니 너무 슬퍼

Remember: “The clause before ~다~ is a quoted sentence.” So in the example above, the following is a quote:

네가 떠나(다)~

But how is that a quote? It does not look like one.

In these compounded quoted grammatical principles, the ~다~ is an abbreviation. It is short for:

~ㄴ/는다고 하다 (remember that the rules change when quoting an adjective)

So the sentence above could actually be written like this:

네가 떠난다고 하니 너무 슬퍼
I am sad because you said you are leaving

However, as this lesson will show, these sentences are usually abbreviated so that only ~다니 sits between the two clauses.

There are many other compounded quoted grammatical principles, each introduced in its own lesson when it matters. At this level, this is the only one you need to worry about. Still, remember: if you see a grammatical principle attached to ~다~ instead of directly to the verb or adjective, you are most likely looking at a compounded quoted grammatical principle.

One more example before we move on. The following:

그녀가 예쁘다더라

Is actually an abbreviation of:

그녀가 예쁘다고 하더라

We will not get into the meaning of ~다더라 here (though based on Lesson 118, you can probably guess it) or any other compounded quoted grammatical principle. For now, just be able to recognize one when you meet it while studying on your own.

2

What does this grammatical principle mean?

Now that we know what this ending is made of, we can talk about its meaning. Much like ~더니 from the previous lesson, ~다니 is typically used in one specific type of sentence. Here is the earlier example again, because ~다니 almost always appears in this format:

네가 떠나다니 너무 슬퍼

The format: the speaker repeats something another person said (placed before ~다니 as the quoted part), then expresses his or her emotion or feelings on hearing that fact. A simple translation of the sentence above would be:

네가 떠나다니 너무 슬퍼
I’m sad that you are leaving

Remember, though, that the first part of the sentence is really a quote. A more detailed (but less flowing) translation would be:

네가 떠나다니 너무 슬퍼
Now that/because I have heard that you are leaving, I am sad, or
네가 떠나다니 너무 슬퍼
After hearing that you are leaving, I am sad

The most common emotions after the quoted sentence are adjectives expressing that one is sad, happy, surprised or impressed. Here are many examples, all following a similar format:

너의 아버지도 오시다니 기뻐
I’m glad that your dad is coming as well (Now that I have heard that your father is coming as well, I am glad)
너의 부모님이 이혼하시다니 안됐다
It’s too bad that your parents are getting a divorce (now that I have heard that your parents are getting a divorce, it is too bad)
네가 나에게 고맙다니 내가 더 고맙다
After hearing you say ‘thank you’ to me, I feel more thankful
슬기가 대학원에 가다니 믿을 수 없어
I can’t believe Seulgi is going to graduate school (now that I have heard that Seulgi is going to graduate school, I can’t believe it)
북극에 있는 빙하가 다 녹다니 큰 일이다
It is a big problem that all the glaciers in the north pole are melting (After hearing that all the glaciers in the North pole are melting, (I can see that) this is a big problem
우리 집 앞에 경찰서가 생기다니 다행이다
Thankfully a police station is being built in-front of our house (Now that I have heard there is a police station being built in-front of our house, I am thankful)

The full “quoted sentence” translation is left out of the sentences below, since by now you get the idea:

해양 생물이 점점 사라지고 있다니 믿을 수가 없다
I can’t believe that the organisms in the ocean are gradually disappearing
부모님의 허락도 없이 쌍꺼풀 수술을 하다니 정말 실망이다
I’m disappointed that you would get a double eyelid surgery without the permission of your parents
아이들은 추워도 아이스크림을 많이 먹는 경향이 있다니 신기하다
It’s crazy that kids have the tendency to eat ice cream even when they are cold
지중해 해수면이 해마다 점점 높아지고 있다니 이제라도 조치를 취해야 한다
The Mediterranean Sea level is gradually increasing each year, so we’ll need to act right now
그 프로그램에 대한 시청자 반응이 이렇게 안 좋다니 다시 프로그램을 만들어야 해요
The reactions of the viewers to that program weren’t good, so we need to make the program again

The clause before ~다니 can also be conjugated into the past tense, which puts the quoted part in the past tense. Some of the examples above are reused below so you can see the slight difference:

네가 떠났다니 너무 슬퍼
I’m sad that you left (Now that/because I have heard that you left, I am sad)
슬기가 대학원에 갔다니 믿을 수 없다
I can’t believe Seulgi went to graduate school (now that I have heard that Seulgi went to graduate school, I can’t believe it)
너의 부모님이 이혼하셨다니 안됐다
It’s too bad that your parents got a divorce (now that I have heard that your parents got a divorce, it is too bad)

Okay, enough of the extra translations:

네가 나에게 고마웠다니 내가 더 고맙다
After hearing that you said ‘thank you’ to me, I feel more thankful
너의 강아지가 죽었다니 너무 안됐다
It is too bad that your dog died
그 토끼의 서식지가 다 없어졌다니 안됐다
It is too bad that that rabbit’s habitat disappeared
놀이터에 있는 그네가 망가졌다니 믿을 수 없다
I can’t believe that the swing in the playground broke
우리가 만든 드라마의 시청률이 떨어졌다니 너무 실망스러워요
I’m very disappointed that the ratings for the drama we made dropped
동네 내과에 갔더니 대학 병원 내과에 가라고 하다니 점점 걱정이 되기 시작했다
I went to a local clinic, and they told me to go to a university hospital, so I started to get more worried

Depending on the situation, you can also end a sentence with ~다니 in this form: use the sentences above, but leave out the emotion after ~다니. The emotion is then understood from context. For example, if somebody just said the following:

너의 부모님이 이혼하셨다니…

Unless the speaker is some weirdo who wants to start dating your mother or father after the divorce, you can assume from context that the speaker means something like “it’s too bad.”

Another good example would be:

너의 강아지가 죽었다니

Remember that Korean speakers love shortening their sentences.

3

~ㄴ/는다니

Earlier in the lesson we stressed that the ending we are dealing with is ~다니 and not ~ㄴ/는다니.

So what does ~ㄴ/는다니 mean?

Surprisingly, it has the same meaning described above, except that the quoted part of the sentence is in the future tense. For example:

슬기가 대학원에 간다니 믿을 수 없어요

This would translate to:

I can’t believe Seulgi will go to graduate school (now that I have heard that Seulgi will go to graduate school, I can’t believe it)

This may not look like it should be the correct translation, but it is. Here are the earlier examples again, conjugated this way:

너의 부모님이 이혼하신다니 안됐다
It’s too bad that your parents will get a divorce (now that I have heard that your parents will get a divorce, it is too bad)
우리 집 앞에 경찰서가 생긴다니 다행이다
Thankfully a police station will be built in-front of our house (Now that I have heard there will be a police station built in-front of our house, I am thankful)
북극에 있는 빙하가 다 녹는다니 큰 일이다
It is a big problem that all the glaciers in the north pole will melt (After hearing that all the glaciers in the North pole will melt, (I can see that) this is a big problem
학교 후문을 내일부터 이용 못 한다니 짜증난다
It’s annoying that we won’t be able to use the back door of the school starting from tomorrow
집 앞에 있는 오래된 단풍나무를 잘라야 한다니 너무 안타까워요
It is too bad that we we will have to cut the old maple tree in front of our house
