Introduction

This lesson could be called: grammatical principles you should have met by now, taught before we get deep into the harder material of Unit 5. It covers a variety of smaller principles that you absolutely need in order to speak, read and understand Korean.

1

~아/어지다 vs. ~아/어뜨리다

The vocabulary list of Lesson 38 includes 떨어지다 and 떨어뜨리다. Words ending in ~아/어지다 are often intransitive (they cannot act on a noun) and have a transitive (can act on a noun) ~아/어뜨리다 equivalent. This is not the grammatical principle ~아/어지다 that attaches to adjectives (for example 나쁘다 + ~아/어지다 = 나빠지다).

Words ending in ~아/어지다 indicate that something occurs, usually without saying who or what caused it. For example:

병이 탁자에서 떨어졌다
The bottle fell from the table

Words ending in ~아/어뜨리다, by contrast, have a subject (usually a human, but not always) that actually does the action to the object. For example:

나는 병을 떨어뜨렸다
I dropped the bottle

Here are other words that follow this same pattern:

쓰러지다
for something to collapse
쓰러뜨리다
to collapse something
건물이 쓰러졌다
The building was knocked down
바람은 건물을 쓰러뜨렸다
The wind knocked down the building

———–

무너지다
for something to collapse
무너뜨리다
to collapse something
건물이 무너졌다
The building collapsed
그 폭발은 건물을 무너뜨렸다
That explosion collapsed the building

쓰러지다 and 무너지다 are easy to confuse because they mean essentially the same thing. 쓰러지다 is used when something falls over, like a tree blown down. 무너지다 is used when something collapses within itself without toppling, like a building purposely imploded so that debris does not hit the next building. The end result is the same, both structures are on the ground, but the way they fall is slightly different.

2

~ification:화 (化)

화 is a Hanja character found in many Korean words to mean “change.” Some very common examples:

변화
change
화장실
bathroom/restroom
화학
chemistry

If you have been keeping up with your Hanja studies, you will know that the character for 화 is 化.

화 is often placed after the noun form of a ~하다 verb to indicate that the noun represents some kind of change. In English, this usually adds “~ification” to the word. For example:

국제
international/global
국제화
changing into being international/global… which is better translated to:
국제화
internationalization/globalization

The vocabulary list of this lesson shows that many nouns can take ~화. You can also add “하다” to words with ~화 attached to turn them into verbs. For example:

활성화하다
to vitalize
일반화하다
to generalize
산업화하다
to industrialize
상업화하다
to commercialize
가속화하다
to accelerate
현대화하다
to modernize
합리화하다
to rationalize
차별화하다
to differentiate
서구화하다
to westernize
다양화하다
to diversify
대중화하다
to popularize
국제화하다
to globalize
평준화하다
to standardize
간소화하다
to simplify

Here are those words used in sentences:

운동은 피의 순환을 활성화해요
Exercise vitalizes blood flow
그 상황을 일반화하는 것은 어려워요
It is hard to generalize that situation
산업화는 경제 성장을 가속화할 수 있어요
Industrialization can accelerate the development of the economy
우버는 택시 서비스를 상업화해서 성공했어요
Uber succeeded in commercializing the service of getting a taxi
요즘에 오래된 도시를 현대화하는 곳이 많아요
These days there are a lot of old cities that are modernizing themselves
산업화를 빨리 할수록 경제가 더 빨리 성장해요
The quicker industrialization happens, the quicker the economy develops
그 살인자가 많은 사람을 죽인 것을 자꾸 합리화해요
That murderer keeps rationalizing all of the people he killed
수학 수업을 할 때 수업 내용을 차별화하는 것이 좋아요
When you do a math class, it is good to differentiate the contents of the class

It seems like the Chinese government is resisting the westernization of China

Adding이 to Names

If a Korean name ends in a 받침 (final consonant), you will often see 이 added between the name and the particles ~는/은, ~이/가 and ~를/을. This is mostly about pronunciation, and it is quite colloquial.

For example, take a friend named 희연. Saying “희영은” actually sounds closer to “희여는”, which makes the name ambiguous. Is it 희여 or 희연? To prevent this, it is common and colloquial to say:

희영이는 이빨을 부러뜨렸어요

The same goes for all names ending in a 받침. Although Hangul has many letters, the only ones you are likely to see as the 받침 on the final syllable of a Korean name are ㄱ, ㅁ, ㄴ, ㅇ, ㄹ, ㅂ. Names ending in a vowel do not get this treatment, since there is no ambiguity.

Here are some examples:

은혁이가 시험준비를 하고 있어요
은혁 is preparing for the exam
기범이는 희영이를 만나러 어제 학교에 갔어요
기범 went to school yesterday to meet 희영

The 이 is never required. The two sentences above would be fine without it. Ask a Korean person what the difference is and they will say “ahh, it just sounds ‘느끼해’”. “느끼해” is usually the word for how you feel after too much pasta, bread, or something greasy, though it also describes the feeling of hearing something so lovey-dovey it is corny. The feeling is hard to explain in words. To be safe, just add “이” to names ending in a 받침, unless you are writing a romance novel and want to sound “느끼해”.

Adding~아/어하다 to Adjectives

You have probably come across these pairs of words before:

부끄럽다
부끄러워하다

슬프다
슬퍼하다

기쁘다
기뻐하다

부럽다
부러워하다

부끄럽다
부끄러워하다

… and other adjectives with ~아/어하다 attached.

What is going on here? What is the difference between the two forms?

The adjectives above (and others like them) take ~아/어하다 when the speaker is describing what another person is feeling. For example:

그는 너무 부끄러워했어요
He was very shy

Look at the adjectives listed above. What do they have in common? They all describe what somebody else is feeling. How can you know what another person feels? You can call someone pretty or handsome; those are your own opinions. But when the adjective describes another person’s feelings, it is more natural to add ~아/어하다.

That does not mean you will never see a sentence like this:

그는 너무 부끄러웠어요
He was very shy

It would be strange to say that sentence out loud, but you might find it in a novel. Authors describe characters who are, most of the time, figments of their own imagination. So it is acceptable to leave off ~아/어하다 because the author knows for certain what the character is feeling.

As you have learned, 싶다 (from ~고 싶다) is an adjective. It also expresses what somebody wants. Since one person cannot inherently know what another wants, ~아/어하다 is often added to 싶다. For example:

희영이는 밥을 먹고 싶어해
Heeyoung wants to eat (rice)

In all the examples above, adjective + ~아/어하다 is used when the speaker is not the subject, and specifically with words describing a feeling one experiences. So these would (typically) be incorrect on their own:

제가 너무 부끄러워했어요
제가 너무 기뻐했어요

제가 너무 슬퍼했어요
제가 너무 부러워했어요

If the subject in these examples were changed to another person, the sentences would be correct.

However, you can use the ~아/어하다 form of an adjective with the speaker as the subject if the sentence has an object. For example, all of these are correct:

제가 가난을 너무 부끄러워했어요
I am shy about/of my poorness (the fact that I’m poor)
제가 친구의 성공을 너무 기뻐했어요
I am very happy/glad about/of my friend’s success
제가 친구의 죽음을 너무 슬퍼했어요
I am sad about/of my friend’s death
제가 남의 성공을 너무 부러워했어요
I am envious about/of others success

You can remove the objects in the sentences above, but only if the object is obvious from context. This is the same as changing 좋다 to 좋아하다 and 싫다 to 싫어하다. You could not just walk into a room and say:

“나는 좋아해”

Instead, you need an object to specify what you like. For example:

“나는 과자를 좋아해”

But if your friends were all talking about 과자 and going around the room saying whether they liked it, you could answer with just “나는 좋아해”. In the same way, you could only say the examples above if the object was already understood.

Formal Subject Marker~께서(는)

The particle ~께서 marks the subject of a sentence when the subject is a person who deserves a high amount of respect. For example:

아버님께서 밥을 드셨습니다
The father ate
이 잡채는 장모님께서 만드신 것이에요
This 잡채 was made by my mother in law
할아버지께서 옛날에 런던에서 사셨어요
Our grandfather lived in London a long time ago
지금 교장선생님께서 잠깐 말씀을 하시겠습니다
Now, the principal will speak for a moment
우리 부장님께서 오늘 오후에 출발할 예정이십니다
Our boss is scheduled to depart this afternoon

You can do this whenever somebody deserves a lot of respect, but it is not strictly necessary. It is more common in writing and in very polite speech. For example, a vice principal introducing the principal to a room of 100 parents would probably use ~께서. Failing to use the honorific ~요 or ~ㅂ/습니다 forms with somebody who deserves respect might get you thrown out of the room; most people would not care if you skipped ~께서.

Very few people use “~께서” in daily life. It is usually reserved for very formal situations. It is also common to see “~는” added to ~께서. It could have no function at all, or any of the subtle functions that ~는 carries; it depends entirely on context. Any of the example sentences above could have “~는” added to “께서”, for example:

아버님께서 밥을 드셨습니다
The father ate
아버님께서는 밥을 드셨습니다
The father ate (but somebody else, maybe the mother, didn’t)
