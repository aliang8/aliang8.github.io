Vocabulary

The vocabulary is split into nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs to keep things simple.

Nouns:

사실 = fact

Common usages:

사실을 인정하다
to admit the truth
그것이 사실이다
that is the truth
사실은…
the fact is…

Notes: Often used at the start of sentences to mean “actually…” When used like this, it is common to colloquially say “실은…”

Also commonly used as the noun in a quoted version of ~는 것. For example:

그녀는 아이가 죽었다는 사실을 숨겼어요
She hid (the fact that) her child died

See Lesson 52 for more information on this.

It can also be used to literally mean “fact”

그것은 사실이에요?

저는 부장님에게 그 사실을 말할 거예요

늦다 = to be late

The pronunciation of this word is closer to “늗따”

Common Usages:

늦게까지
until a late time

This is often irrelevant, but 늦다 is both a verb and an adjective. As a verb, it usually indicates that one arrives late. For example:

우리가 늦어서 죄송해요
Sorry we arrived late

It is often used as an adjective to describe a time that is late. For example:

우리는 늦은 저녁을 먹었어요
We ate a late dinner

As an adjective, ~게 is often added to it in order to create an adverb. For example:

그녀는 언제나 늦게 와요
She comes late every time
저는 늦게 도착했어요
I arrived late

Usually, adverbs like this end in “-ly,” however, “lately” is unnatural in these English sentences. Korean people often incorrectly use the word “lately” because of this confusion.

Again, the fact that it can be an adjective or a verb is usually irrelevant. The only thing that matters is that it can be conjugated correctly to 늦다 (as an adjective) or 늦는다 (as a verb).

Introduction

In Lesson 12 you learned some important particles. There are a few more basic ones to know before you move on to more complex grammar. Most of them are very common, so you will see them in almost every sentence from here on.

1

Korean Particles (and):~과/와, ~랑/이랑 and~하고

~과/와, ~랑/이랑 and ~하고 can all be used interchangeably to mean “and.”
~과 and ~와 are the same: ~과 attaches to words ending in a consonant, ~와 to words ending in a vowel. Likewise ~랑 and ~이랑 are the same: ~이랑 attaches to words ending in a consonant, ~랑 to words ending in a vowel. ~하고 attaches to either. All of them simply follow a noun. For example:

우리는 밥과 빵을 팔아요
We sell rice and bread
나는 사과와 바나나를 샀어
I bought apples and bananas

In the two examples above, ~와/과/랑/이랑/하고 sits between two nouns that together act as the object of the sentence. There are two nouns, but together they behave like a single object.

Other particles can attach to the construction made with ~와/과/랑/이랑/하고 as well. For example:

나는 인천이랑 서울에 갈 거야
I will go to Seoul and Incheon
형하고 아버지는 영화를 봤어
My brother and dad saw a movie
저는 약과 녹차만 샀어요
I only bought medicine and green tea
2

Korean Particles (with):~과/와, ~랑/이랑 and~하고

This sounds strange to an English speaker, but the same particles mean both “and” and “with.” You tell them apart from context. That sounds hard, but in practice the sentence structure makes it clear, even for a beginner, which meaning is intended.

When the meaning is “and,” a noun always follows 과/와/(이)랑/하고. For example:

나는 사과와 바나나를 샀어
I bought apples and bananas

After 와 comes another noun, so you are talking about apples AND bananas. But look at this sentence:

나는 친구와 갔어
I went with my friend

There is no noun after 와, so it can only mean “with.” If ~와 in that sentence meant “and,” it would translate to:

I went, my friend and…
… which is nonsense.

Here are some examples:

저는 친구와 집에 갔어요
I went home with my friend
나는 아버지랑 공원에 갈 거야
I will go to the park with my dad
선생님은 학생들과 박물관에 갔다
The teacher went to the museum with the students
요즘에 사람들이 친구들이랑 매운 음식을 먹지 않아요
These days, people don’t eat spicy food with their friends

You can also use these particles to mean both “and” and “with” in the same sentence:

저는 밥을 친구랑 저의 어머니랑 먹었어요
I ate (rice*) with my mom and my friend

*Koreans often use “밥” (rice) to mean “food” in general, because rice comes with (almost) every meal. “밥을 먹었어” can simply mean “I ate.”

Two adverbs often appear in sentences with “with”: 같이 and 함께. Both mean “together,” and they can be used even when the sentence has no particle meaning “with” (과/와/랑/이랑/하고). For example:

우리는 빵을 같이 먹었어요
We ate bread together
우리는 빵을 함께 먹었어요
We ate bread together
우리는 집에 같이 들어갔어요
We went into the house together
우리는 집에 함께 들어갔어요
We went into the house together
우리는 음악을 같이 들었어요
We listened to music together
우리는 음악을 함께 들었어요
We listened to music together

In English, using “together” and “with” in the same sentence usually sounds unnatural. For example, this sounds odd:

I ate bread together with a friend

Instead, in English we would say one of these:

I ate bread with a friend
We ate bread together

In Korean, however, 같이 or 함께 is fine in either case, with or without the word for “with” in the sentence. For example:

저는 빵을 친구랑 같이 먹었어요
I ate bread with a friend (together)
저는 빵을 친구와* 함께 먹었어요
I ate bread with a friend (together)
저는 빵을 친구랑 먹었어요
I ate bread with a friend
저는 빵을 친구와 먹었어요
I ate bread with a friend

Notice that ~와 is used with 함께 here instead of ~랑. As with the meaning “and,” “~와/과” is more likely in writing and formal situations, while “~(이)랑” is more likely in speech. This depends on the speaker, but it is generally true. 함께 is likewise more common in writing and formal situations, so it tends to pair with ~과/와.

One more layer: 같이 is used far more often than 함께 (같이 is one of the most common words in Korean; 함께 is much further down the list). But when 함께 is used, it is more likely to appear with ~과/와. These are generalizations from years of listening to Korean speakers.

More examples:

저는 녹차를 엄마랑 같이 마셨어요
I drank green tea with my mom
저는 엄마랑 유명한 영화를 같이 봤어요
I saw/watched a famous movie with my mom
저는 선생님과 함께 공부했어요
I studied with my teacher
저는 여자 친구와 함께 영화를 봤어요
I watched a movie with my girlfriend

You can also use these particles to say that you are simply “with” somebody in a location. To do this, use 있다 along with one of the adverbs meaning “together.” For example:

나는 친구랑 같이 있어
I’m with my friend
저는 친구와 집에 함께 있어요
I’m with a friend at home
This meaning of “with” cannot be used like this:
I built a house with my hands
Here “my hands” are the method by which you did something, so, as you learned in Lesson 12 ~(으)로 should be used instead. For example:

저는 손으로 집을 지었어요

3

Korean Particles (to) 에게/한테/께

These three particles all indicate that you are doing (usually giving) something TO somebody. 에게, 한테 and 께 mean the same thing, but ~한테 is usually spoken, ~에게 is usually written (though it is still very common in speech), and ~께 is used when the person you are giving to deserves respect (께 is the honorific form of 에게/한테).

아버지는 아들에게 돈을 준다
The father gives money to his son
나는 학생들한테 한국어를 가르쳤어
I taught Korean to the students
저는 부장님께 그 사실을 말할 거예요
I will tell that (fact) to my boss

In the sentence above with ~께, a different verb (말씀) and grammatical form (드리다) would more naturally be used. You have not learned those yet, so they are left out here; they come up in Lesson 39. For now, focus on the use of ~께.

Using ~께 does not mean your sentence has to end politely. ~께 is used when the person being given to is of high importance, regardless of who you are talking to. For example, a teacher talking to a student about something given TO the principal could say:
나는 책을 교장선생님께 줬어
I gave the principal a book

Again, “드리다” would most likely replace 주다 here. For now, focus on ~께; we will come back to this in Lesson 39.

4

Korean Particles (from):~에게서/한테서/(으)로부터

You learned in Lesson 12 that ~에서 can mean “from” in many situations. ~에게서/한테서 can also translate as “from,” but their use is more restricted.

~에게서/한테서 is the opposite of ~에게/한테/께: it is used when somebody receives something from somebody. It attaches to the person you receive from. For example:

나는 나의 여자친구에게서 편지를 받았어
I received a letter from my girlfriend

The “thing” received does not have to be physical. It can be abstract, like a story or an explanation. For example:

저는 교감선생님에게서 한국어를 배웠어요
I learned Korean from my vice principal
저는 그것을 친구한테서 들었어요
I heard that from my friend

A very similar particle is ~(으)로부터. It can also attach to the person you receive something from. For example:

나는 나의 여자친구로부터 편지를 받았어
I received a letter from my girlfriend
저는 교감선생님으로부터 한국어를 배웠어요
I learned Korean from my vice principal
저는 그것을 친구로부터 들었어요
I heard that from my friend
저는 친구들로부터 사랑을 많이 받았어요
I received a lot of love from friends
아버지로부터 선물이 왔어요
A present came from my father

~(으)로부터 can also be used when receiving something from a non-person (a company, the government, etc.). For example:

나는 돈을 정부로부터 받았어
I received money from the government
이것을 하늘로부터 받았어요
I received this from the sky (heavens)

You cannot use ~에게서/~한테서 for receiving something from a non-person, though.

To sum up: ~(으)로부터 works for receiving something from a person or a non-person. ~에게서 and ~한테서 have a similar meaning but only work when you receive from a person.

5

Do something for somebody:~을/를 위해(서)

To say that you are doing something FOR (the benefit of) somebody, add ~를/을 to that person, followed by 위해(서):

나는 나의 여자 친구를 위해(서) 꽃을 샀어
I bought flowers for my girlfriend
나는 부장님을 위해(서) 이것을 썼어
I wrote this for my boss
저는 친구를 위해 빵을 만들었어요
I made bread for my friend
아버지를 위해 시원한 물을 준비했어요
I prepared cool water for my father

There does not seem to be any difference between 위해 and 위해서.

This is usually used for doing something for a person, but it can sometimes be used for a non-person too:

저는 회사를 위해 열심히 일할 거예요
I will work hard for the company

The key point is that the thing you are doing something for must be a noun. You can use 위해 to say you are doing something for the purpose of a verb (I am going there to/for the purpose of see(ing) a movie), but that comes in Lesson 32, after you learn how to turn verbs into nouns.

Remember that “for” has many meanings in English. Saying “for” in English does not mean you can translate it directly to ~를/을 위해. In Korean, ~를/을 위해 means for the benefit of. Take this sentence:

I am waiting for the bus. The “bus” is the object you are waiting for, so in Korean you attach ~을/를 to “bus,” not ~을/를 위해:

나는 버스를 기다린다

A side note: “위해” is actually a verb, and its base form is 위하다. When ~아/어/여 is added to 위하다, it becomes 위하여. You first saw this in Lesson 5. 하다 officially becomes 하여 when ~아/어/여 is added, but this is usually shortened to 해. So “위하여” can be used instead of “위해.” Technically this works for any word ending in ~하다, but it is far more common with 위하다 than with any other verb or adjective.

6

About something~에 대해

~에 대해 also attaches to nouns, like 를/을 위해, but it means “about.” In simple sentences it is easy to follow:

나는 너에 대해 생각했어
I thought about you
나는 나의 아버지에 대해 말했어
I spoke about my father
나는 그것에 대해 책을 쓸 거야
I will write a book about it
그 회계사는 정부에 대해 나쁜 말을 했어요
That accountant said bad things about the government

As with ~을/를 위해서, there is little (if any) difference between ~에 대해 and ~에 대해서. The sentences above could all be written as:

나는 너에 대해서 생각했어
I thought about you
나는 나의 아버지에 대해서 말했어
I spoke about my father
나는 그것에 대해서 책을 쓸 거야
I will write a book about it
그 회계사는 정부에 대해서 나쁜 말을 했어요
That accountant said bad things about the government

One place you cannot use ~에 대해 is a sentence like this:

My favorite thing about you is your eyes.

Here is that sentence in Korean, even though the grammar in it is not covered until Lesson 28.

너에 있어서 내가 가장 좋아하는 것은 너의 눈이야.

The grammar in that sentence is too advanced for now; it is explained in Lesson 28.
