Vocabulary

The vocabulary is split into nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs to keep things simple.

Nouns:

길 = street

Common Usages:

길을 잃다
to get lost (literally, to lose a street)
길을 건너다
to cross a street
길이 막히다
for the streets to be “clogged” with traffic

Notes: This is also used to indicate that somebody is “on the way” somewhere. For example:

저는 집에 가는 길이에요
I am on my way home

This grammar is taught in Lesson 113.

Example:

이 길에서 오른 쪽으로 가세요
Turn right at this street
이 길에서 직진하세요
Go straight on this road
우리는 옛길을 따라 걸었어요
We walked along, following the old road

Using Adjectives~ㄴ/은

This will not help with those greetings, but it is a major step in learning Korean. Remember these two facts from the previous lesson:

  • All sentences must end with either a verb or adjective
  • All verbs/adjectives end with the syllable ‘다’

Both are always true, but let’s look deeper:

  • All sentences must end with either a verb or adjective
    Yes, but verbs and adjectives can ALSO go elsewhere in a sentence. In the previous lesson, you learned this sentence:
저는 배를 원하다
I want a boat

(나는 배를 원해 / 저는 배를 원해요)

What if you want to say “I want a big boat”? That sentence has a verb and an adjective. Where does the adjective go? In Korean, it goes in the same position as in English. For example:

나는 배를 원하다
I want a boat
나는 big 배를 원하다
I want a big boat

Simple. So we just put the Korean word for big (크다) into that sentence?:

나는 크다 배를 원하다
Not correct. Not by a long shot.
Remember that second rule I taught you?:
  • All verbs/adjectives end with the syllable ‘다’
    – Yes, but the version of the word with ‘다’ as the last syllable is simply the dictionary form of that word and is rarely used. Every verb/adjective in Korean has a ‘stem,’ which is made up of everything preceding 다 in the dictionary form of the word. Let’s look at some examples:
크다
크 (stem) + 다
작다
작 (stem) + 다
좋다
좋 (stem) + 다
길다
길 (stem) + 다
배우다
배우 (stem) + 다

Most of the time, when you use a verb or adjective, you remove ~다 and add something to the stem.

To make an adjective that describes a noun, as in:

small boy
big boat
delicious hamburger
soft hand

you remove ‘~다’ and add ~ㄴ or ~은 to the stem of the adjective.

When the last syllable of the stem ends in a vowel (크다/비싸다/싸다), add ~ㄴ to the last syllable:

Word Stem Adjective that can describe a noun Example Translation
크다 크 큰 큰 배 Big boat
비싸다 비싸 비싼 비싼 음식 Expensive food
싸다 싸 싼 싼 것 Cheap thing

When the last syllable of the stem ends in a consonant (작다/좋다/많다), add ~은 to the stem:

Word Stem Adjective that can describe a noun Example Translation
작다 작 작은 작은 남자 Small man
좋다 좋 좋은 좋은 아들 Good son
많다 많 많은 많은 돈 A lot of money

Back to the sentence we wanted to write:

I want a big boat = 저는 크다 배를 원하다 = incorrect
I want a big boat = 저는 큰 배를 원하다 = correct

The key is seeing the difference between the following:

음식은 비싸다
The food is expensive
비싼 음식
expensive food

The first is a sentence. The second is not; it needs more words. You need a verb or adjective to predicate the noun “expensive food.” For example:

나는 비싼 음식을 먹다
I eat expensive food

(나는 비싼 음식을 먹어 / 저는 비싼 음식을 먹어요)

The verb “to eat” predicates this sentence.

저는 비싼 음식을 좋아하다
I like expensive food

(나는 비싼 음식을 좋아해 / 저는 비싼 음식을 좋아해요)

The verb “to like” predicates this sentence.

비싼 음식은 맛있다
Expensive food is delicious

(비싼 음식은 맛있어 / 비싼 음식은 맛있어요)

The adjective “to be delicious” predicates this sentence. Notice there is no object.

(One last reminder: you do not yet know how to conjugate verbs and adjectives at the end of a sentence. That is the next lesson. So this lesson shows un-conjugated forms. These sentences are technically incorrect, but understanding them is crucial to understanding Korean sentence structure.

As in the previous three lessons, conjugated examples appear below each un-conjugated one. You will probably not understand these conjugations yet.)

More examples of adjectives describing nouns within a sentence:

나는 작은 집에 가다
I go to the small house

(나는 작은 집에 가 / 저는 작은 집에 가요)

나는 큰 차를 원하다
I want a big car

(나는 큰 차를 원해 / 저는 큰 차를 원해요)

나는 잘생긴 남자를 만나다
I meet a handsome man

(나는 잘생긴 남자를 만나 / 저는 잘생긴 남자를 만나요)

나는 많은 돈이 있다
I have a lot of money

(나는 많은 돈이 있어 / 저는 많은 돈이 있어요)

나는 뚱뚱한 학생을 만나다
I meet a fat student

(나는 뚱뚱한 학생을 만나 / 저는 뚱뚱한 학생을 만나요)

Lessons 1 and 2 said that adjectives cannot “act” on objects. Looking at the sentences above, you might say “Those sentences have an object and an adjective!” The rule is that adjectives cannot act on an object to predicate a sentence. So a sentence like this is impossible in either language:

나는 집을 작다
I small house

But adjectives and objects can appear in the same sentence. An adjective can describe an object that is predicated by a verb. The examples below continue this.

In all the examples above, notice how an adjective describing a noun differs from an adjective predicating a sentence. For example:

나는 작은 집에 가다
I go to the small house

(나는 작은 집에 가 / 저는 작은 집에 가요)

The verb “to go” predicates this sentence.

그 집은 작다
That house is small

(그 집은 작아 / 그 집은 작아요)

The adjective “to be small” predicates this sentence. Notice there is no object.

저는 큰 차를 원하다
I want a big car

(나는 큰 차를 원해 / 저는 큰 차를 원해요)

The verb “to want” predicates this sentence.

이 차는 크다
This car is big

(이 차는 커 / 이 차는 커요)

The adjective “to be big” predicates this sentence. Notice there is no object.

The adjective is always descriptive, but when it goes before a noun to describe it, that noun can sit anywhere in the sentence (subject, object, location, or elsewhere). English works the same way. Take a simple sentence like this:

남자는 음식을 먹다
The man eats food

(남자는 음식을 먹어 / 남자는 음식을 먹어요)

We can use adjectives to describe each noun in it. For example:

행복한 여자는 작은 차 안에 있다
The happy girl is inside the small car

(행복한 여자는 작은 차 안에 있어 / 행복한 여자는 작은 차 안에 있어요)

Some adjectives end in “~있다.” The most common for a beginner are:

맛있다
delicious
재미있다
fun, funny

When an adjective ends in “~있다” like this, attach ~는 to the stem instead of ~ㄴ/은. For example:

그 남자는 재미있는 남자이다
that man is a funny man

(그 남자는 재미있는 남자야 / 그 남자는 재미있는 남자예요)

나는 맛있는 음식을 먹다
I eat delicious food

(나는 맛있는 음식을 먹어 / 저는 맛있는 음식을 먹어요)

This comes from what we call the “~는 것” principle. For now, do not worry about why ~는 is used instead of ~ㄴ/은; just memorize it as an exception. The idea is introduced in Lesson 26 and continues through Unit 2. It relates to verbs describing nouns. For example:

“The man who I met yesterday will go to the park that I want to go to”

That is complex, and it is the basis of the ~는 것 principle. You start learning it in Lesson 26.

To be a lot of:많다

A good way to practice adjectives describing nouns versus predicating sentences is the word “많다.” 많다 is an adjective meaning there is “many” or “a lot” of something, and its English translation depends on how it is used. Describing nouns, it works like this:

나는 많은 음식을 먹다
I eat a lot of food

(나는 많은 음식을 먹어 / 저는 많은 음식을 먹어요)

나는 많은 돈이 있다
I have a lot of money

(나는 많은 돈이 있어 / 저는 많은 돈이 있어요)

나는 많은 아내가 있다
I have a lot of wives (ha!)

(나는 많은 아내가 있어 / 저는 많은 아내가 있어요)

Predicating a sentence, “많다” works like this:

사람이 많다

Never think of a Korean sentence as an exact English translation. Korean and English grammar are completely different, and forcing English structure onto Korean is unnatural. If we stuck with “a lot of” for “많다” and forced it onto “사람이 많다”, we would get:

People are a lot of

… which is clearly wrong. What is “사람이 많다” describing? That there is a lot of something. So the translation should be:

사람이 많다
there is a lot of people

(사람이 많아 / 사람이 많아요)

So when 많다 predicates a sentence, it usually translates as “There is/are a lot of…”. Another example:

음식이 많다
there is a lot of food

(음식이 많아 / 음식이 많아요)

This also applies to very complex sentences. Eventually you will be able to say:

There are a lot of singers who become famous and spend all of their money too quickly

That sentence would also end in “많다.” The structure is basically:

(singers who become famous and spend all of their money too quickly)가 많다

You are far from building sentences like that, but this lesson brings you a step closer.

Also notice that 이/가 attaches to the subjects of sentences ending in “많다.” With some words, ~이/가 on the subject is more natural than ~는/은, and 많다 is one of them. Later lessons point out more cases where ~이/가 is more natural than ~은/는.

4

Particle~도

~도 is another very useful particle. It means “too/as well.” It replaces the subject particles (는/은) OR the object particles (를/을), depending on what you are saying “too” about. For example:

저도 한국어를 말하다
I speak Korean as well (In addition to other people)

(나도 한국어를 말해 / 저도 한국어를 말해요)

which is different from:

저는 한국어도 말하다
I speak Korean as well (in addition to other languages)

(나는 한국어도 말해 / 저는 한국어도 말해요)

Notice the difference between those two examples. In English they are written the same but sound different when spoken. The first emphasizes that YOU also speak Korean, in addition to other people. The second emphasizes that you speak Korean in addition to other languages.
The next two examples show the same thing:

저도 사과를 먹다
I eat apples as well

(나도 사과를 먹어 / 저도 사과를 먹어요)

저는 사과도 먹다
I eat apples as well

(나는 사과도 먹어 / 저는 사과도 먹어요)

Again the English differs in pronunciation. The first means “other people eat apples, but I too eat apples.” The second means “I eat other food too, but I also eat apples.” Whatever noun “~도” attaches to is the thing being expressed as “too.” More examples:

나도 그것을 알다
I know that, too

(나도 그것을 알아 / 저도 그것을 알아요)

나도 피곤하다
I am tired, too

(나도 피곤해 / 저도 피곤해요)

나의 딸도 행복하다
My daughter is happy, too

(나의 딸도 행복해 / 저의 딸도 행복해요)
