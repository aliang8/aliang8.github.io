Introduction

This lesson covers ~(이)나 and ~거나. They create similar meanings but attach to different kinds of words.

1

(Noun) or (Noun):~(이)나

~(이)나 attaches to words to create a few related meanings. Most often it shows that it hasn’t been decided which noun will be acted on. A simple example:

저는 빵이나 밥을 먹고 싶어요

Here the speaker hasn’t decided whether to eat bread or rice. The usual English translation of ~(이)나 is “or.” For example:

저는 빵이나 밥을 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat bread or rice

Below are some examples:

저는 술이나 콜라를 마시고 싶어요
I want to drink alcohol or a soft drink (cola)
딸이나 아들을 낳고 싶어요?
Do you want to have (give birth to) a daughter or a son?
저는 저 남자나 저 여자를 뽑을 거예요
I’m going to choose/hire that man or that woman
양식이나 그런 것을 작성 안 해도 돼요?
I don’t need to fill out a form or something like that?
대학교에서 철학이나 화학을 공부하고 싶어요?
In University, do you want to study philosophy or chemistry?
오늘 학생들이랑 체육관에서 재미있는 활동이나 게임을 할 거예요
Today I’m going to do a fun activity or game in the gymnasium with the students

In those examples ~(이)나 is attached to the object. It can also attach to the subject, to show that it hasn’t been decided who will perform the action. “Or” still works as a translation. For example:

우리 엄마나 우리 아빠가 저를 차로 거기까지 데려다 줄 거예요
Either mom or dad will take me there by car
저 선수나 이 선수가 오늘 새로운 기록을 세울 것 같아요
Either that athlete or this athlete will probably set a new record today

So far ~(이)나 has marked a choice between two nouns. It can also attach to a single word, with no second option following. For example:

밥이나 먹을래?

In these cases the speaker would rather do something else, but settles on the thing specified as a last resort. ~(이)나 expresses that this option is the best of what remains. For example:

밥이나 먹을래?
Well… I guess we could eat rice?

That sentence fits many situations. Say you wanted pizza or a hamburger, but it is too late for delivery. As a last resort you could say “well, shall we just eat rice?” It’s not the best option, but it’s the best of the remaining ones.

There is no neat English equivalent for this meaning. In the examples below, “(Well…)” stands in for it, but focus on the Korean:

저는 공부나 할 거예요
(Well…) I’m going to study
산책이나 할래요?
(Well…) Shall we go for a walk?
영화나 보자
(Well…) Let’s go see a movie
그냥 줄거리나 설명해 주세요
(Well…) Just explain the plot to me
운동이나 할래?
(Well…) Shall we exercise?
돈이나 주세요
(Well…) Give me money

You already met ~(이)나 in Lesson 25, in these constructions (which we’ll call “words” for simplicity):

아무 거나
아무 데나
아무 때나

In these words ~(이)나 is attached to the nouns 거 (thing), 데 (place), and 때 (time) to create the following meanings:

아무 거나
anything
아무 데나
anywhere (any place)
아무 때나
anytime

Now that you know what ~(이)나 does, it is worth seeing where those meanings come from. Essentially, the words above could be translated as:

아무 거나
there’s no specific thing, but “any” thing would be okay I guess
아무 데나
there’s no specific place, but “any” place would be okay I guess
아무 때나
there’s no specific time, but “any” time would be okay I guess

Here are some sentences from Lesson 25, each with an extra translation:

저는 아무 거나 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat anything (I’d eat anything)

This could also be translated as “there’s no specific thing I’d like to eat – I’d eat anything”

저는 아무 데나 가고 싶어요
I want to go anywhere (I’d go anywhere)

This could also be translated as “there’s no specific place I’d like to go, I’d go anywhere”

아무 때나 좋아요
Anytime is good

This could also be translated as “there’s no specific time, anytime is good”

So far you have seen ~(이)나 attached to nouns. ~나 can also attach to adverbs, or on top of other grammatical principles that turn words into adverbs.

An adverb is a word that tells you where, when, how, or to what degree something happens. “집” is a noun, but once ~에서 is attached, the whole construction counts as an adverb in Korean, because it tells us an action happened in the house. English doesn’t attach particles to nouns like this, so there is no exact parallel.

When only one option is given (not two), you will often see ~(이)나 attached to other grammatical principles that turn words into adverbs. For example:

중간지점에나 데려다 주는 게 어때요?
Well, how about I take you to the halfway point

When two options are given, though, it is more natural to add ~(이)나 directly to the first option, without the extra grammatical principle in between. Compare these two sentences:

저는 캐나다에서나 미국에서 일하고 싶어요

저는 캐나다나 미국에서 일하고 싶어요
I want to work in Canada or America

In the first example, ~(이)나 is attached to ~에서, which is attached to “캐나다.” Notice that ~에서 also appears on the second option (미국). There is no need to add ~에서 twice, so it can be dropped from the first option.

Both sentences are grammatically correct, but every Korean speaker would prefer the second. It is much more natural to omit the grammatical principle between the noun and ~(이)나 when it will be attached to the next noun anyway.

Here is another example:

저는 버스로나 택시로 갈 거예요

Here the speaker will go “by bus” or “by taxi.” ~(이)나 is attached to both “버스로” and “택시로.” There is no need to add ~(으)로 twice, so it can be dropped from the first option. This is much more natural:

저는 버스나 택시로 갈 거예요
I will go by bus or by taxi

Below are some other examples:

농구를 체육관이나 밖에서 하고 싶어?
Do you want to play basketball in the gymnasium or outside?
선생님! 시험을 어렵게나 길게 만들지 말아 주세요!
Teacher, please don’t make the exam difficult or long
주위를 살피고 저쪽으로나 이쪽으로 가세요
Look around (at your surroundings) and then go this way or that way
2

(Verb) or (Verb):~거나

So far ~(이)나 has attached to nouns or adverbs, to show that a choice between them hasn’t been made. ~거나 attaches to a verb to show that it hasn’t been decided which action will be done. A very simple example:

여자 친구를 위해 편지를 쓰거나 선물을 사 줄 거예요
I will write a letter, or buy a present for my girlfriend

With ~(이)나 the focus is on which of two nouns (or adverbs) will be acted on. With ~거나 the focus is on which action will take place. Take our simple sentence from before:

저는 빵이나 밥을 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat bread or rice

Here it has probably already been decided that you will eat. The question is what you will eat: “bread” or “rice.” With ~거나, it hasn’t been decided which action will happen. Our sentence from above:

여자 친구를 위해 편지를 쓰거나 선물을 사 줄 거예요
I will write a letter, or buy a present for my girlfriend

Here you are not choosing between two objects but between two actions. More examples:

신발끈을 묶거나 신발을 벗으세요
Tie your shoes or take them off
오래된 차를 고치거나 새로운 차를 사야 돼요
I need to fix my old car or buy a new one
제가 하는 말에 뭔가를 덧붙이거나 수정하고 싶으면 지금 말씀하세요
If you want to add something to what I said, or modify it, please tell me now

~거나 attaches to verbs, but it can be used with adjectives too. For example:

이 문제가 너무 어렵거나 도움이 필요하면 교무실로 오세요
If this question/problem is too difficult or you need help, come to the office
고객님이 호텔에 계실 때 불편하거나 필요한 것이 있으면 이 번호로 연락하면 됩니다
When you (the customer) are at the hotel, if you are uncomfortable or need something, you can contact us using this number

Many bus signs in Korea carry a warning that uses ~거나. It says something like this:

문이 완전히 열릴 때까지 버스에서 내리거나 문에 기대지 마세요
Until the door is completely opened, don’t get off the bus or lean on the door

Two patterns in how ~거나 is used are worth pointing out. They are discussed below.

———————————–

In the examples so far, ~거나 (and ~(이)나) appears once per sentence, separating two actions that could be done.

~거나 (or ~(이)나) can also appear twice in one sentence, attached to both possible actions (or options). For example:

비가 오거나 안 오거나 중요하지 않아요
It’s not important if it rains or not (doesn’t rain)

Why is ~거나 used twice here, but only once in the other sentences? The difference takes a moment to explain, but there is a simple way to see it.

~거나 is used once in a sentence to say that one action or the other will happen. For example:

여자 친구를 위해 편지를 쓰거나 선물을 사 줄 거예요
Here, either you will write a letter, or you will buy a present

~거나 is used twice (on both verbs) when the speaker wants to talk about the choice itself. One action or the other may still happen, but the speaker is describing the choice and how he or she might react, feel, or act because of it. For example:

(비가 오거나 안 오거나) 중요하지 않아요

The part in parentheses is what the speaker wants to talk about. By putting ~거나 on both verbs, the speaker isn’t saying one action will happen and the other won’t. The point is what follows from either one happening. More examples below. Each sentence appears twice (with and without parentheses) so you can see that the speaker is discussing the two options in the parentheses.

“Discuss” here is broad: the speaker could say anything about the choices. The two options “are not important,” he “doesn’t care about either of them,” he “is worried” about them, and so on.

나한테 선물을 주거나 아무 것도 안 해 주거나 나는 행복해 보여야 돼

(나한테 선물을 주거나 아무 것도 안 해 주거나) 나는 행복해 보여야 돼
If they give me a present or don’t do anything, I need to look happy

학생들이 수업 시간에 자거나 열심히 공부하거나 선생님들이 수업을 해야 돼요

(학생들이 수업 시간에 자거나 열심히 공부하거나) 선생님들이 수업을 해야 돼요
If students sleep or study during class time, teachers (still) need to do classes

나머지를 먹거나 버리거나 결정해야 돼요

(나머지를 먹거나 버리거나) 결정해야 돼요
You need to decide if you’re going to eat the rest or throw it out

선물을 사거나 파티에 빈손으로 들어가거나 어떻게 할지 고민 중이에요

(선물을 사거나 파티에 빈손으로 들어가거나) 어떻게 할지 고민 중이에요
I’m not sure what/how to do it – I might go to the party with a present, or go empty handed

생선이나 고기나 모두 좋아할 거예요

(생선이나 고기나) 모두 좋아할 거예요
It doesn’t matter if it is fish or meat, everybody will like it

이 영화나 저 영화나 둘 다 괜찮아요

(이 영화나 저 영화나) 둘 다 괜찮아요
Either this movie or that movie, both are fine

기말고사를 부지런히 공부하거나 안 하거나 시험을 잘 못 볼 거예요

(기말고사를 부지런히 공부하거나 안 하거나) 시험을 잘 못 볼 거예요
If I study diligently for the final exam or not, I’ll still do poorly on the exam

반말을 쓰거나 존댓말을 쓰거나 모든 한국 사람들이 이해할 수 있을 거예요

(반말을 쓰거나 존댓말을 쓰거나 모든 한국 사람들이) 이해할 수 있을 거예요
Regardless of if you use informal speech or honorific speech, all Korean people will be able to understand you

In most sentences like these, “I don’t care if…” or “it doesn’t matter if…” fits naturally. You will see more examples of ~거나 and ~(이)나 in Lesson 74 and Lesson 75, where you learn to build these sentences in Korean.

The examples in this lesson were grouped by the usages given in Korean dictionaries. Some sentences kept refusing to fit those groups, and they point to a second pattern.

You learned that one general meaning of ~(이)나 and ~거나 is that it hasn’t been decided which noun will be acted on, or which action will be done. Look again at the first sentence in this lesson:

저는 빵이나 밥을 먹고 싶어요
I want to eat bread or rice

Here the speaker hasn’t decided whether to eat bread or rice.

I tried to make another example:

무기나 칼이 없어요
I don’t have a weapon or a knife

Here it is not a choice between two objects. You don’t have either of them.

The same thing happens with ~거나 on verbs. Look again at the first ~거나 sentence:

여자 친구를 위해 편지를 쓰거나 선물을 사 줄 거예요
I will write a letter, or buy a present for my girlfriend

Here the speaker hasn’t decided whether to write a letter or buy a present

I tried to make another example:

저는 눕거나 앉을 수 없어요
I can’t lay down or sit down

Here it is not a choice between two actions. Both actions can’t happen

It may seem obvious once stated: when ~(이)나 and ~거나 are used in sentences with negative endings, the speaker is not choosing between two things or actions, but saying that neither is chosen or done. Here are examples (with both ~(이)나 and ~거나) using the negative endings you have learned so far:

희미한 빛이나 소리도 없었어요
There wasn’t even a glimmer of light or sound

Here it is not a choice between light and sound. Neither exists.

기말고사를 볼 때 말을 하거나 밥을 먹어서는 안 돼요
You shouldn’t talk or eat while writing the final exam

Here it is not a choice between speaking and eating. Neither should happen.

등교를 늦게 하거나 하교를 일찍 하면 안 돼요
You shouldn’t come to school late or leave school early

Here it is not a choice between going to school late and leaving early. Neither should happen.

저는 그 남자를 보거나 생각하지 않을 거예요
I’m not going to look at that man or think about him

Here it is not a choice between looking at the man and thinking about him. Neither will happen.
