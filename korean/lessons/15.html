속 = inside

Common Usages:

물속
inside the water
뱃속
inside a stomach/belly
꿈속에
in one’s dream

Notes: Korean people can’t explain the difference between 안 and 속.

속 is more commonly used when, if you enter something, the place will be filled with stuff and is not “empty”. Conversely, “안” would be more commonly used when, if you enter something, the place is basically empty.

For example, if you enter water or if you enter your body. In both cases, “속” would be used to refer to the inside of a body, or the inside of water. If you enter your body, it is filled with stuff, and if you enter the water, it is still all water.

The opposite is if you are talking about a room, or a building. You can go into those places and it is relatively empty.

Example: 저는 속이 안 좋아요
I don’t feel good (my insides aren’t good)

가져오다 = to bring an object

The pronunciation of this word is closer to “가저오다”

This is not used when you “bring” a person somewhere. Instead, the word “데려오다” is used

가져오다 often translates to “to bring” and 가져가다 often translates to “to take.” However, the translation of “to bring” could work for both 가져오다 and 가져가다.

가지다 means “to possess” and “오다” and “가다” mean “to come” and “to go” respectively.  Deciding to use 가져오다 or 가져가다 depends on the point of reference of the acting agent in the sentence to the speaker. Specifically, whether the acting agent is coming or going to the location in question.

Imagine you have money at your house, and you will go to your friend’s house later to give it to him. Therefore, you will have to “bring” or “take” (same meaning) that money with you when you head over there. If you are currently at your house and are talking to your friend about what you will do, you should use the word “가져가다” because you are going to your friend’s house while in possession of the money (저는 돈을 가져갈 거예요). In this example, 가져가다 is used and the best English translation would be “I will bring the money.”

However, imagine you have already arrived at your friend’s house with the money. You can use the word “가져오다” because you came to your friend’s house while in possession of the money (저는 돈을 가져왔어요). In this example, 가져오다 is used and the best English translation would be “I brought the money.”

People would read those two examples and think “Oh, so if it is something happening in the future – I should use 가져가다 and if it is something happening in the past, I should use 가져오다.”

No. It has nothing to do with the tense of the sentence. It has everything to do with the point of reference of the acting agent of the sentence to the speaker.

For example, imagine you are at your house with the money. If your friend wants to tell you to “bring the money,” he should use the word “가져오다” because you are coming (not going) to him. To his reference, you are “coming.” In this case, 가져오다 should be used.

Examples:

저는 저의 숙제를 가져왔어요
I brought my homework
저는 지갑을 안 가져왔어요
I didn’t bring my wallet
저는 친구가 사과를 가져오는 것을 원해요
I want my friend to bring apples
비가 올 까봐 우산을 가져왔어요
I brought an umbrella because I was worried that it was going to rain

Notes: A compound verb made up of “돌다” and “가다.” If you are currently in a place and are talking about returning to another place. Notes: A compound verb made up of “돌다” and “오다.” If you are currently in a place and are talking about returning to the same place. For example, if you are from Canada talking about returning to Canada (i.e. coming back to Canada), you should use this word instead of “돌아가다.”

More about좋다/싫다 to좋아하다/싫어하다

You have seen several times that in most words ending in 하다, you can remove ~하다 and the remaining word is the noun form of that verb. For example:

말
speech/words/the thing that you say
말하다
to speak
주문
an order
주문하다
to order
결혼
marriage
결혼하다
to marry
존경

존경하다
to respect

This cannot be done with 좋아하다 and 싫어하다. That is:

좋아 is not a noun that means “likeness,” and
싫어 is not a noun that means “dis-likeness”

좋아 and 싫어 can appear in sentences, but only as conjugated forms of 좋다/싫다, not as the noun form of 좋아하다 and 싫어하다. As you learned, 좋다 and 싫다 are adjectives, so they can describe an upcoming noun or predicate a sentence. For example:
저는 좋은 김치를 먹었어요
I ate good kimchi
김치는 좋아요
Kimchi is good

A quick note. You would rarely actually say ‘김치는 좋아요.’ To describe 김치 as good, you would normally use 맛있다 instead. You would only use this sentence when contrasting with something bad (something unhealthy, say): “… is bad, but Kimchi is good.” It is grammatically correct, though, and it is used here to make a point you will understand later in the lesson.

좋아하다 is made by adding ~아/어하다 to the stem of 좋다. This turns 좋다 from an adjective (good) into a verb (to like). Likewise,

싫어하다 is made by adding ~아/어하다 to the stem of 싫다. This turns 싫다 from an adjective (not good) into a verb (to dislike).

You can add ~아/어하다 to some other adjectives as well. 좋다 and 싫다 are the most common and most important for now, but other common examples are:

부끄럽다
shy (this is an adjective)
부끄러워하다
shy (this is a verb)
부럽다
envious (this is an adjective)
부러워하다
envious (this is a verb)

For now, just know that one is a verb and one is an adjective. How these pairs are used differently is covered much later, in Lesson 105. Here we focus on 좋아하다 and 싫어하다.

As a verb, 좋아하다 says that someone “likes” something. For example:

김치는 좋아요
Kimchi is good
저는 김치를 좋아해요
I like Kimchi

Likewise, 싫어하다 says that someone “dislikes” something. For example:

김치는 싫어요
Kimchi is bad/not good
저는 김치를 싫어해요
I don’t like Kimchi

However, “좋다” and “싫다” are also commonly used in these sentences to say:

김치가 좋아요
I like Kimchi
김치가 싫어요
I don’t like Kimchi

Or, other examples:

학교가 좋아요
I like school
학교가 싫어요
I don’t like school
맥주가 좋아요
I like beer
맥주가 싫어요
I don’t like beer

How this works is explained next.

Subject – Object – Adjective Form

A basic rule of grammar (not just Korean grammar) is that an adjective cannot act on an object. So in Korean, a sentence whose predicate is an adjective can never act on a word with the object particle ~를/을. You cannot say this:

저는 김치를 좋다
I kimchi good

(this does not make sense in either language)

But, you can say any of these:

저는 김치를 먹었어요
I ate kimchi

An object predicated by a verb

김치가 좋아요
kimchi is good

A subject predicated by an adjective

저는 좋은 김치를 먹었어요
I ate good kimchi

An object being described by an adjective predicated by a verb

That said, Koreans DO sometimes make sentences with an adjective predicate and an “object.” Remember, an adjective absolutely cannot act on an object. So how do they do it? They add ~이/가 to the object instead of ~을/를. Technically this makes the grammar correct, because now no adjective is acting on an object. Look at the example:

김치는 좋아요
Kimchi is good
저는 김치를 좋아해요
I like kimchi, which can also be said like this
저는 김치가 좋아요
I like kimchi

The point is that Korean often has an adjective or passive verb that seems to act on an object. But these must always be treated as adjectives or passive verbs.

Adjectives and passive verbs can never act on objects, so instead of ~를/을 you must use ~이/가. Another common case is 그립다:

그립다
this word is translated as “to miss,” but is usually used when talking about missing a non-person (it is sometimes used to say that you miss a person, but we will talk about how to say you miss a person in Lesson 17).

그립다 is an adjective in Korean (it describes a feeling rather than an action). So to say “I miss Korean food,” you cannot say:

저는 한국 음식을 그리워요. Instead, you must say:

저는 한국 음식이 그리워요
I miss Korean food

More examples. Notice that the predicate of each sentence is an adjective:

나는 네가 자랑스러워
I am proud of you
나는 그 사람이 싫어
I don’t like that person
저는 한국이 좋아요
I like Korea

You saw the same thing in the previous lesson with passive verbs, which also cannot act on an object. That is why the previous lesson had examples like these:

저는 그것이 기억나요!
I remember that!
저는 땀이 나요!
I’m sweating!
저는 화가 났어요
I was/I am angry

Korean Word:들다

The word 들다 is difficult because it has so many uses. Two of the most common are:

들다
to carry/hold something
들다
to enter/go into something/somewhere

Most uses of 들다 fit under one of these two umbrellas. The difficulty is that with so many uses, no single translation fits every situation. Here are three examples of 들다 under the umbrella of “to enter/go into something/somewhere.”

나는 동아리에 들었어
I joined a club (I “entered” a club)
(나는) 잠이 들었다
I fell asleep (I “entered” sleep)
저는 그 그림이 마음에 들어요
I like that picture (That picture enters my heart)

The word 마음 generally refers to one’s heart or mind

Now here are examples of 들다 under the umbrella of “to carry/hold something.”

저는 손을 들었어요
I raised my hand (I “held up” my hand/carried my hand)
저는 가방을 들었어요
I carried the/my bag

Okay, so what’s my point?

Well, I have three points actually.

1) The first goal was to introduce how 들다 is used. With a general grasp of the two umbrella meanings (and the specific situations in the example sentences), you should be able to handle most uses of 들다 as you move on to more advanced sentences.

2) This point is crucial for how you relate Korean words to English meanings. Korean and English are fundamentally different, and sentences are sometimes very hard to translate. In those cases, try not to pin a word to one specific definition. Be open to it having many meanings depending on context.

For example, imagine you knew these words and their definitions:

  • 저 = I/me
  • 마음 = heart/mind
  • 들다 = enter
  • 그림 = picture

And you saw the following sentence:

저는 그 그림이 마음에 들어요

Would you understand the meaning without the explanation above? Many learners would read it and think “It looks like a picture is entering that person's heart or mind… but I am not sure what that means.”

This is the first of many times you will be encouraged not to translate sentences literally. Instead, think about what a sentence could mean based on the words in it. When you meet “들다” in your studies, remember it has many uses. Even if it does not obviously look like “enter” or “carry,” an open mind lets you see it differently.

3) 들다 is introduced here because it is common in compound words, which come next.

to own/have/posses

가지다

저는 저의 모자를 가져갈 거예요
I will bring/take my hat

가져오다 often translates to “to bring” and 가져가다 often translates to “to take.” However, “to bring” could work for both 가져오다 and 가져가다.

가지다 means “to possess,” and “오다” and “가다” mean “to come” and “to go.” Choosing 가져오다 or 가져가다 depends on the point of reference of the acting agent relative to the speaker: whether that person is coming or going to the location in question.

Imagine you have money at your house and will go to your friend’s house later to give it to him. You will “bring” or “take” (same meaning) that money with you. If you are at your house talking to your friend about what you will do, use “가져가다” because you are going to your friend’s house with the money (저는 돈을 가져갈 거예요). The best English translation is “I will bring the money.”

Now imagine you have already arrived at your friend’s house with the money. Use “가져오다” because you came to your friend’s house with the money (저는 돈을 가져왔어요). The best English translation is “I brought the money.”

You might read those two examples and think “So for the future use 가져가다 and for the past use 가져오다.”

No. It has nothing to do with tense. It is entirely about the point of reference of the acting agent relative to the speaker.

For example, you are at your house with the money. If your friend wants to tell you to “bring the money,” he should use “가져오다” because, from his point of view, you are coming (not going) to him. So 가져오다 is used.

You will meet many of these words as you learn Korean. They are not very hard, but be aware of them; it helps to notice that a word is made up of two separate words.

That is enough for now; you will keep seeing these as you progress.

Different/Similar/Same in Korean(다르다/비슷하다/같다)

Three words you learned in earlier lessons are:

다르다
different
비슷하다
similar
같다
same

Using these words is not as straightforward as it seems. In simple sentences they work like any other adjective. For example:

그것은 비슷해요
That is similar
우리는 매우 달라요
We are so different
우리는 같아요
We are the same

The sentence above sounds unnatural in Korean. Although “같다” translates to “the same,” in most cases (especially when nothing is being compared, as here) it is more natural to use “똑같다,” which usually translates to “exactly the same.”

For example:

우리는 똑같아요
We are exactly the same

When comparing things like this in English, each word takes a different preposition. For example:

I am similar to my friend
That building is different from yesterday
Canadian people are the same as Korean people

In Korean, the particle ~와/과/랑/이랑/하고 covers all of these meanings. For example:

저는 친구와 비슷해요
I am similar to my friend
그 건물은 어제와 달라요
That building is different from yesterday
캐나다 사람들은 한국 사람들과 같아요
Canadian people are the same as Korean people
이 학교는 우리 학교와 똑같아요
This school is exactly the same as our school

Because ~와/과/랑/이랑/하고 covers all these cases, Koreans learning English often make mistakes like:

“This school is the same to our school”

Notice that ~와/과/랑/이랑/하고 marks what something is different from, similar to, or the same as. In theory, you could reorder the sentence (to match the structure from Lesson 13) to say that two things (this and that) are different, similar or the same. For example:

우리 학교와 이 학교는 똑같아요
Our school and this school are exactly the same

As the English translations show, this does not change the meaning. It only changes the wording and the particles' roles slightly.

같다 is covered further in Lessons 35 and 36. In particular, Lesson 36 shows how 같다 is more commonly used to say “something is like something.” This section only introduces the grammar in these sentences so you can apply it to what comes next.

This is probably an easy sentence for you now:

나는 잘생긴 남자를 만났어
I met a handsome man

Subject – adjective (describing an) – object – verb

What about these next sentences?

나는 비슷한 남자를 만났어
I met a similar man, or
나는 같은 남자를 만났어
I met the same man

These sentences have the same structure as before:
Subject – adjective (describing an) – object – verb

That should be easy too. But what about “I met a man who is similar to your boyfriend”? It seems complicated, so break it down:

너의 남자친구와 비슷하다
similar to your boyfriend

비슷하다 is an adjective, so it can modify a noun:

비슷한 남자
similar man
너의 남자친구와 비슷한 남자
A man (that is) similar to your boyfriend
나는 ( — )를 만났어
I met —
나는 (너의 남자친구와 비슷한 남자)를 만났어
I met a man that is similar to your boyfriend

This structure is complex. It is a first look at describing nouns with phrases rather than a single adjective. In Lesson 26 you will learn more about describing nouns with verbs and complex phrases.

“Different” in English has more than one nuance, and so does “다르다.” The meaning in the two sentences below is similar, but notice the slight difference:

I am different than him
I saw a different movie

The first says that something is not the same as something else.

The second means something like “other” or “another”: the person did not see the movie originally planned, but saw “another” or a “different” movie instead.

다르다 works in both situations. For example:

저는 그와 달라요
I am different from him
저는 다른 영화를 봤어요
I saw a different (another) movie

“또 다르다” usually translates to “another,” while “다르다” translates to “other.” In the example above, though, replacing “another” with “other” sounds odd.

“또 다르다” is hard to explain, but easier if you think of it as two separate words (which it is): the adjective “다르다” plus the adverb “또,” which is used when something happens again.

“또 다르다” is used when one thing has already been described and you are explaining another one. Imagine you are in a meeting with coworkers discussing potential problems with a plan. Everyone is raising problems they see, and you can point out:

또 다른 문제는 그것이 비싸요
Another problem is it (that thing) is expensive

In the same way, you can say the following sentence. The English translation is similar, but try to feel the difference that “또” adds:

저는 또 다른 영화를 봤어요
I saw ANother movie

Here, maybe the person saw one movie and then again saw a different movie.

This may seem obvious, but it is worth pointing out: many Korean words have more than one meaning. English is the same, though most people never notice how many there are. 쓰다

Each of these has had a separate entry in the vocabulary lists. But when a word has many meanings that can be grouped under a few “umbrella” meanings, only those umbrella meanings are shown. 들다, from earlier, is a good example: it has so many meanings, most of which fit into three or four broad definitions.

Either way, be aware that many Korean words have many meanings:

걸리다

걸리다

There are more uses, but focus on these four for now:

걸리다

Like the passive verbs from the previous lesson, this verb can describe the passive “state” of hanging:

그림은 벽에 걸려 있어요
The picture is hanging on the wall

Although each of these has a very different English meaning (to be hanging, to be caught, to take a certain amount of time), they are actually quite similar. A picture “hanging” on the wall is technically “stuck/trapped” on the wall. Likewise, if you go from Incheon to Seoul, the time it takes (2 hours) is “stuck/trapped.” That is one way to make the connection.

Try to think outside the English box. One Korean word often covers many English words. Usually the meanings are not actually very different; the different translations just make them seem that way. Read these sentences again with that in mind:

The picture is caught on the wall
I was caught over the line
2 hours are caught to get from Seoul to Incheon

Not natural English, but you can probably understand what they mean.

The point: just because a word seems to have many meanings does not mean those meanings are very different from each other. Think of 들다 from earlier in this lesson once more. 들다 has many meanings, but most group into only 2 or 3. Keep this in mind.

7

Being Sick in Korea

One of the first things people want to learn in a new language is how to express themselves at the doctor. It does not fit into any specific lesson, so it is covered here:

You already know 아프다, which can mean you are sick OR sore somewhere. In English “sore” and “sick” mean slightly different things, which is why Koreans learning English often say “My arm is sick.” Note also that 아프다 is an adjective, and that ‘이/가’ is used instead of 는/은 in sentences about a part of your body:

배가 아파요
My stomach is sore
팔이 아파요
My arm is sore
저는 어제 너무 아팠어요
I was very sick yesterday

You can also use 걸리다 to say you have some disease or sickness. You met 걸리다 in the previous section; this use has essentially the same meaning as the other examples (I am caught in a sickness). It is used like this:

저는 감기에 걸렸어요
I caught a cold/I have a cold
저는 독감에 걸렸어요
I caught the flu/I have the flu

Notice that “에” is used in these sentences because 걸리다 has the nuance of being stuck IN something

Also note that even though you have the cold now, Koreans use the past tense “걸렸다” to say they currently have a cold.

기침 (a cough) and 재채기 (a sneeze) are not nouns of Chinese origin, but both take 하다 to form the verb (to cough and to sneeze). For example:

저의 아들은 시끄럽게 기침했어요
My son coughed loudly

(More naturally, “My son was coughing loudly.” Koreans do not distinguish simple and progressive past tenses as much as English does. The progressive tense comes in Lesson 18.)
