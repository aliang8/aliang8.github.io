Nietzsche, slowly

Notes on a philosopher who asked whether you could say yes to your life with nothing behind it. Written to be read in order, one chapter at a time.

Friedrich Nietzsche, 1844 to 1900About 40 minutesFive parts
Part I

The man

Chapter 1A life in boarding houses

Friedrich Nietzsche was a sickly boy from a line of Lutheran pastors. His father died when he was four, of what the doctors called softening of the brain, and the son spent the rest of his life half expecting the same fate. He was brilliant in the old-fashioned way: Greek and Latin, music, a gift for prose. At twenty-four, before he had finished his doctorate, Basel made him a full professor of classical philology. Ten years later his eyes and his stomach had failed so badly that he resigned on a small pension and began the life that produced the books.

For the next decade he moved between cheap rooms in Sils Maria, Genoa, Nice, and Turin, following the weather, writing in notebooks on long walks because he could not read for more than an hour without pain. He had almost no readers. His books sold in the dozens. In January 1889 he collapsed in a Turin street, and when his friends arrived he was writing letters signed "Dionysus" and "The Crucified." He lived another eleven years without his mind, cared for first by his mother and then by his sister Elisabeth, who took control of his papers and spent the rest of her life editing him into a prophet of German nationalism. He had despised nationalism and had broken with Wagner partly over antisemitism. Much of what people think they know about him is her work.

He wanted to be read slowly, out loud, with resistance. He wrote in aphorisms because he thought a system was a kind of dishonesty, a pretense that the world holds still long enough to be diagrammed. Everything that follows is therefore already a betrayal. Read it as a map, and then go and get lost in the territory.

Part II

The ideas, in the order they build

Eight ideas. Each one needs the one before it. The last is the question the others were clearing space for.

Chapter 2Where good and evil came from

Most moral philosophers before him asked what we ought to do. Nietzsche asked a different question, one that sounds like history and turns out to be dynamite: who first decided what counts as good, and what did they get out of it?

His answer, worked out in On the Genealogy of Morality, is that there were once two moralities, and that one of them conquered the other so completely that we have forgotten the war.

Master moralityBelonged to the warrior aristocrats of the ancient world. Its basic word was "good," meaning noble, strong, healthy, open-handed, proud. The opposite was simply "bad": common, low, pitiable. Nothing much moral about it. A hawk is good at being a hawk; a lamb is just a lamb.
Slave moralityInvented by the people the masters ruled, above all the priests among them. They could not fight back, so they reversed the vocabulary. Pride, force, ambition became evil. Meekness, patience, humility, pity became good. Christianity made the reversal universal.

The feeling that drove the reversal he named with a French word, ressentiment, because he thought no German word carried the right poison. It is the resentment of someone who cannot act on their anger and so converts it into a system of values. I cannot be powerful, so power is wicked. I cannot take revenge, so forgiveness is holy. You can hear the sound of it in any morality that spends more time condemning than creating.

He did not think this was simply a story of good masters and bad slaves. The priestly reversal, he admitted, made human beings deep. It gave them an inner life, a conscience, the capacity for cruelty turned inward that we call guilt, and out of that came art and philosophy and everything interesting about us. His complaint was that the victory had been total and had been forgotten. We now take slave morality for morality as such, and so we have come to hate the very things in ourselves that make life expand.

Chapter 3The death of God

In The Gay Science a madman runs into a marketplace at midday with a lantern, crying that he is looking for God. The people standing around are atheists already, and they laugh at him. Has God wandered off? Is he hiding? And the madman turns on them.

Whither is God? I will tell you. We have killed him, you and I. All of us are his murderers. But how did we do this? How could we drink up the sea? Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the entire horizon? What were we doing when we unchained this earth from its sun?

The Gay Science, section 125

This is the famous "God is dead," and it is almost always misread. Nietzsche was not announcing a discovery about theology. The men in the market had already stopped believing. What they had not understood was the bill. Everything they still trusted had grown from Christian roots: the equal worth of persons, the duty to the weak, the authority of conscience, the faith that the universe is intelligible and that truth is worth having for its own sake. Pull up the root and the plant will stand for a long while on habit. Then one morning it will not.

The madman's point is that the news has not yet arrived. The light from a dead star keeps traveling. Nietzsche thought it would take two centuries for Europe to feel what it had done, and he placed himself at the beginning of that stretch, the first to take the death seriously and ask what could possibly come after.

Chapter 4The last man

What he feared was not that people would become monsters. He feared they would become comfortable.

Zarathustra, the prophet of his most literary book, comes down from the mountain to teach the townspeople and finds they do not want what he brings. So he tries to frighten them with a picture of what they will become if they refuse. He calls this figure the last man. The last man has invented happiness. He is a little warm, a little cold, works a little, rests a little, keeps a small vice for the evening and a smaller one for the day, never quarrels much because quarreling upsets the digestion, and never risks anything that might cost him a good night's sleep. No shepherd and one herd. Everyone wants the same, everyone is the same, and whoever feels differently goes voluntarily into the madhouse.

"We have invented happiness," say the last men, and they blink.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Prologue

The crowd loves it. Give us the last man, they shout, you can keep your overman. Zarathustra realizes they have not understood a word.

This was Nietzsche's picture of nihilism, and it is quieter than the word suggests. Not despair, not violence, just the slow lowering of every ceiling until no one remembers there was once room to stand up.

Chapter 5What life is

Against the philosophers who said that living things seek pleasure, and against Darwin's followers who said they seek survival, Nietzsche set a third candidate. Living things seek to grow. To expand, to overcome what resists them, to stamp their form on whatever they touch. He called it the will to power.

The phrase has a bad history, and it is easy to hear it as a license for brutes. He meant something wider. The painter who wrestles chaos into a composition is exercising will to power over her material. The mathematician who breaks a problem is exercising it over his own confusion. The monk who fasts and prays has turned the drive inward and achieved a formidable power over his own appetites. Nietzsche thought the ascetic ideal, which looks like the denial of all power, is in fact one of its most ingenious disguises. Even the weakest thing alive wants to be more than it is.

The reason this matters is what it does to morality. If the deepest drive in life is to grow and overcome, then a morality that names that drive evil is at war with life itself. That was his charge against Christianity, and against the secular moralities that inherited its shape. They had taught people to be ashamed of the one thing that was keeping them alive.

Chapter 6Nothing is given

If the values we live by were invented by particular people for particular reasons, then they are not laws of nature, and not commandments either. They are human artifacts, and they can be examined, criticized, and replaced.

The title Beyond Good and Evil means exactly this. Not that there is no better or worse, but that the particular pair "good and evil," with its inheritance of resentment, is one historical creation among others that are possible.

He pushed the same thought into the theory of knowledge. There is no view from nowhere. Every claim is made from somewhere, by someone with needs and drives, and it carries the shape of those needs. He did not conclude that all beliefs are equal. He concluded that the interesting question about any belief is what it does for the person who holds it. Does it make them stronger or weaker? Does it help them live or help them hide? He noticed that even the scientist's unconditional will to truth was itself a value with a history, inherited from the Christian conviction that God is truth, and he wondered what would happen when people began to ask what truth was for.

The consequence is a weight most people would rather not carry. If no one hands you your values, you are responsible for them. You cannot say that you were only following the rules. Nietzsche thought that most moral obedience was a way of avoiding exactly this responsibility, and that the person brave enough to accept it would have to become a kind of artist.

Chapter 7Giving style to a life

There is a passage in The Gay Science that may be the most practical thing he ever wrote, and it is rarely quoted because it does not sound dramatic.

One thing is needful: to give style to one's character. A great and rare art. It is practiced by those who survey all the strengths and weaknesses of their nature and then fit them into an artistic plan until every one of them appears as art and reason and even weaknesses delight the eye.

The Gay Science, section 290

A good deal of second nature is added. A good deal of first nature is removed. What cannot be removed is concealed or reinterpreted until it looks as if it was meant to be there all along. Whether the taste behind the plan is good or bad matters less than that there is one taste, one hand.

This is what "become who you are" means. It is not the modern idea of discovering a true self that was always waiting underneath. It is closer to sculpture. You are the marble, and the marble has flaws, and the question is what you can make from it.

His name for the person who has done this fully is the Übermensch, the overman, and it is the most abused word in his vocabulary. The Nazis took it to mean a master race. He meant a possibility open to any human being who could stop inheriting values and start creating them, who could affirm their own existence without the props of heaven or progress. Zarathustra says man is a rope stretched between the animal and the overman, a bridge and not an end. No one in Nietzsche's writing actually is one. When he reached for an example he usually said Goethe.

Chapter 8The heaviest weight

All of this leads to a single question, and he put it in the mouth of a demon.

What if some day or night a demon were to steal into your loneliest loneliness and say to you: "This life as you now live it and have lived it you will have to live once again and innumerable times again; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh and everything unspeakably small or great in your life must return to you, all in the same succession and sequence." Would you not throw yourself down and gnash your teeth and curse the demon who spoke thus? Or have you once experienced a tremendous moment when you would have answered him: "You are a god, and never have I heard anything more divine."

The Gay Science, section 341

He called this eternal recurrence. Notice that it comes as a demon, not a comforter. Notice that it includes everything, with no exceptions for the parts you would rather forget. And notice that he does not ask whether it is true. He asks what you would do with it.

The ordinary ways we make life bearable all work by leaving something out. This pain is temporary. That failure was not really me. The person I will be once I get through this is the one who counts. Recurrence takes away every one of those exits at once. What remains is the bare question of whether you can want your life as it actually is.

He called the attitude that can say yes amor fati, love of fate: to want nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity. Not merely to bear what is necessary, still less to hide it, but to love it. The full chapter on this is in Part III.

Chapter 9What he got wrong

He was a far better diagnostician than physician. He could show you that the old values were hollow with a precision no one has matched, and when you asked what should replace them he grew vague. Create your own, he said, and the people best equipped to hear that advice were often the ones least in need of it.

He admired strength in ways that have aged badly. He wrote contemptuously about women, about democracy, about the English. He had almost nothing to say about how people should live together, only about how one person might live. A philosophy with no room for the ordinary decency of neighbors has a hole in it, and the hole was large enough for very bad people to climb through. His sister held the door.

And the hardest objection goes to the center. Can you really affirm everything? Your own cruelties. A child's death. The suffering of people who never had the leisure to sculpt themselves. Many decent readers conclude that a view which forbids wishing any of this away is not profound but monstrous. Nietzsche would answer that this is precisely the point where the old morality sneaks back in. You can decide whether he is right. He knew the weight of what he was asking, and he did not pretend otherwise.

Part III

Deeper

Two chapters that take their time: the argument he worked out most fully, and the thought he built everything toward.

Chapter 10Where good and evil came from, in full

The question nobody had asked

Kant said morality demands that you act only on rules you could will for everyone. Mill said it demands the greatest happiness for the greatest number. The Christians said it demands obedience to God. All of them took for granted that there is a morality, singular, and that the job is to find its foundation.

Nietzsche, trained as a philologist, had spent his twenties studying how Greek words shifted meaning across centuries. He brought that habit to ethics and noticed something odd. In language after language, the oldest sense of "good" is not "kind" or "selfless" but "noble," "of high rank," "one of us." The Greek agathos, the Latin bonus, the German gut all began as the self-description of an aristocracy. The opposite word, "bad," meant "common," "low," with no more moral charge than the difference between a thoroughbred and a cart horse.

So he asked the question that gives his book its title. Not "what is good?" but "who first called things good, and what were they doing when they did it?" He called this a genealogy because he meant it like a family tree. Trace the ancestry of a value and you learn what it is made of.

The first morality

The earliest moral vocabulary was made by the people on top. Warrior aristocrats in Homer's Greece, in early Rome, among the Vikings, among the samurai. They looked at themselves and said: this is good. Strength, courage, health, pride, generosity to friends, ferocity to enemies, the frank enjoyment of one's own power. They looked at the people below them and said: that is bad. There was no hatred in it, or not much. A hawk does not hate the lamb.

Master morality begins with a yes. The master says "I am good" first and only afterward, almost as an afterthought, "and that is bad." And it has no concept of evil. There are only better and worse, higher and lower, and the lower is a shame but not a sin.

The reversal

Now consider the people underneath. They cannot win by force. But they have something the masters lack: time to think, and a grievance to think about. Among them the priests become the leaders, because priests are the most intelligent of the powerless and the most practiced at turning weakness into authority.

What the priests invented was a reversal of the words. They took the aristocrat's "good" and renamed it "evil." Pride became the first of the sins. Strength became cruelty, ambition became vanity, the enjoyment of life became worldliness. And they took their own condition, the condition they had no choice about, and renamed it "good." Meekness, humility, patience, obedience, pity, love of enemies, the renunciation of this world for the sake of another. The lamb's inability to be a hawk became the lamb's virtue.

The masterstroke was to promise that the reversal would be enforced. In this life the hawk still eats the lamb. But there is another life, and in it the hawk will burn forever and the lamb will sit in glory. Nietzsche quotes Tertullian and Aquinas describing the pleasure the saved will take in watching the damned. He thought that pleasure was the secret of the whole system. Heaven is revenge deferred.

Ressentiment

Ordinary anger acts and is finished. You are wronged, you strike back, the matter is settled. Ressentiment is anger that cannot act. It belongs to someone who has been wronged and cannot strike back, and so the anger turns inward, festers, and finally does the only thing left to it: it reinterprets the world so that the wrong becomes a virtue and the wrongdoer becomes a monster.

I cannot be strongTherefore strength is wicked, and my weakness is purity.
I cannot take revengeTherefore forgiveness is holy, and I am better than the one who hurt me.
I cannot enjoy this lifeTherefore this life is a vale of tears, and the real life is elsewhere.

What makes ressentiment so effective is that it never admits what it is. It presents itself as morality, as justice, as love. The person in its grip believes sincerely that they are being good. You can detect it by a certain tone: a morality that spends more energy condemning than building, that is more interested in the sinner than in the saint, that seems to need its enemies.

Why he did not simply take the masters' side

The slave revolt, he admitted, was the event that made human beings interesting. The master is a splendid animal, but he is an animal. He acts and forgets. The slave, who cannot act, must think, must plan, must learn to read faces and hide intentions and wait. Out of that waiting came the inner life. Out of the anger turned inward came conscience, and from conscience came everything that distinguishes us from cattle.

Only here did the human soul acquire depth and become evil, and these are the two basic forms of the superiority that man has so far shown over other animals.

On the Genealogy of Morality, First Essay

So the complaint is not that slave morality won. It is that it won so completely that we have forgotten it was ever one side of a fight. We no longer have the master's vocabulary available even as a counterweight, and so we cannot see what we have lost.

The bad conscience

The second essay asks where guilt comes from. The German word for guilt, Schuld, is also the word for debt, and Nietzsche argues this is no accident. The oldest justice is the creditor's right to take payment in pain when the debtor cannot pay in goods. Punishment began as a pleasure.

Then society grew, and the old freedom to discharge aggression outward was walled in. The instinct did not disappear. It turned inward. The cruelty that could not be spent on others was spent on oneself, and the result was the bad conscience: the human animal gnawing at itself in its cage. Religion then took this raw material and gave it a story. You are not merely a creature who hurts itself. You are a sinner. You owe a debt so vast that only a god could pay it, and he did, and now you owe him for that too. It took a pain that was already there and made it meaningful, which made it bearable, which made it permanent.

The ascetic ideal

The third essay asks why human beings have so often made a virtue of denying themselves. His answer is that the ascetic ideal is will to power in disguise, and the disguise is necessary because it belongs to people who have no other outlet. The sick, the weak, the defeated need a reason to go on, and the ascetic priest gives them one. Your suffering is a test, a purification, a debt being paid. By giving suffering a meaning, the priest preserves the sufferer and binds them to himself. Life uses even the denial of life as a tool for keeping itself going.

He admired this. He thought it was the single most powerful idea in human history. He also thought its time was ending, and that the scientist's will to truth was its last and subtlest form, still denying life in favor of something higher, now called fact instead of God.

What to do with this

The genealogy is not an argument that we should stop being kind. Nietzsche was against pity as a value system, not as a feeling. What the genealogy does is remove the floor. Once you see that "good" was invented, and by whom, and for what reason, you cannot go back to treating it as a law of nature.

Look at the moral convictions you hold most firmly and ask whether each began as an affirmation of something you are, or as a grievance against something you cannot be. Most of us, if we are honest, will find some of each. The honesty is the point.

Chapter 11Eternal recurrence, in full

The problem he was solving

Nietzsche lived in a time when people were losing their religion but still running on its rules. He saw a problem coming. If there is no heaven and no cosmic plan, what makes any of this worth it? He worried people would slide into "nothing matters."

He also noticed that almost every answer humans had come up with worked the same way: the real point of life is somewhere else. Heaven. A better future. The person you will become once you finish the hard part. He thought this was a quiet insult to the life you are actually living right now. So he wanted a test for whether you could say yes to this life, the real one, with no backup plan.

Why it hits so hard

We all have three escape hatches that make life bearable.

It's temporaryThis bad stretch will end.
It doesn't countThat mistake wasn't really me.
The real me is laterI'll be happy once I get there.

Eternal recurrence closes all three at once. Nothing is temporary, because it comes back. Nothing is excluded. And there is no later version of you to make up for this one, because this one is what repeats. What is left is a bare question: can you want this, exactly this?

What passing the test looks like

Nietzsche called the right attitude amor fati, love of fate. Not "I accept it." Not "I'll put up with it." Love it.

My formula for greatness in a human being is amor fati: that one wants nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity. Not merely bear what is necessary, still less conceal it, but love it.

Ecce Homo

This sounds impossible, and he knew that. Zarathustra is nearly destroyed by the thought. He lies for seven days as if dead before he can rise and affirm it. The point is not that you pass on the first try. The point is that it shows you where you are stuck.

The obvious objections

  1. Isn't this just fatalism? If everything repeats, why bother doing anything? His answer: your effort is part of what repeats. The thought does not tell you to stop trying. It asks whether you would gladly do this trying again.
  2. Can you really say yes to everything? Your worst moments. Other people's suffering. This is the real problem and he did not dodge it. He would say the urge to make exceptions is the old morality sneaking back in. Whether that is deep or monstrous is a fair question, and serious people land on each side.
  3. Did he mean it literally? He flirted with a physics argument in his notebooks, but what he published was always the test, not the cosmology. The idea works whether or not the universe repeats. It is a mirror, not a prediction.

How to actually use it

Pick anything you spend time on. Ask: if this exact hour had to repeat forever, would I want it? Most people discover large stretches of their life are lived in "paying the cost" mode, endured for some later payoff. The test does not say that is wrong. It makes you look at it. Then you have two honest options: find a way to genuinely want it, or change it.

That is why the idea has outlasted everything else about Nietzsche. It is a tool, and it still works.

Part IV

On God

Chapter 12Did he believe?

No. He was an atheist, and about as thorough a one as has ever written. But the shape of his unbelief is unusual, and it is where most people misread him.

He grew up devout. His father and both grandfathers were Lutheran pastors, and as a boy he was called "the little pastor" because he could recite scripture with such feeling that it made the other children cry. He lost his faith at around twenty, reading Schopenhauer and the higher criticism of the Bible, and never wanted it back. He wrote to his sister at that age that if you want peace of mind and happiness, believe; if you want to be a disciple of truth, inquire. He chose inquiry and knew what it would cost.

What makes him different from the ordinary atheist of his century is that he was not very interested in whether God exists. He thought that argument was over, and that the people still having it, on both sides, had missed the point. Spending your life proving there is no God struck him as a confession that you still needed there not to be one. He reserved his real contempt for the comfortable unbelievers, the ones who dropped God and kept everything that had depended on him, as if those things could float unsupported. That is who the madman is shouting at. Not believers. Atheists who have not noticed what they did.

He also did not think religion was simply a mistake. He read it the way a doctor reads a fever. Why did people need a God? What did believing do for them? Christianity, he concluded, was a brilliant solution to the problem of suffering. It took pain that was already there and made it mean something, and a pain that means something can be borne. He respected that achievement more than most philosophy. His quarrel was with what it cost: a hatred of the body, of this world, of strength and pride and the present moment, all sacrificed to a world that did not exist.

He admired Jesus and was savage about Paul. In The Antichrist he reads Jesus as a kind of holy innocent, a man who lived entirely in the present without resentment or judgment, and Paul as the priest who turned that life into a doctrine of sin, guilt, and revenge. "There has been only one Christian," he wrote, "and he died on the cross."

If he had a god, it was Dionysus, the Greek god of wine, ecstasy, and destruction, whom he used as a name for the affirmation of life in all of it, the suffering included. The last letters he wrote before his collapse were signed "Dionysus" and, in a few cases, "The Crucified." Whatever was happening to his mind in those weeks, the two names had been fighting in him for twenty years.

Chapter 13How to think about God, on his terms

Does God exist?

Nietzsche would say no, and then he would tell you that you asked the wrong question. The Christian God had become unbelievable, not because someone refuted him but because the culture that needed him had changed. Belief did not lose an argument. It lost its footing. If you ask him whether God exists, the honest answer from his side is: the question is not where the action is. The action is in what the question was doing for you.

What is God, then?

Nietzsche reads every god as an answer to a human need, and he asks what need the Christian God answered. His answer is suffering. Life hurts, often without reason, and a creature that can ask "why" cannot bear pain that has no why. The Christian God solved this. Your pain is a test, or a punishment, or a mystery held in a wiser hand. He never mocked the need behind it.

But he also read the specific shape of the Christian God as a product of ressentiment. A god who sides with the weak against the strong, who will punish the proud in the next life, who tells you this world is fallen and the real one is elsewhere. That god, he argued, was invented by people who could not win here and needed a world where they would. So when you think about God, on his view, you are thinking about two things at once: a genuine human need for meaning in the face of suffering, and a particular historical answer that carries a particular poison. He wants you to keep seeing the need and to stop drinking the poison.

Should you care whether he exists?

Yes, but not in the way the question usually means. The question he would put to you is not "does God exist" but "what in your life is still leaning on him without your knowing it?"

Where do you get your sense of right and wrong, and would it survive if you admitted there is no judge? What makes your life feel worthwhile, and is that thing actually here, or is it a promissory note on a future that never arrives?

And underneath, the question he cared about most. If there is no God, no afterlife, no cosmic plan, can you still say yes to this life? Not tolerate it. Want it. If you can, you have replaced God with something. If you cannot, then you have not yet faced what the madman was shouting about.

Where this leaves you

If you find yourself wanting God to exist, he would ask you to look at the want. Is it fear of death? Fear that nothing matters? A wish for someone to punish the people who hurt you? He thought every one of those wants was understandable and every one was a place where you had not yet grown up, in the specific sense that you were asking something outside yourself to carry a weight that is yours.

If you find yourself certain God does not exist, he would ask whether you have paid for that. Have you given up the morality that came with him, or are you still running on it? Have you found something to affirm, or have you just stopped believing and kept on blinking?

You do not have to agree with him. Kierkegaard, who faced the same abyss a generation earlier, concluded that faith is exactly the leap you make when no proof is available, and that this is what makes it faith. Nietzsche would call that cowardice. Kierkegaard would call Nietzsche's affirmation of life a faith that refuses to name itself. They may both be right about each other. What Nietzsche gives you is not the answer but a way to tell whether your answer is yours.

Part V

For you

Chapter 14Questions to sit with

These are the questions his work actually puts to a reader, arranged from the ones that are easiest to start on to the ones that take years.

  • Which hours of your life would you take again?

    Go through an ordinary week and sort it. Which stretches would you want to repeat, exactly as they were, forever? Which are you enduring on the way to something else? Most people find the second category is much larger than they expected, and that it includes things they would have said they chose.

  • Where do your strongest moral convictions come from?

    Take the two or three things you are most sure are wrong. Did that conviction begin as an affirmation of something you are, or as a grievance against something you could not be or have? The ones that come from a yes you can keep with a clear conscience. The ones that come from a no deserve a second look.

  • Who would you have to be for your judgments of others to be fair?

    Pick someone you judge harshly and ask whether you would still judge them if you had their strength, their luck, or their freedom. If the answer is no, your judgment is telling you something about yourself and nothing about them.

  • What are you leaning on that you no longer believe in?

    You may have dropped religion, but have you dropped the sense that someone is keeping score? That good behavior will be rewarded in the long run? That your life has a direction given from outside? Each of those grew from a root you may have already pulled up.

  • What do you want when you want comfort?

    When you reach for reassurance, for a story in which everything works out, what is the fear underneath? He was not against comfort. He was against not knowing what you are buying with it.

  • What does your suffering mean, and who gave it that meaning?

    Everyone has a story about why their hardest experiences happened. None of these stories is given; each is chosen, usually without noticing. Ask which story you are telling and whether you would tell it again.

  • If you survey everything your nature has given you, what is the design?

    What would someone who loved you and had no illusions say your real gifts and real limits are? Then ask what could be made from that, and whether you are making it.

  • What would you be if no one were watching and no one would ever know?

    If your goodness depends on an audience, including an imagined divine one, it is obedience. You find out which you are by imagining the audience gone.

  • Where in your life are you blinking?

    Where have you settled for comfort and called it maturity? Where did you stop reaching, and when, and why? The answer is often a specific year and a specific disappointment.

  • What would you have to affirm that you currently refuse to?

    There is almost certainly something in your past that you wish had not happened and cannot stop wishing. Could you come to want even that, not because it was good, but because it is part of the only life you have? You may conclude that he asks too much. But you should know exactly what you are refusing, and why.

How to work on these

He walked. Hours a day, alone, in the mountains above Sils Maria or along the coast at Genoa, and wrote in a notebook when a thought arrived. He distrusted any idea that came to him sitting down. You do not need the Alps, but you probably need to be moving and alone, and you need to come back to the same question more than once.

He also thought you should write. A thought you cannot put into a sentence is a thought you do not yet have. Pick the question above that made you most uncomfortable and write a page on it. Then put it away and write another page in a month. The difference between the two pages is where you are moving.

Chapter 15What if I just want a comfortable life?

Then Nietzsche is not your philosopher, and he would be the first to say so.

What he would say

He would not try to talk you out of it. He thought most people should want a comfortable life, that they are built for it, and that the world runs on them. The last man is a portrait of a civilization, not an insult to a person. His books are addressed, by his own account, to a very small number of readers who have a particular sickness: they cannot be comfortable, they feel the ceiling pressing down, and they need to know whether that feeling is a defect or a calling.

What he would object to is pretending. If you want comfort, want it openly. Do not dress it up as wisdom, or balance, or maturity. Do not tell yourself that the people who reach for more are fools. Those are the lamb's stories about the hawk. Just say: I want a warm house and good friends and a quiet mind, and I am willing to pay for that with the things I will not do. He would respect that more than most of what passes for philosophy.

What he would get wrong

His whole frame assumes that reaching is higher than resting, and he never argues for it. It is an aristocrat's assumption, inherited from the Greeks he loved. There is another tradition, just as old, that he dismissed too quickly. Epicurus spent his life in a garden with friends and considered untroubled calm the summit of wisdom. The Stoics wanted peace of mind above all. The Daodejing says the wise person is like water, going low, not striving. Montaigne retired to his tower at thirty-eight and said the greatest thing in the world is to know how to belong to oneself. None of these people were last men. They chose comfort with their eyes open, and they are not obviously smaller than Nietzsche, who died mad and alone having loved almost no one.

He also undersells how much courage an ordinary life takes. Raising a child, staying married, caring for a dying parent, showing up to the same work for thirty years and doing it well. None of this looks like the overman and all of it is harder than writing aphorisms in a boarding house.

What is actually true

ChosenYou looked at what you are and what you want and decided: these people, this work, this pace, this town. You would take it again. You are not waiting for something else to begin. This life passes his test. Recurrence does not care whether your life is large. It cares whether you would want it back.
DefaultedYou took the job because it was offered, stayed because leaving was hard, stopped reaching because reaching was tiring, and somewhere along the way quietly agreed with yourself not to notice. This is the one he is warning about. Not because it is small but because it is not yours. It happened to you.

From the outside the two can look identical. Same house, same routine, same quiet evenings. The difference is entirely inside, and it is the difference between a person who could answer the demon and a person who would have to look away.

The question is not whether you should want a comfortable life. Plenty of wise people have. The question is whether you have actually chosen it, whether you know what it costs and are glad to pay, and whether you can look straight at the parts you gave up and say: yes, that too. If you can, you have nothing to learn from Nietzsche except how to argue with him. If there is a flinch somewhere, that flinch is the only thing he was ever writing about.

Chapter 16How to read him

In this order. Slowly. He asked for that, and he meant it.

  • The Gay ScienceStart here. The most varied and the most cheerful. Sections 125 (the madman), 276 (amor fati), 290 (giving style to one's character), 341 (the demon).
  • On the Genealogy of MoralityHis most sustained argument. Read the preface first, then the first essay slowly; ressentiment arrives in section 10 and you will want to sit with it. The Clark and Swensen translation is clear and well annotated.
  • Beyond Good and EvilThe mature position in compressed form.
  • Thus Spoke ZarathustraLast. It is a prose poem and rewards readers who already know what it is dramatizing. Part III, "The Convalescent," is where Zarathustra faces the thought of recurrence.
  • The Will to PowerSkip it. His sister assembled it from notebook scraps, arranged to serve her purposes.
  • Kaufmann, Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, AntichristThe book that rescued him from the Nazis' embrace after the war, and still the best single volume. Kaufmann's translations are the standard in English.
  • Leiter, Nietzsche on MoralityA shorter, careful guide if you want one.
